CHAPTER 11
Cranial Nerve VIII (The Vestibulocochlear Nerve)
Anatomy of Cranial Nerve VIII
The eighth cranial nerve consists of two distinct components: the auditory (cochlear) nerve, concerned with hearing, and the
vestibular nerve, concerned with balance[ 55 , 177 , 228 , 234]. The auditory nerve receives information from the tonotopically organized
cochlea. The vestibular nerve receives inputs from the saccule and utricule, which sense linear acceleration of the head, and from
the cristae of the semicircular canals, which sense angular acceleration of the head.
Auditory Pathways
The auditory pathway (Fig. 11-1) consists of a four-tiered neuronal network: (a) the auditory (cochlear) nerve, which extends from
the organ of Corti to the cochlear nucleus, (b) fibers from the cochlear nucleus that cross to the contralateral inferior colliculus, (c)
fibers from the inferior colliculus that travel up to the medial geniculate nucleus, and (d) fibers from the medial geniculate nucleus
that project to the auditory cortex in the superior temporal gyrus[ 33 , 91 , 159].
First-Order Neurons
The auditory receptors are the neuroepithelial internal and external hair cells of the organ of Corti, which resides on the basilar
membrane inside the scala media. Inner hair cells located at the cochlear apex are stimulated by low-frequency tones, whereas hair
cells located at the base are stimulated by high-frequency tones. First-order neurons have their cell bodies in the spiral ganglion of
the cochlear nerve, housed in the canal of Rosenthal at the base of the bony spiral lamina. Afferent components of these cells
connect with the cochlear hair cells. Most converge on the internal hair cells; a smaller set diverge to reach the external hair cells.
When hair cells are activated, impulses are triggered in fibers whose perikarya sit at the spiral ganglion; these fibers enter the
brainstem at the level of the ventral cochlear nuclei as the cochlear nerve.
Second-Order Neurons
On reaching the lower brainstem at the medullopontine junction, afferent cochlear nerve fibers split to innervate the dorsal cochlear
nucleus and the anteroventral and posteroventral nuclei of the cochlear nuclear complex. Following a tonotopic pattern, the more
dorsal parts of these nuclei receive fibers innervating “high-frequency” basal hair cells, whereas the ventral aspects receive fibers
from “low-frequency” apical hair cells. The dorsal and ventral cochlear nuclei contain the second-order neurons of the auditory
pathway and carry information to the contralateral brainstem, as the lateral lemniscus, which travels up in the pontine and midbrain
tegmentum to end in the inferior colliculus. These projections include the dorsal acoustic striae (from the dorsal cochlear nucleus),
the intermediate acoustic striae (from the dorsal part of the ventral cochlear nucleus), and the ventral acoustic striae (from the
ventral cochlear nucleus), a part of the trapezoid body located in the caudal pons. Decussating fibers of the trapezoid body in the
contralateral superior olivary complex travel up in the contralateral lateral lemniscus. The lateral lemniscus ends in the inferior
colliculus. The ventral acoustic striae also end in the ipsilateral as well as the contralateral reticular formation, superior olivary
nuclei, and nuclei of the trapezoid body.
Third-Order Neurons
The inferior colliculus carries the third-order neurons and serves as the central relay nucleus of the auditory pathway, receiving
ascending and descending inputs. All ascending auditory pathways end at the inferior colliculus. Fibers from the lateral lemniscus
end at the tonotopically organized central nucleus. Projections from the inferior colliculus synapse at the medial geniculate nucleus
of the thalamus. “Low-frequency” fibers end in apical-lateral areas, whereby “high-frequency” fibers end in medial areas.
Fourth-Order Neurons
The medial geniculate nucleus is the thalamic auditory relay nucleus. It generates the geniculotemporal fibers or auditory radiations,
which travel laterally in a dense tract penetrating the ventral and lateral portions of the posterior half of the putamen and the white

Although many commissural connections exist along the auditory pathway, none exist at the level of the medial geniculate nucleus.
This bilateral representation has major clinical relevance whenever dealing with unilateral brainstem lesions. Neurons of the
superior olivary complex receive input from both ears. There are also connections between the two cochlear nuclei, between the two
dorsal nuclei of the lateral lemniscus through the commissure of Probst, between the inferior colliculus on each side through the
commissure of the inferior colliculus, and also between the central nucleus of the inferior colliculus and the contralateral medial

geniculate nucleus through the brachium of the inferior colliculus[ 147]. Furthermore, from the inferior colliculus rostrally, there are
two different projection systems. The core system (central nucleus of the inferior colliculus, medial geniculate nucleus, and primary
auditory cortex), a direct auditory pathway with tonotopic organization, and the belt projection (pericentral region of the inferior
colliculus, nonlaminated parts of the medial geniculate nucleus, and secondary auditory cortex), with less tonotopic organization,
which serves as a polymodal system receiving both auditory and nonauditory information.
There are also several descending auditory pathways running parallel to ascending fibers, including corticogeniculate,
corticocollicular, and geniculo-collicular fibers, as well as collicular efferents, and an efferent cochlear bundle from the superior
olive to the hair cells of the spiral organ of Corti.
Blood supply to the cochlea and auditory brainstem nuclei arise from the internal auditory artery (labyrinthine artery), a branch of
the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA). Within the internal auditory canal, the internal auditory artery supplies ganglion cells,
nerves, dura, and arachnoid, splitting into the common cochlear artery and the anterior vestibular artery. Circumferential branches of
the basilar artery supply the superior olivary complex and lateral lemniscus. Branches of the superior cerebellar and quadrigeminal
arteries provide blood flow to the inferior colliculus. The medial geniculate nuclei receive their blood supply from the
thalamogeniculate arteries. Middle cerebral artery branches supply the primary and associated auditory cortices.
The Vestibular System
The vestibular system monitors angular and linear accelerations of the head in space, providing information of head movements,
changes in head position, and a sense of balance. These accelerations are transduced into neuronal signals within the membranous
labyrinth. The labyrinth consists of the otolith organ, which includes the utricle and saccule, and the three semicircular canals [4 , 34 ,
56]. Linear acceleration is detected by the macules of the utricle and saccule. Angular acceleration and deceleration are detected by
the cristae ampullaris of the semicircular canals. These specialized receptors consist of numerous hair cells that serve as transducers,
transforming mechanical movements of sensory hairs into changes of receptor potentials in the hair cells and afferent neurons.
The three semicircular canals, each in a specific plane and oriented at approximately right angles to each other, detect angular
accelerating movements of the head. They include the lateral or horizontal canal, the anterior or superior canal, and the posterior
or inferior canal. With the head in the erect position, the horizontal canal is almost horizontal, and the superior and posterior canals
arranged in two vertical planes form a 45-degree angle with the frontal and sagittal planes. Thus, the horizontal canals of both
labyrinths are in the same plane, whereas the superior canal on one labyrinth is in a similar plane as the posterior canal of the
opposite labyrinth.
The utricle and saccule lay out at right angles, with the utricle parallel to the skull base and the saccule to the sagittal plane [80].
Thus, horizontal head movements stimulate the utricle linearly, whereas head tilting activates the saccule.
When the cristae or macules are stimulated, potentials originating in their afferent nerve endings in cell bodies of the vestibular
ganglion of Scarpa are transmitted by the vestibular nerve.
Information from the membranous labyrinth is conducted differently by the two vestibular nerve components. The superior aspect of
the vestibular nerve carries input from the anterior and horizontal semicircular canals and from the utricle, whereas the inferior
portion of the vestibular nerve conveys information from the posterior semicircular canal and the saccule. The vestibular nerve
enters the brainstem at the pontomedullary level, bifurcates into ascending and descending fascicles, ending in the vestibular nuclei
found beneath the floor of the fourth ventricles lateral to the sulcus limitans. The vestibular nuclei consist of the superior nucleus of
Bechterew, the lateral nucleus of Deiters, the medial nucleus of Schwalbe, and the inferior or descending nucleus of Roller. These
nuclei help to maintain stable vision during head movements and a stable posture during body movements [ 102 , 181]. The
semicircular canals preferentially relate to the superior and medial vestibular nuclei, whereas the macular fibers project

mainly to the medial and inferior vestibular nuclei. Other vestibular nerve afferents reach the cerebellum by way of
the inferior cerebellar peduncle, ending in the vestibulocerebellum.
Most of the vestibular nuclei output is concerned with feedback integration with the cerebellum, spinal cord, and brainstem. The
main vestibular connections include the following neuroanatomic structures.
Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus
Through the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), the vestibular nuclei influence conjugate eye movements and head posture.
Although all the vestibular nuclei contribute to the MLF, only the superior vestibular nucleus projects to the ipsilateral MLF; other
nuclei send fibers only to the contralateral MLF.
Medial Vestibulospinal Tract


<!-- PDF page 556 -->

The medial vestibulospinal tract (MVT) originates primarily from the medial vestibular nucleus and, to a lesser degree, from the
inferior and lateral vestibular nuclei. Through the MVT, the medial vestibular nucleus exerts excitatory and inhibitory effects on the
contralateral cervical and upper thoracic spinal cord.
Lateral Vestibulospinal Tract
The lateral vestibulospinal tract (LVT) originates primarily from the lateral and inferior vestibular nuclei and projects to the
ipsilateral spinal cord. Fibers aimed for the cervical cord arise from the rostral and ventral portion of the lateral vestibular nucleus,
whereas lumbosacral fibers originate from the dorsal and caudal portion. The LVT facilitates extensor trunk tone and the action of
antigravity axial extensor muscles, reflecting the input the vestibular nuclei receive from the utricular “gravity detector.”
Cerebellum
The vestibulocerebellum receives afferent fibers from the vestibular ganglion and from ipsilateral vestibular nuclei. Primarily, the
inferior and medial vestibular nuclei project to the ipsilateral flocculonodular lobe, uvula, and fastigial nucleus. There are also
reciprocal connections, including cerebellar corticovestibular fibers from the nodulus, uvula, flocculus, other vermian areas, and
fastigio-vestibular fibers. These connections travel in the juxtarestiform body, which coordinates balance and eye movements by
communication between the vestibular system and the cerebellum.
Reticular Formation
The vestibular nuclei specifically influence the lateral reticular nucleus and the nucleus reticularis pontis caudalis. Neurons in the
superior lateral and inferior vestibular nuclei project bilaterally to the ventral posterolateral and posterior nuclear group of the
thalamus. Cortical representation of vestibular function takes place in the postcentral gyrus, near Brodmann areas 2 and 5, area 6 of
the frontal lobe, and superior temporal gyrus.
Blood supply of the membranous labyrinth originates from the internal auditory artery (labyrinthine artery) or, less commonly, from
the basilar artery. After giving off a branch to the eighth cranial nerve at the cerebellopontine angle, the internal auditory artery
branches into the (a) anterior vestibular artery to the anterior and lateral semicircular canals and utricular macula; (b) posterior
vestibular artery to the posterior semicircular canal, saccular macula, and part of the cochlea; and (c) the cochlear artery. Thus, the
anterior and posterior vestibular arteries carry blood flow to structures innervated by the superior and inferior branches of the
vestibular nerve, respectively [ 142].
Clinical Evaluation of Cranial Nerve VIII Function
Early recognition of hearing impairment is critical, because delayed recognition may account for learning disabilities and impaired
acquisition of language skills. Hearing impairment is classified as mild (20 to 39 dB), moderate (40 to 69 dB), severe (70 to 89 dB),
or profound (>90 dB). Based on age at onset, hearing loss is further categorized as prelingual or postlingual [178 , 195].
Sensorineural Deafness
Sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL) refers to deficits in the perception of either tones or speech, due to lesions central to the oval
window. SNHL may involve the cochlea (sensory), the cochlear nerve and nuclei (neural), or the central auditory pathways. SNHL
may be bilateral and progressive (e.g., presbyacusis, ototoxic drugs), unilateral and progressive (e.g., Ménière disease, vestibular
shwannomas), unilateral and sudden (e.g., idiopathic, impaired cochlear blood flow, viral infection, perilymphatic fistula,
autoimmune inner ear disease, or, rarely, vestibular schwannoma) [ 132 , 159] (
Video 11-1). Less commonly, SNHL is bilateral
and sudden (e.g., toxic, neoplastic vascular, autoimmune, infectious, including neurosyphilis, or iatrogenic) [ 193].

Hearing loss evaluation requires otoscopic examination of the external auditory canal, inspection of the tympanic membranes, and
detailed general physical and neurologic examination, including a thorough search for a craniofacial, musculoskeletal, ocular, hair,
or skin pigmentary abnormalities. Next, it must be established whether the hearing impairment is due to SNHL, conductive hearing
loss (i.e., lesion between the environment and the organ of Corti), or mixed hearing loss. Hearing impairment may result from an
array of syndromic or nonsyndromic genetic disorders, environmental disorders, infectious, inflammatory, autoimmune, vasculitic,
metabolic, traumatic, and structural conditions, as well as selective cochlear neurotoxicity. Approximately one-fourth of cases of
childhood hearing impairment are due to prematurity, congenital hyperbilirubinemia, neonatal hypoxia, bacterial meningitis, viral
infections (rubella, measles, herpes, cytomegalovirus), trauma, and ototoxic drugs [195]. Genetic causes of hearing loss may result
from a number of autosomal recessive, X-linked, or mitochondrial disorders such as mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic
acidosis and stroke-like episodes (MELAS), myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (MERRF), and Kearns-Sayre syndrome
(KSS). Most of these genetic disorders are nonsyndromic and autosomal recessive [63 , 171]. Drug-induced ototoxicity may result
from aminoglycoside antibiotics, loop diuretics, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, antimalarials, quinine, and salicylates.
Aspirin at higher doses may cause tinnitus, dizziness, and hearing loss. Hearing loss may also result from high doses of
propoxyphene and the combination of hydrocodone/acetaminophen [69 , 84 , 93 , 168]. Reversible hearing loss has been reported with
gabapentin use in patients with preexisting renal dysfunction [179].
Progressive bilateral and symmetric SNHL is a main feature of superficial central nervous system siderosis [96 , 116 , 187] and
leptomeningeal carcinomatosis [156 , 169]. Hearing loss may also complicate cranial radiation, Vinka alkaloids, cis-platinum, and
interferon alpha [106 , 136 , 152 , 194]. Progressive unilateral or bilateral hearing loss may be observed in patients with Fabry disease [216
] and with the antiphospholipid antibody syndrome [ 154].
Conductive hearing loss results from failure of sound conduction from the environment to the inner ear. Common causes of
conductive hearing loss include obstructions of the external auditory meatus by cerumen, otosclerosis, tympanosclerosis, otitis
externa, serous otitis media, ossicular chain trauma, and cholesteatoma.
Bedside qualitative assessment of hearing loss requires the use of a tuning fork (256 Hz or preferably 512 Hz). The tests that are
often used are the Weber and Rinne tests.
Weber Test
The Weber test assists in differentiating conductive from SNHL. The examiner places a vibrating tuning fork over the patient's
midline of the skull or forehead, nasal bones, or anterior upper incisors. Vibrations are normally appraised symmetrically in both
ears. In cases of conductive hearing loss, vibrations are recognized louder in the deaf ear (lateralization to the affected ear). In cases
of SNHL, the vibrations are recognized as louder in the normal ear (lateralization to the normal ear).
Rinne Test
The Rinne test compares air and bone conduction. The examiner applies the stem of the vibrating tuning fork against the patient's
mastoid process. Once the patient no longer perceives the vibration, the fork is placed next to the ear with the tines parallel to the
sagittal plane of the skull, 1 to 2 inches from the external auditory meatus. Because air conduction is transmitted better than bone
conduction, the vibrations are normally perceived in the ear after they are no longer perceived at the mastoid. The test is reported to
be normal or positive when the tuning fork vibrations are heard approximately twice as long by air conduction than by bone
conduction. In cases of conduction hearing loss, bone conduction is better than air conduction, and, therefore, the tuning fork


<!-- PDF page 558 -->

vibrations cannot be further perceived when the fork is placed next to the ear. With SNHL, both air and bone conduction are equally
decreased, with air conduction remaining greater than bone conduction.
In summary, with SNHL:
The Weber test lateralizes to the normal ear.
The Rinne test result is positive.
With conductive hearing loss:
The Weber test lateralizes to the affected ear.
The Rinne test result is negative.
Vertigo and Vestibular Function
Clinical evaluation of patient with dizziness or vertigo should focus on the following [31 , 82 , 211].
Symptoms characterization
Dizziness often means either vertigo, near-syncope, disequilibrium, or ill-defined lightheadedness [ 162 , 192]. Vertigo refers to an
illusion or motion due to a vestibular disorder. Presyncopal faintness results from impaired blood flow to the brain. Disequilibrium
is characterized by imbalance or unsteadiness while standing or walking due to impaired vestibulospinal, proprioceptive, visual, or
motor integration. Nonspecific lightheadedness refers to vague symptoms of floating, swimming, giddiness, rocking, falling, and


### [Page 396 begins | PDF 558]

“spinning inside the head” due to psychogenic or psychiatric disorders such as anxiety, somatoform
disorders, and depression.
Associated Auditory Symptoms
When vertigo is associated with unilateral hearing loss, a primary ear disease should be suspected. A sensation of aural
fullness may occur with external, middle, or inner ear pathology. Autophony, or the reverberation of the patient's own voice in
the affected ear, occurs with external or middle ear disorders.
Tinnitus refers to a sensation of noise in one or both ears in the absence of any obvious stimulus. Tinnitus may be pulsatile or
nonpulsatile. It may be subjective or objective [88 , 133 , 167]. Tinnitus may be paroxysmal or continuous and may be associated
with a variety of otologic infections (otitis media, mastoiditis, labyrinthitis), neoplastic process (vestibular schwannomas,
meningiomas), heterogeneous otologic disorders (presbyacusis, otosclerosis, impacted cerumen, noise exposure), temporal
bone trauma or surgery, Ménière disease, ototoxicity/vestibulotoxicity (antimicrobials, analgesics, chemotherapy,
corticosteroids, diuretics, immunosuppressive agents, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs), sudden onset of SNHL, head
or neck injury, rheumatoid arthritis, arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, hormonal changes during
pregnancy, and a variety of psychological disorders [6 , 54 , 133]. Tinnitus is more frequently reported with peripheral lesions.
Patients with either unilateral tinnitus, pulsatile tinnitus, fluctuating tinnitus, or tinnitus associated with vertigo must be
thoroughly assessed for potentially serious underlying pathologies [138]. Low roaring tinnitus suggests Ménière disease,
whereas high-pitched tinnitus suggests presbyacusis or a vestibular schwannoma. Pulsatile tinnitus may result from various
neoplastic and congenital or acquired arterial or venous disorders, including glomus tumors (paragangliomas), hemangiomas,
meningiomas, arterial tortuosity, vascular loops, persistent stapedial artery, “aberrant” carotid artery, hypertrophic inferior
tympanic artery, high-grade carotid artery stenosis, cervicocephalic arterial dissections, internal carotid artery fibromuscular
dysplasia, intracranial aneurysms, brain arteriovenous malformations, intracranial or extracranial dural arteriovenous fistulae,
high jugular bulb, jugular diverticulum, dehiscent jugular bulb, enlarged jugular vein, and intracranial venous stenoses.
Pulsatile tinnitus may be a presenting feature of glomera jugulare, tympanicum, or jugulotympanicum [ 225]. Intracranial
hypertension due to aqueductal stenosis [198] and idiopathic intracranial hypertension may be associated with pulsatile
tinnitus [101 , 145 , 176 , 226]. Unilateral venous hum tinnitus may be due to turbulent flow in the internal jugular vein [164].
Gaze-evoked tinnitus may follow removal of cerebellopontine angle tumors [224] and may also be associated with saccades,
pursuit, and vestibulo-ocular eye movements. Tinnitus may result from temporomandibular joint disease, Paget disease of the
bone, thyrotoxicosis, anemia, sickle cell disease, endolymphatic sac tumors, high cardiac output, loud cardiac murmurs,
labyrinthitis, perilymphatic fistulas, hydrocephalus, congenital neurosyphilis, palatal tremor, patulous eustachian tube, middle

ear myoclonus, and spasms of the tensor tympani muscle [68 , 89 , 129 , 145]. Unusual repetitive episodes of oscillopsia and
bilateral “sparking” tinnitus may occur every 100 ± 10 seconds, during which cycles of disconjugate opsoclonus, square-wave
jerks, and saccadic dynamic overshoot disrupt stable fixation (see Chapter 8) [212].
Associated Symptoms Suggesting Central Neurologic Dysfunction
Brainstem, cerebellar, and/or cranial nerve dysfunction localizes to the central pathways; associated auditory hallucinations suggest
temporal lobe disease.
Etiologic Search
A thorough history should search for associated viral infections, head or neck trauma, barotrauma, toxin or drug exposure, alcohol
abuse, endocrine and metabolic diseases, cardiovascular disease, or previous luetic infection.
A complete otologic and audiologic evaluation and a detailed neurologic evaluation should emphasize the following:
1. Blood pressure measurements in both arms, including tests for orthostasis.
2. Auscultation for cervical bruits and cardiac arrhythmias.
3. Detailed cranial nerve examination, including repetition of whispering words and numbers, tuning fork evaluation as
previously described, and ocular mobility evaluation, including smooth pursuit, saccades, and fixation suppression, with
special attention to the presence of spontaneous or gaze-evoked nystagmus and presence or absence of skew deviation [27 , 35 ,
108 , 217].
4. Cerebellar testing, with special attention to gait and station abnormalities.
5. With the Romberg test, patients tend to fall to the side of vestibular hypofunction. When taking a few steps with both eyes
closed, patients with unilateral vestibular disorders are prone to veer to the affected side. If asked to take three steps forward
and three steps backward several times with both eyes closed, patients may show a tendency to veer to one side, resulting in a
progressive deviation to

the affected side. Rather than walking on a straight line forward and
backward, the steps resemble a star pattern (star gait). To examine the effects of vestibular function on distal movements,
patients are asked to place the pointed index finger of the outstretched arm in front of the examiner's index finger. Patients
move their arm in a wide arc from above their head to meet the examiner's finger placed in front. Consistent past-pointing is

6. Provocative tests are designed to induce symptoms or positional nystagmus: postural changes, head-thrust test (head-

Cerebral Lesions
The human auditory cortex, located in the superior temporal gyrus (Brodmann areas 41, 42, and 22), is subdivided into an audito-
sensory region (Brodmann area 41) and an audito-psychic region (Brodmann areas 42 and 22).
Even if bilateral, lesions of the auditory cortex do not cause complete deafness. A subtle hearing impairment may be seen with
unilateral lesions, but more often these lesions impair sound localization. Unilateral dominant posterior temporal lesions or bilateral
temporal lobe lesions affecting Heschl gyri may result in pure word deafness, also known as auditory verbal agnosia. This is
characterized by inability to understand spoken language despite normal auditory acuity. With pure word deafness, reading, writing,
naming, and comprehension of nonlanguage sounds are preserved [ 76 , 134]. Bilateral lesions of auditory cortical regions may result
in cortical deafness, generalized or selective auditory agnosia, pure word deafness, amusia, or milder disturbances in the temporal
analysis of sounds [146].
Severe hearing loss may follow bilateral temporal or temporoparietal lesions or bilateral subcortical lesions [ 7 , 79 , 99 , 206 , 230]. In


<!-- PDF page 560 -->

most cases, however, the hearing loss eventually resolves, with only minor residual audiometric deficits associated with variable
impairment in the ability to interpret nonverbal as well as verbal sounds (word deafness or auditory agnosia) [206]. With dichotic
listening tasks, overall performance is worse for stimuli presented to the ear contralateral to the damaged Heschl gyrus. Left
hemispheric lesions impair speech, whereas right hemispheric lesions mainly impair complex-pitch discrimination. Prenatal lesions
result in similar deficits but to a lesser degree compared with adults [161].
Auditory hallucinations resulting from irritative temporal lobe cortical lesions may be simple or complex. Most often, the
hallucinations are perceived by the contralateral ear and are more commonly observed with irritative lesions involving Brodmann
areas 42 and 22, compared with lesions involving Brodmann area 41. Likewise, partial complex seizures of temporal lobe origin
may result in auditory or vertiginous auras [ 90 , 135].
Brainstem Lesions
Due to the binaural representation of the ascending auditory tracts rostral to the cochlear nuclei, brainstem lesions involving the
auditory pathways seldom result in hearing impairment. Bilateral hearing loss may follow severe bilateral brainstem destructive
lesions such as hemorrhages or infarction, including lesions of the inferior colliculus, trapezoid bodies, pons, midbrain tegmentum,
medial geniculate nucleus, and cochlear nuclei. Inferior collicular lesions seldom cause central type of deafness [ 95 , 200 , 219]. Sudden
onset of paracusis and palinacusis may result from discrete hemorrhagic lesions of the medial geniculate nucleus [72]. Seven of 503
patients with vertebrobasilar steno-occlusive disease and four with a locked-in state had sudden onset of bilateral hearing
impairment, associated with tinnitus and vertigo [97].
Pineal and midbrain tumors may result in sudden and complete bilateral deafness (central stem deafness of Brunner[ 201])
presumably due to involvement of the inferior colliculi [199]. Lower midbrain or rostral pontine tegmental lesions may also result in
auditory hallucinations and hearing impairment, likely due to interruption of central auditory pathways, resulting in “release
hallucinations” [ 40]. Lower pontine tegmental hemorrhages may also cause auditory hallucinosis [113 , 118].
Peripheral Nerve Lesions and the Cerebellopontine Angle Syndrome
Peripheral cochlear nerve lesions may result in partial or complete hearing loss, often associated with ipsilateral tinnitus. Hearing
loss is most prominent for high-frequency tones and may result from trauma such as skull-based fractures, infections (e.g., syphilis,
bacterial infections), drugs (e.g., streptomycin, neomycin), AICA aneurysms, or cerebellopontine angle tumors (e.g., vestibular
schwannomas, epidermoids, meningiomas, arachnoid cysts).
The cerebellopontine angle syndrome is often due to vestibular schwannomas [ 85], meningiomas,

cholesteatomas, arachnoid cysts, epidermoids, lipomas, vascular loops of the AICA or posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA),
vertebrobasilar dolichoectasia, aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, and vascular tumors. Vestibular schwannomas originate
from the vestibular Schwann cells of the vestibulocochlear nerve in the internal auditory canal at the glial-Schwann (Obersteiner-
Redlich zone) junction. Vestibular schwannomas usually result in insidious and progressive unilateral SNHL associated with poor
speech discrimination and tinnitus. Some patients have sudden SNHL, most likely as a result of intratumoral hemorrhage or
occlusion of the internal auditory artery. Imbalance, unsteadiness, or disequilibrium is more frequent than vertigo. Some patients
report tinnitus, headaches, and facial paresthesias [70]. As the tumor grows, a full ipsilateral nerve pattern of hearing impairment
ensues, with the tinnitus subsiding as the deafness progresses. With medial tumoral expansion, other neighboring cranial nerves may
be affected, eventually, resulting in brainstem and ipsilateral cerebellar compromise. Progressive tumoral enlargement may also
result in hydrocephalus and raised intracranial pressure.
Anterior tumoral extension may cause ipsilateral facial numbness, paroxysmal facial pain, and depressed ipsilateral corneal reflex
due to the trigeminal nerve involvement. Ipsilateral impairment of ocular abduction and horizontal binocular diplopia result from
involvement of the sixth nerve. Postero-inferior tumoral extension may cause dysphagia, impaired pharyngeal reflexes, and vocal
cord paralysis due to vagus nerve involvement, as well as ipsilateral sternocleidomastoid and trapezius paresis due to spinal
accessory nerve compromise. Facial nerve involvement results in ipsilateral peripheral facial paresis with associated loss of taste on
the anterior two-thirds of the tongue and impaired ipsilateral tearing. There may be hypesthesia of the posterior pinna (Hitselberg
sign), or hemifacial spasm [ 85 , 86 , 139 , 141 , 163]. Vestibular schwannomas associated with neurofibromatosis type 2 are usually
bilateral [140 , 227].

Localization of Lesions Causing Vertigo
Vertiginous syndromes often result in perceptual, ocular motor, postural, and vegetative manifestations: vertigo, nystagmus, ataxia
or postural imbalance, and nausea and/or emesis [28]. Vertigo results from misinformation of corticospatial orientation, nystagmus
from a direction-specific imbalance of the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR), and ataxia or postural imbalance from abnormal activation
of the vestibulospinal pathways. Nausea and/or emesis is due to chemical activation of medullary emetic centers in the area
postrema [28].
Vertigo may be due to peripheral (vestibular labyrinthine), central (vestibular connections), or systemic (endocrine, hemopoietic, or
metabolic disorders) [59 , 66 , 80 , 211].
Peripheral Causes of Vertigo
Lesions of the semicircular canals often cause rotatory sensations, whereas otolithic disorders cause sensations of tilt or levitation. In
cases of acute vertigo due to labyrinthine disease, the involved side may be transiently the more active of the two (irritative phase),
but it soon becomes less active (paretic phase). With eyes closed, patients experience a rotational sensation toward the side opposite
to the paretic labyrinth. Conversely, during the paretic phase, the eyes slowly deviate toward the affected side; the same side patients
tend to past-point and fall if they remain standing with both eyes closed. Patients with severe peripheral vertigo seem more
comfortable lying on one side, usually with the affected ear up. With labyrinthine disorders, acoustic stimuli may result in
paroxysms of vertigo, oscillopsia, postural imbalance, the ocular tilt reaction, and nystagmus (Tullio phenomenon) [58].
Peripheral vestibular syndromes are usually brief with intense paroxysmal vertigo, tinnitus, and hearing impairment. Nystagmus is
typically unidirectional (fast phase “away from” the affected side), horizontal rotatory (never vertical or exclusively rotatory), and
inhibited by visual fixation. The subjective environmental twirl, past-pointing, deviation of the outstretched hands, and falls
associated with the Romberg test are toward the direction of the slow phase of the nystagmus. The peripheral vestibular syndrome is
described as complete and congruent, implying that all “slow deviations” are ipsilateral to the lesion.
Unilateral complete loss of horizontal semicircular canal function (canal paresis) may be detected by

Oscillopsia refers to the sensation of constant motion of the surrounding environment most often resulting from abnormal eye
movements or from impaired VOR [37 , 208 , 215].
Benign Paroxysmal Positioning Vertigo


<!-- PDF page 562 -->

Benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) is a common inner ear disorder characterized by brief attacks of acute and severe
vertigo associated with nystagmus and autonomic symptoms. BPPV is triggered with certain head movements, causing bone crystals
get into the wrong part of the inner ear [ 10]. Cochlear symptoms are typically absent. Most patients have at least one exacerbation
following an initial remission [10]. Patients are otherwise asymptomatic between attacks [ 26]. BPPV most often involves the
posterior semicircular canal. Involvement of the superior semicircular canal is unusual. BPPV may be associated with head trauma,
viral labyrinthitis, Ménière disease, migraines, or inner ear surgery. BPPV results from canalithiasis (“canal rocks,” floating
otoconial debris) or cupulolithiasis (“cupula rocks,” fixed otoconial debris) [196]. Most cases of posterior canal BPPV result from
canalithiasis [5 , 174]. Stray otoconial (calcium carbonate crystals) particles detached from the otoconial layer gravitate and settle on
the cupula of the posterior semicircular canal, causing it to become heavier than surrounding endolymph and thus sensitive to
changes in the direction of gravity [ 26 , 196]. Following rapid head tilt toward the affected ear or following head extension, when the
posterior semicircular canal is moved in the specific plane of stimulation, an ampullofugal deflection of the cupula occurs, with
onset of rotational vertigo following a brief latency of a few seconds. Most commonly, there is upbeat geotropic nystagmus with the
affected ear down. The nystagmus usually adapts and subsequently fatigues [26].

With the lateral or horizontal canal BPPV variant, there is a strong linear horizontal nystagmus beating toward the lowermost ear
induced by rapid turning of the head from side to side around the longitudinal axis. The nystagmus exhibits short latency without
fatigability and often reverses its direction. Vertigo may be induced by turning the head to either side in the supine position and is
always more prominent on the pathologic side. The horizontal variant of BPPV resolves faster than the posterior variant. The
horizontal variant of BPPV has been attributed to canalithiasis and cupulolithiasis [220]; most cases result from cupulolithiasis [100].
Some patients have both variants [12 , 134 , 172 , 203].
Positional vertigo may also result from trauma, infection, ischemia [78], demyelinating disease, neurosarcoidosis [221], Chiari
malformations, posterior fossa tumors, decompression sickness [53], maxillary dental implants [175], use of whole-body vibration
training plate [ 3], intense physical activity [ 74 , 88 , 218], cochlear implants [130], and perilymphatic fistulas.
The provocative positioning maneuver (Dix-Hallpike or Nylén-Bárány) (Fig. 11-2) helps differentiate peripheral from central
positional vertigo.

In normal subjects, these maneuvers do not result in nystagmus. With peripheral lesions, vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and nystagmus
occur following a latency of about 1 to 15 seconds after positional head changes. The nystagmus is typically torsional, geotropic,
and fatigable, often abating within 10 seconds from onset. When patients are rapidly brought back to a sitting position, the
nystagmus beats in the opposite direction (rebound), and with repetition of the provocative maneuver, it habituates, becoming
progressively less evident.
Central vestibular lesions should be suspected and further investigations pursued with any of the following: (a) positioning testing
maneuver is positive when the head is turned to either side, (b) ageotropic positional nystagmus not changing to geotropic, (c)
nystagmus changing direction immediately following shift in position and remaining for as long as the head is kept down, (d)
nystagmus without associated nausea or vomiting, and, if present, vertigo is short lasting (<60 seconds), and (d) the nystagmus lacks

Matutinal vertigo, characterized by vertiginous attacks occurring in the morning, may result from central or peripheral disorders,
and is thus nonlocalizable. Matutinal vertigo may be prevented by altering the sleeping position [ 16 , 231].
Peripheral Vestibulopathy
Peripheral vestibulopathy includes vestibular neuronitis, acute labyrinthitis, epidemic vertigo, and viral labyrinthitis.
Vestibular neuronitis, also known as vestibular neuritis, neurolabyrinthitis, or unilateral vestibulopathy of unknown cause, is a
disorder characterized by sudden severe attacks of prolonged vertigo, spontaneous horizontal-rotatory nystagmus, nausea, vomiting,
and impaired vestibular function on caloric testing. The vertigo is nonpositional, typically rotatory, developing over a few hours, and
at times recurrent [ 9]. There is no associated hearing loss. Vestibular neuronitis has been attributed to inflammation of the vestibular
nerve [24 , 26 , 39].
Acute labyrinthitis is a condition characterized by inflammation of the inner ear or labyrinth. It resembles vestibular neuronitis
except for the associated tinnitus and hearing loss. Usually caused by a virus, acute labyrinthitis may also result from bacterial
infections or exposure to ototoxic drugs. Viral labyrinthitis has been associated with measles, mumps, and rubella.
Disabling positional vertigo or vestibular paroxysmia is a rare episodic peripheral vestibular disorder presenting with short attacks
of spinning or nonspinning vertigo frequently provoked by head or body turns. The condition has been attributed to ephaptic
depolarization secondary to neurovascular pulsatile cross compression of the vestibular nerve by a loop of the AICA, and less so by
a loop of the PICA, the vertebral artery, or a vein [19 , 32 , 98 , 103].
Episodic vertigo followed by gait imbalance and oscillopsia may result from autosomal dominant familial vestibulopathy [11].
Patients with familial vestibulopathy have profound bilateral vestibular loss despite normal hearing [189].
Paroxysms of vertigo and visual blurring associated with complex torsional, horizontal, and vertical nystagmus have also been
observed in association with arteriovenous malformations near the vestibular nuclei in the pons and medulla oblongata [119]. These
recurrent paroxysms of vertigo occurred regularly at 2-minute intervals, each attack lasting for about 15 seconds. The disorder has
been attributed to brief bursts of vestibular nuclei hyperactivity [119].
Episodic vertigo secondary to an abnormal oculovestibular response may also develop in relationship to various optokinetic stimuli,
such as walking down a grocery store aisle or driving a car [94]. Neurologic examination, including optokinetic responses, is
normal. Patients often benefit from the administration of acetazolamide. A familial acetazolamide-responsive vestibulocerebellar
syndrome has also been described in patients with long-standing histories of episodic vertigo, nausea, and vomiting followed by
slowly progressive truncal ataxia [ 14]. Between attacks, patients may have rebound and downbeat nystagmus. The attacks decrease
or abate with acetazolamide.
Ménière Disease
Ménière disease is a rare chronic inner ear disorder characterized by episodic acute and disabling attacks of vertigo, fluctuating
SNHL, and tinnitus [ 52]. In early stages of this disorder, hearing loss affects only low frequencies, fluctuates, and exacerbates
during the acute attacks. Hearing returns to normal after each attack early in the disease. However, as the disease progresses,
residual hearing loss following each attack accumulates, and the hearing loss spreads to higher frequencies. A subjective distortion
of sounds may also occur. There is often a sensation of aural fullness in the affected ear.
Ménière disease is more often unilateral than bilateral. When bilateral, Ménière disease may resemble autoimmune inner ear disease
[25 , 188]. Ménière disease is most often sporadic, but in approximately 5% to 13% of cases there is a positive family history
suggestive of an autosomal dominant pattern [45 , 71 , 114].
The pathophysiology of Ménière disease is poorly understood [120]. Proposed mechanisms include genetic predisposition, trauma,
developmental temporal bone abnormalities, ischemia of the inner ear or endolymphatic sac, autoimmunity, and viral

### [Page 401 begins | PDF 563]

infections [20]. Pathologically, there is buildup of endolymphatic fluid, leading to distention of the semicircular
canals known as endolymphatic hydrops. Other conditions with similar manifestations (Ménière syndrome) include congenital
syphilis; viral and bacterial labyrinthitis; Mondini dysplasia, also known as Mondini malformation or Mondini defect; fenestration
of the otic capsule; Paget disease of bone; hypothyroidism; dyslipidemia; diabetes mellitus; labyrinthine otosclerosis;
dysproteinemia; leukemic infiltrates; labyrinthine concussion; acoustic trauma; temporal bone trauma; Cogan syndrome; relapsing
polychondritis; Eales disease; otorenal syndrome; and vestibular schwannomas [157 , 182 , 183 , 223]. Ménière disease often overlaps
with vestibular migraines [131 , 185].


<!-- PDF page 564 -->

Patients with Ménière disease initially have distressing, fluctuating, and episodic vertigo of variable duration and intensity. Most
patients have associated nausea and vomiting. Nonpulsatile, low-pitched, continuous “roaring” tinnitus and SNHL may precede the
onset of vertigo by months or years. Individual attacks last several minutes to several hours. Between attacks, patients are initially
symptom free, but some have residual disequilibrium. Eventually, there is progressive deterioration of hearing, typically for low-
frequency tones (below 3 kHz), which may culminate in complete hearing loss. Two main variants are recognized: cochlear
Ménière, where vertigo and imbalance are absent, and vestibular Ménière, in which vertigo is prominent but hearing loss, tinnitus,
and ear fullness or ear pressure are absent in the early stages [ 2].
Drop attacks of vestibular origin must be distinguished from drop attacks due to vertebrobasilar ischemia or seizures, or other
conditions. A rare condition, known as Tumarkin's otolithic catastrophe or otolith crisis of Tumarkin is characterized by sudden
episodes of vertigo, during which muscle tone and power are lost, causing the affected person to fall to the ground without warning.
There is no loss of consciousness [ 23 , 126 , 149]. Rarely, patients with Ménière syndrome may have drop attacks, described as a
sensation of being pushed, thrown, or knocked to the ground or as a sudden illusion of environmental movement that cause patients
to fall to the ground without loss of consciousness [13]. The attacks probably result from a sudden mechanical deformation of the
otolithic membrane of the utricle or saccule due to pressure gradients within the inner ear [ 13].
Vertigo Secondary to Middle Ear Disease
Peripheral vertigo may result from acute or chronic ear infections, cholesteatoma, and congenital or acquired syphilis [ 191].
Vertigo Secondary to Viral Infections
Viruses have been implicated in many inner ear disorders [20]. Rubella and CMV are well-established causes of viral-related
congenital hearing loss. Reactivation of latent varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection in the geniculate ganglion may cause a
blistering rash affecting the ear or mouth, otalgia, and an acute peripheral paralysis of the facial nerve. Vestibulocochlear nerve
involvement may result in tinnitus, vertigo, hyperacusis, and hearing loss (Ramsay Hunt syndrome). Affected individuals may also
have multiple, unilateral cranial nerve paralysis as well as C2-C4 dermatomal involvement [17 , 21 , 75 , 87 , 153 , 204]. Ramsay Hunt
syndrome may be complicated by encephalitis, hemiparesis, and posterior circulation strokes [64 , 170].
Vertigo Secondary to Trauma
Closed head injuries or cervical trauma may result in vertigo due to inner ear concussion, temporal bone fracture, or “whiplash”
injury. Cervicogenic vertigo remains a debatable and contested diagnosis [29].
Central Causes of Vertigo
Unlike the peripheral vestibular syndrome, the central vestibular syndrome is often more protracted, and signs of brainstem and
cerebellar dysfunction are usually present. Auditory symptoms are infrequent. Vertigo is less severe and ill-defined. The nystagmus
is bidirectional or unidirectional. It may be exclusively horizontal, rotatory, or vertical and is not impacted by visual fixation. The
directions of subjective environmental rotation, past-pointing, deviation of the outstretched hands, and Rombergism are variable and
barely altered by changes in head position. The central vestibular syndrome can thus be conceptualized as incomplete or partial and
incongruent.
Brandt [26] classified central vestibular syndromes of the brainstem tegmentum into the following three categories:
1. Disorders of the VOR in the horizontal (yaw) plane (horizontal nystagmus and pseudo-vestibular neuritis due to partial
AICA or PICA infarction or multiple sclerosis plaques)
2. Disorders of the VOR in the sagittal (pitch) plane (downbeat nystagmus and vertigo or upbeat nystagmus and vertigo)
3. Disorders of the VOR in the frontal (roll) plane (ocular tilt reaction)
Vascular Causes of the Central Vestibular Syndrome
The inner ear, semicircular canals, saccule, utricle, and cochlea receive their blood supply from the

vertebrobasilar circulation. Not infrequently, vertebrobasilar ischemia results in vertigo and dizziness, thus mimicking an acute
peripheral vestibulopathy [111 , 122]. In a retrospective review of 907 patients presenting to an academic emergency department with
a primary complaint of dizziness, vertigo, or imbalance, the diagnosis of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) was
made in only 32 patients (4%) [162]. Stroke syndromes mimicking an acute peripheral vestibulopathy often involve the territory of
the medial branch of PICA and AICA [122 , 127 , 209].

A normal HIT is a useful bedside test to identify a peripheral vestibular deficit [121 , 123]. The three-step bedside oculomotor
examination—Head Impulse/Nystagmus/Test of Skew (HINTS) has a greater sensitivity than modern neuroimaging with MRI in
ruling posterior circulation strokes in patients presenting with acute vertigo [ 184]. Skew deviation, a vertical misalignment of the
two eyes caused by a supranuclear lesion, is also a good predictor of brainstem involvement and reliably detected stroke when an
abnormal horizontal HIT falsely suggested a peripheral vestibular lesion [27 , 48 , 107 , 108]. A negative HIT, skew deviation, gazed
evoked nystagmus, direction changing nystagmus, and severe truncal instability should raise concerns for possible
brainstem/cerebellar stroke even if the initial brain MRI is normal [46]. Rostral pontomesencephalic lesions often cause ipsilesional
hypertropia, whereas caudal pontomedullary lesions result in contralateral hypertropia. Skew deviation has also been observed
following unilateral vestibular neurectomy and labyrinthectomy and with acute peripheral vestibulocochlear disorders [217].
Vascular causes of the central vestibular syndrome include the following disorders [65 , 67 , 77 , 78 , 210]:
Transient ischemic attacks. Vertigo is a frequent manifestation of vertebrobasilar ischemia. However, isolated vertigo, diplopia,
dysarthria, and dysphagia are usually not considered to be the result of a vertebrobasilar TIAs unless they occur in combination with
or are associated with episodic or bilateral or shifting motor or sensory dysfunction, partial or complete loss of vision in both
homonymous fields, or any possible combination of these symptoms [49 , 67 , 78 , 155]. Vertebrobasilar TIAs may also be associated
with episodes of tinnitus, hearing impairment, and the visual inversion illusion (seeing the ceiling as the floor) [67 , 77 , 78 , 97 , 109 , 155].
Labyrinthine stroke. A labyrinthine infarction resulting from thrombosis, embolism, or vasospasm of the internal auditory artery or
one of its branches may cause vertigo, tinnitus, nausea, and vomiting. With involvement of the cochlear branch, there is associated
deafness [78 , 112]. Intralabyrinthine hemorrhage, a very rare cause of SNHL and peripheral vertigo, has been observed in patients
with leukemias, sickle cell anemia, receiving anticoagulant therapy, coagulopathies, endolymphatic sac tumors, labyrinthitis, and
trauma [151 , 173].
Patients with Wallenberg syndrome (lateral medullary infarction) often experience vertigo and an illusion of environmental tilting.
Wallenberg syndrome most often results from occlusion of the intracranial segment of the vertebral artery and less so by PICA
occlusion. A constellation of signs and symptoms include ipsilateral limb ataxia, facial hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia, paresis of
pharyngeal muscles, Horner syndrome, and contralateral trunk and extremity hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia. Nystagmus,
including head-shaking nystagmus, and a host of oculomotor symptoms result from involvement of ipsilateral vestibular nuclei [47].
Other vascular causes of vertigo include the medial medullary (Dejerine syndrome) infarction [113], AICA territory infarction [121],
basilar migraine [22], subclavian steal syndrome, vertebral artery dissection, and cerebellar infarction. Main symptoms of cerebellar
infarction include headaches, vertigo, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, gait unsteadiness, inability to stand without support even with
eyes opened, limb clumsiness, dysarthria, diplopia, and impaired alertness [165]. Prominent signs include limb and gait ataxia,
dysarthria, nystagmus not suppressed by visual fixation, and impaired alertness. Isolated vertigo is an unusual manifestation of
infarction of the cerebellar nodulus that may mimic an acute vestibular neuritis [197 , 213]. Infarctions of the cerebellar nodulus are
associated with a normal HIT and ageotropic central positional nystagmus [160]. Other findings include periodic alternating
nystagmus, perverted head-shaking nystagmus, paroxysmal positional nystagmus, and impaired tilt suppression of the postrotatory
nystagmus [105 , 148]. Very small border zone cerebellar infarctions may also resemble acute peripheral vestibular disorders,
including canal paresis [110]. Isolated vertigo mimicking vestibular neuritis is an unusual manifestation of superior cerebellar artery
territory infarctions [ 124].
Vestibular migraine, characterized by recurrent vestibular symptoms with migrainous features, is the second most common cause of
recurrent spontaneous vertigo after BPPV. The duration of the attacks varies from seconds to several minutes or several hours, up to
a few days. Tinnitus, hearing loss, and ear fullness are reported in approximately 40% of patients. Diagnostic criteria for vestibular
migraine have been recognized in the appendix of the beta version of the International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd
edition (ICHD-III). Vestibular symptoms proposed as required for the diagnosis of vestibular migraine include spontaneous vertigo,
positional vertigo, visually

and nausea [8 , 42 , 43 , 57 , 104 , 150 , 186 , 202 , 206 , 222].

Vestibular Epilepsy
Episodic vertigo may represent the aura or the sole manifestation of partial complex seizures of temporal lobe origin. Tornado
epilepsy is often associated with a sense of spinning, simulating peripheral vestibulopathies, or Ménière syndrome [166].
Other Central Nervous System Disorders
Many CNS disorders result in dizziness and disequilibrium, including progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP), other atypical
parkinsonisms, and many of the spinocerebellar ataxias [ 44]. In the pediatric population, cerebellar abnormalities (i.e., congenital
absence of the cerebellum, hypoplasia of the cerebellar vermis, Dandy-Walker syndrome), posterior fossa tumors, and Chiari
malformations are among the most common implicated conditions.
Familial periodic ataxia. Individuals with these autosomal dominant syndromes present with episodic dizziness, disequilibrium, and
gait instability [ 41].
Benign paroxysmal vertigo of childhood. Children affected by this condition, likely to be a migrainous precursor or equivalent, may
present with brief (seconds to minutes) and recurrent spells of dizziness, vertigo, or disequilibrium, without cochlear symptoms or
loss of consciousness. During the attacks, children often appear frightened, pale, and sweaty. Caloric testing is often abnormal.
Many of these children also have motion sickness [1 , 15 , 60 , 61 , 92 , 137 , 186 , 190].
Systemic Causes of Dizziness and Vertigo
A number of systemic conditions affecting peripheral or central vestibular structures may result in dizziness or vertigo [232]:
Cardiovascular disorders. Cardiac arrhythmias, aortic stenosis, congestive heart failure, cardiomyopathies, and carotid sinus
hypersensitivity.
Systemic autoimmune disorders and vasculitides. Vertigo is a common manifestation of Cogan syndrome, a rare autoimmune
disorder of young adults characterized by relapsing attacks of vertigo, tinnitus, SNHL, and flares of nonsyphilitic interstitial keratitis
[51]. Vertigo and SNHL may be associated with Behçet syndrome, Vogt-Koyanagy-Harada syndrome, systemic lupus
erythematosus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, relapsing polychondritis, rheumatoid arthritis, granulomatosis with
polyangiitis, Sjögren syndrome, myasthenia gravis, sarcoidosis, Hashimoto thyroiditis, and systemic vasculitides [25 , 73].


<!-- PDF page 567 -->

Hematologic disorders. Anemia, polycythemia vera, Waldenström macroglobulinemia, and other hyperviscosity syndromes.
Hypoglycemia. Dizziness or faintness occurring a few minutes after a meal may be due to reactive hypoglycemia.
Hypothyroidism. Hypothyroidism may be associated with episodic vertigo, SNHL, tinnitus, and signs of cerebellar dysfunction.
Hyperventilation syndrome. Hyperventilation, usually caused by anxiety or panic attacks may result in lightheadedness often
associated with circumoral and digital paresthesias. Rapid lowering of PCO2 levels reduces cerebral blood flow and may result in
dizziness, confusion, and, rarely, seizures.
Multiple sensory deficits. Dizziness and unsteady gait among older patients may result from a combination of sensory deficits,
including visual impairment, proprioceptive deficits, vestibular dysfunction, and cervical spondylosis [59]. Dizziness is especially
prominent during ambulation, particularly when turning corners or changing gait direction. Further contributing factors include use
of sedative and antihypertensive medications, sarcopenia, and fear of falling in open spaces (pseudoagoraphobia) [115].
Drugs. Vestibular toxicity may be transient or permanent and may be associated with symptoms of cochlear toxicity. Dizziness is a
common side effect of many drugs, including analgesics, antiarrhythmics, cytotoxic drugs, anti-inflammatory drugs, aminoglycoside
antibiotics, loop diuretics, aspirin, sedatives, and antiseizure drugs. Dizziness may also result from low-level exposure to carbon
monoxide [117].
Ocular disorders. Vertigo and dizziness may occur in association with glaucoma, extraocular muscle paresis, use of strong
corrective lenses, and refractive abnormalities.
Mal de débarquement (mal de mer). This is a rare disorder of the vestibular system characterized by

### [Page 404 begins | PDF 567]


sensations of motion, rocking, bobbing, and swaying commonly experienced with sea travel that persist among some individuals on
returning to land. Mal de débarquement may also follow air and car travel. Symptoms are worse when patients are not moving, such
as standing still. Typically, symptoms may transiently improve with passive motion activities such as riding a car or train. Patients
with mal de débarquement syndrome do not have vertigo, nausea, or vomiting [38 , 81 , 158 , 214].
Miscellaneous. Dizziness and imbalance has been reported with an occult temporal encephalocele bulging into the left transverse
sinus [50].
Psychiatric disorders (psychogenic dizziness). Dizziness and vertigo can be a complaint in various psychiatric conditions, such as
anxiety disorders, panic attacks, mood disorders, somatoform and dissociative disorders, claustrophobia, agoraphobia, and
schizophrenia. However, psychogenic dizziness should remain a diagnosis of exclusion. A special form of psychogenic vertigo,
thought to be unrelated to panic attacks, is phobic postural vertigo primarily described among patients with obsessive-compulsive
personality [30 , 31]. Phobic postural vertigo has six distinctive characteristics proposed by Brandt et al. [30 , 180].
1. Dizziness and subjective disturbances of balance occur while standing or walking despite normal clinical balance tests.
2. Fluctuating unsteadiness in episodes lasting seconds to minutes or momentary perception of illusory body perturbations.
3. Although attacks can occur spontaneously, there is usually a perceptual stimulus (bridge, staircase, empty room) or social
situation (department store, restaurant, crowd) from which patients have trouble withdrawing and that they recognize as a
provoking factor.
4. Anxiety and distressing vegetative symptoms during or following the vertigo.
5. Obsessive-compulsive type personality, labile affect, and/or mild depression.
6. Onset frequently follows a particular emotionally stressful situation, following a serious illness, or an organic vestibular
disorder.
