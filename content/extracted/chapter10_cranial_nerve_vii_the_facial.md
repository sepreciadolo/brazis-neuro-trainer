CHAPTER 10
Cranial Nerve VII (The Facial Nerve)
Anatomy of Cranial Nerve VII (Facial Nerve)
The facial nerve (cranial nerve [CN] VII) (Fig. 10-1), a mixed, long, and tortuous nerve, is composed of motor, sensory, and
parasympathetic fibers [221]. The facial nerve is commonly divided into four segments (Table 10-1) [168]. Fibers from the motor
division supply the muscles of facial expression (Table 10-2), the stapedius muscle of the inner ear, the stylohyoid, and the posterior
belly of the digastric [ 4 , 36]. Taste sensation to the anterior two-thirds of the tongue is carried by a thinner nerve, the nervus
intermedius of Wrisberg, which has sensory, special sensory, and parasympathetic fibers. Throughout its complex anatomic course,
branches of the facial nerve provide sensation to the skin of the posterior concha of the auricle and external auditory canal and
secretomotor function to the lacrimal glands and sublingual and submandibular salivary glands. In summary, the facial nerve “tears,
snots, tastes, salivates, moves the face, and dampens sounds” [ 29].

Motor Division
Fibers of the motor division arise from the facial motor nucleus, located in the ventrolateral caudal pontine tegmentum, dorsal to the
superior olive, medial to the nucleus of the spinal tract of the trigeminal nerve, and anterolateral to the abducens nucleus. The facial
nerve nucleus harbors the lower motor neurons supplying the muscles of facial expression and is organized and made up of four
separate longitudinally oriented complex cells or subnuclei that supply specific muscle groups [42]: (a) dorsomedial group to the
auricular and occipital muscles, (b) intermediate group to the frontalis and corrugator muscles, (c) ventromedial group to the
platysma, and (d) lateral group to the buccinator and buccolabial muscles. The orbicularis oculi motor neurons are localized to a cap
in the dorsolateral margin of the dorsal aspect of the facial nerve nucleus. A musculotopic organization has been proposed, with
facial motor neurons innervating lower facial muscles primarily found in the lateral aspect of the nucleus, those supplying the upper
facial muscles found in the dorsal aspect of the nucleus, and those innervating the platysma and posterior auricular muscles in the
medial aspect of the nucleus [114]. The intrapontine roots arise dorsally from the motor nucleus running rostrally and dorsally to the
level of the abducens nucleus. These roots then sweep from medial to lateral around the dorsal surface of the abducens nucleus as
the genu of the facial nerve, creating the facial colliculus that bulges into the floor of the fourth ventricle, and then course
ventrolaterally and caudally to emerge on the lateral aspect of the lower pons.
Supranuclear control of facial movements is mediated through corticobulbar fibers originating from the lower third of the precentral
gyrus. These fibers course through the corona radiata, genu of the internal capsule, and medial portion of the cerebral peduncle to
reach the pons. In the pons, most fibers decussate, ending in the contralateral facial motor nucleus. The ventral aspect of the facial
nucleus, which innervates the lower muscles of facial expression, has a predominantly crossed supranuclear control. With
supranuclear lesions, the dorsal aspect of the nucleus, supplying the upper muscles of facial expression such as the frontalis and
orbicularis oculi, is spared due to its bilateral supranuclear control. It has been proposed that descending corticofacial fibers
innervate the lower facial motor nuclear region bilaterally, with contralateral predominance, and that the upper facial motor nuclear
region receives scant direct cortical innervation from either side of the brain [114].
Classic teaching indicates that motor neurons in the upper half of the facial nucleus give rise to axons innervating the lower facial

muscles, whereas neurons in the lower half of the facial nucleus give rise to axons supplying the upper facial muscles [4 , 57 , 203 , 221],
but new evidence suggests at least five cortical facial areas in the frontal and cingulate cortices [ 153]. This schema of supranuclear
facial muscle control remains valid for voluntary facial movements. However, emotional (involuntary) and voluntary facial
movements may be dissociated, and therefore, a separate supranuclear pathway probably exists for the control of involuntary facial
movements. Spontaneous smiling, rather than the voluntary drawing of the corners of the mouth, seems restricted to lesions of the
contralateral striatum, globus pallidus, hypothalamus, and thalamus. Fibers mediating emotional facial movements do not descend
within the internal capsule in their course to the facial motor nuclei. The right cerebral hemisphere is also involved in the control of
supranuclear emotional facial movements and is “dominant” in the expression of facial emotion [33].
Nervus Intermedius
The nervus intermedius of Wrisberg represents the sensory and parasympathetic divisions of the facial

nerve
and contains preganglionic parasympathetic fibers to the submaxillary ganglion (postganglionic fibers go to the submandibular and
sublingual glands) and the pterygopalatine or sphenopalatine ganglion (postganglionic fibers go to the lacrimal, palatal, and nasal
glands). It also receives sensory fibers from the geniculate ganglion, which gathers fibers carrying the sensation of taste from the
anterior two-thirds of the tongue, and also draws afferents from the mucosa of the pharynx, nose, and palate and from the skin of the
external auditory meatus, lateral pinna, and mastoid region.
The parasympathetic fibers arise in the superior salivatory nucleus of the pontine tegmentum; those controlling lacrimation arise
from an associated nuclear mass, the lacrimal nucleus. Gustatory afferents end primarily in the nucleus of the tractus solitarius,
whereas exteroceptive afferents end in the nucleus of the spinal tract of CN V. Some proprioceptive afferents from the facial
musculature also travel in the facial nerve and have their perikarya in the mesencephalic trigeminal nucleus.
Sensory fibers of the nervus intermedius travel through the pons lateral to the motor fibers and together with the motor divisions of
CN VII medially and CN VIII laterally. The nervus intermedius then exits the pons in the cerebellopontine angle to enter the internal
auditory meatus.
Tears are produced by the lacrimal glands (reflex tear secretion), the accessory lacrimal glands of Krause and Wolfring (basal tear
secretion), and the goblet cells of the conjunctiva [134]. Preganglionic parasympathetic neurons responsible for lacrimal secretion
arise from the lacrimal nucleus of the pons. Their axons travel in the nervus intermedius that courses through the cerebellopontine
angle cistern to join the facial nerve. Within the petrous bone, axons destined for the lacrimal gland travel through the geniculate
ganglion without synapsing and then separate from the facial nerve to emerge from the temporal bone in the floor of the middle
fossa as the greater superficial petrosal nerve. The greater superficial petrosal nerve travels under the gasserian ganglion and
enters the vidian canal at the anterior end of the foramen lacerum joining the deep petrosal nerve from the carotid sympathetic
plexus to form the vidian nerve. The vidian nerve passes to the sphenopalatine ganglion in the pterygopalatine fossa, where the
preganglionic lacrimal axons synapse with postganglionic neurons. Postganglionic axons enter the maxillary division of the
trigeminal nerve and travel into the inferior orbital fissure, with its zygomatic branch running in the lateral orbit to reach the lacrimal
gland through anastomosis between the zygomaticotemporal branch and the lacrimal nerve [103].
Anatomy of the Peripheral Course of the Facial Nerve
After emerging from the ventrolateral aspect of the lower pons, the motor division of the facial nerve and the nervus intermedius
proceed laterally in the cerebellopontine angle along with the cochleovestibular nerve (CVN) to enter the internal auditory meatus
together with the internal auditory artery and vein. Four facial nerve segments are recognized within the temporal bone.
Meatal (Canal) Segment
On entering the meatus, the motor division lies on the superoanterior surface of the CVN (CN VIII), with the nervus intermedius
between this division and CN VIII. At this level, the facial nerve runs in close association with the vestibular and cochlear divisions
of CN VIII; no major branches arise from this segment.
Labyrinthine Segment
At the lateral end of the internal auditory meatus, the motor division and the nervus intermedius enter the facial or fallopian canal
of the petrous bone. This segment, the narrowest and shortest segment of the facial nerve, runs almost at right angles to the petrous
pyramid coursing anterolaterally above the labyrinth to reach the geniculate ganglion, containing the pseudounipolar perikarya of
the sensory fibers of the nervus intermedius. The greater superficial petrosal nerve, arising from the apex of the geniculate
ganglion, is composed of preganglionic parasympathetic efferents innervating the lacrimal, palatal, and nasal glands through the
pterygopalatine (sphenopalatine) ganglion. The greater superficial petrosal nerve also contains cutaneous sensory afferent fibers

arising from the skin of the external auditory meatus, lateral pinna, and mastoid.
Horizontal (Tympanic) Segment
From the geniculate ganglion, the facial nerve travels backward, below, and medial to the horizontal semicircular canal. No major
branches originate from this segment.
Mastoid (Vertical) Segment
At the posterior aspect of the middle ear (sinus tympani), the facial nerve changes course bending inferiorly as the mastoid or
vertical segment. Two nerve branches arise from this segment: the nerve to the stapedius muscle originates near the upper end, and
the other branch, the chorda tympani, joins the lingual nerve. This branch contains preganglionic parasympathetic fibers (originating
in the superior salivatory nucleus) and innervates the submandibular and

sublingual glands through the
submaxillary ganglion. The chorda tympani also contains afferent taste fibers from the anterior two-thirds of the tongue destined for
the nucleus tractus solitarius.
After giving off the chorda tympani, the facial nerve exits the facial canal via the stylomastoid foramen. Near its exit, it gives rise to
the posterior auricular nerve (to the occipitalis, posterior auricular, transverse and oblique auricular muscles), the digastric branch
(to the posterior belly of the digastric muscle), and the stylohyoid branch (to the stylohyoid muscle). The facial nerve then pierces
the substance of the parotid gland, dividing at the pes anserinus into five terminal branches: temporal, zygomatic, buccal, marginal
mandibular, and cervical.
Vascular Supply of the Facial Nerve
The intracranial segment of the facial nerve receives its blood supply from the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA); the
intrapetrosal segment receives from the superficial branch of the middle meningeal artery and the stylomastoid branch of the
posterior auricular artery. The extracranial segment receives its blood supply from the stylomastoid, posterior auricular, superficial
temporal, and transverse facial arteries.
Clinical Evaluation of CN VII Function
Diagnostic steps require identification of the lower motor neuron nature of the facial weakness, site of the lesion, underlying
etiology, and clinical grading. A unilateral peripheral facial nerve paralysis is characterized by inability to wrinkle the forehead,
widening of the palpebral fissure, effacement of the nasolabial fold, and drooping of the corner of the mouth. The lesion site can be
deduced by assessing whether there is preservation or impairment of taste in the anterior two-thirds of the tongue (chorda tympani),
hyperacusis (nerve to the stapedius muscle), and pattern of lacrimation (greater superficial petrosal nerve). Although many grading
scales are available, the American Academy of Otolaryngology—Head and Neck Surgery House-Brackmann grading system of
facial nerve functions (Table 10-3) remains the standard [58 , 104 , 223].

Motor Function
Motor function is assessed by facial inspection and detailed assessment of facial movements. Symmetry of eyelid blinking and lip
movements during speech should be noted. Patients are asked to raise the eyebrows, wrinkle the brows, squeeze the eyes tightly,
smile, purse the lips, puff out the cheeks, and retract the chin. Any asymmetry should be noted. The stylohyoid and the posterior
belly of the digastric, occipitalis, and auricular muscles (auricularis anterior, superior, and posterior) cannot be reliably tested.
Unilateral loss of ear wiggling (among natural ear wigglers) is uncommonly observed in patients with Bell palsy [50]. Hyperacusis
resulting from stapedius muscle involvement is more notable for low tones that sound louder on the affected side.
Sensory Function
Sensory examination involves evaluation of taste (salt and/or sugar solutions) on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. Each half of
the protruded tongue is tested with one of the four fundamental tastes (sweet, sour, salty, and bitter), and any asymmetry is
documented.
Reflex Function
The facial nerve is the efferent limb of the corneal and palpebral reflexes, which are ipsilaterally depressed with lower motor neuron


<!-- PDF page 525 -->

facial nerve lesions, while the consensual responses are spared. The orbicularis oculi (glabellar), orbicularis oris, and the palpebral
reflexes may also be depressed with infra-nuclear lesions.
Parasympathetic Function
Infranuclear facial nerve lesions may result in decreased or impaired lacrimation. Testing requires hanging a litmus or filter paper on
each lower lid (Schirmer test). Excessive salivation may also be noted with infranuclear lesions.
Localization of Lesions Affecting CN VII
Supranuclear Lesions (Central or Upper Motor Neuron Facial Paralysis)
With supranuclear lesions, there is paresis of voluntary movements of the muscles of facial expression contralateral to the affected
hemisphere, with relative sparing of the frontalis and orbicularis oculi. The muscles around the mouth are especially affected, but
there may be occasional paresis of the lower or even the upper segment of the orbicularis oculi [114].
Occasionally, there may be a dissociation between voluntary and emotional facial movements [86 , 103]. Volitional facial paresis
without emotional is far more common than emotional facial paresis and results from interruption of corticobulbar pathways due
lesions of the lower precentral gyrus, internal capsule, cerebral peduncle, or upper pons [103]. The reverse pattern emotional or
mimetic facial paresis without a component volitional is observed with lesions anterior to the precentral gyrus, especially if they
involve the right cerebral hemisphere [ 33]. Unilateral emotional facial paresis has also been associated with lesions of the
contralateral supplementary motor area, frontal lobe white matter, mesial temporal lobe and insula, striatocapsular area, anterolateral
thalamus and insula, thalamus and subthalamus, posterior thalamus, posterior thalamus and operculum, dorsal midbrain, and with
postencephalitic parkinsonism [31 , 84 , 91 , 103 , 206].
Isolated volitional facial paresis associated with a mediodorsal midpontine base lacunar infarct suggests that fibers conveying
voluntary orofacial activation descend mediodorsally at the level of the middle pons, whereas fibers conveying emotional activation
are assumed to converge below this level [ 209].
Bilateral upper motor neuron lesions may result in facial diplegia, spastic tongue, dysphagia, and

Nuclear and Fascicular Lesions (Pontine Lesions)
Pontine lesions may affect the facial nerve nucleus or nerve fascicles. Neighboring structures, such as the abducens nucleus,
abducens nerve fascicles, paramedian pontine reticular formation (PPRF), corticospinal tract, spinal tract and nucleus of the
trigeminal nerve, and spinothalamic tract, may be affected as well.
Nuclear and fascicular facial nerve lesions result in peripheral facial nerve palsy. With complete lesions, there is unilateral ipsilateral
paralysis of all mimetic facial muscles, with loss of frontal wrinkling and facial asymmetry observed both at rest and with facial
movements. Patients are unable to frown or raise the eyebrow, close the eye, retract the angle of the mouth, purse the lips, puff out
the cheek, or retract the chin on the affected side. With more subtle involvement, only blink asymmetry with decreased rate of eyelid
blinking on the side of the facial paresis may be present. On attempting to close the eye on the affected side, the eyeball may deviate
up and out (Bell phenomenon) due to relaxation of the inferior rectus and contraction of the superior rectus. The Bell phenomenon
represents a normal response that becomes apparent due to paralysis of eyelid closure. However, it may be lacking in about 10% of
healthy individuals [80]. The cheek often puffs out during breathing, and food crumbs are often retained between the teeth and the
cheek due to buccinator paralysis. Unilateral peripheral facial palsy also results in depressed corneal and palpebral reflexes on the
affected side, with intact consensual responses.
Millard-Gubler Syndrome
The Millard-Gubler syndrome [70] results from ventral pontine lesions interrupting the fascicles of the facial and abducens nerves
and the corticospinal tract and is characterized by:
1. Ipsilateral peripheral facial paralysis
2. Ipsilateral lateral rectus paralysis
3. Contralateral hemiplegia

Foville Syndrome
The Foville syndrome results from pontine tegmental lesions interrupting the fascicles of the facial nerve, the PPRF, and the
corticospinal tract and is characterized by:
1. Ipsilateral peripheral facial paralysis
2. Paralysis of conjugate gaze to the side of the lesion
3. Contralateral hemiplegia
Eight-and-a-Half Syndrome
The eight-and-a-half syndrome results from dorsal tegmental caudal pontine lesions involving the PPRF or the abducens nucleus,
the medial longitudinal fasciculus (MLF), and the nucleus or facial nerve fascicles and is characterized by:
1. Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) plus horizontal gaze palsy
2. Ipsilateral peripheral facial palsy
Isolated Peripheral Facial and Abducens Nerve Palsy
Discrete caudal tegmental pontine lesions involving the facial nerve fascicles (or nucleus) and the abducens nerve fascicles [182]
may result in:
1. Ipsilateral peripheral facial palsy
2. Ipsilateral paresis of ocular abduction
3. No other neurologic abnormalities
Posterior Fossa Lesions (Cerebellopontine Angle Lesions)
In the posterior fossa, the motor division of the facial nerve is in close proximity to the nervus intermedius of Wrisberg and the
CVN. Cerebellopontine angle lesions (e.g., vestibular schwannoma, meningioma, epidermoid, metastasis, arteriovenous
malformation) may result in the following findings:
1. Ipsilateral peripheral facial paralysis (including impaired taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue) without hyperacusis
2. Ipsilateral tinnitus, deafness, and vertigo
Cerebellopontine angle lesions frequently involve neighboring structures, including the pons (nystagmus or ipsilateral gaze palsy),
cerebellar peduncles and cerebellum (ipsilateral ataxia), trigeminal nerve (ipsilateral facial pain and/or sensory changes), and
abducens nerve (ipsilateral lateral rectus palsy).
Lesions Affecting the Meatal Segment of the Facial Nerve in the Temporal Bone
In the temporal bone, the facial nerve is closely associated with the CVN. Therefore, lesions at this site such as temporal bone
fractures, primary or secondary tumors, result in clinical findings similar to those seen with the cerebellopontine angle syndrome,
except for lack of hyperacusis.

Lesions Affecting the Facial Nerve within the Facial Canal Distal to the Meatal Segment but Proximal to
the Exit of the Nerve to the Stapedius Muscle
Lesions within this segment of the facial nerve involve the motor division and the nervus intermedius, resulting in ipsilateral
peripheral facial paralysis, impaired taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue, and hyperacusis. With lesions proximal to the
greater superficial petrosal nerve, lacrimation is impaired; with distal lesions, lacrimation is normal. Geniculate ganglion
involvement, as in cases of reactivation of latent varicella zoster virus (VZV), results in unilateral peripheral facial paralysis,

hyperacusis, impaired taste, geniculate neuralgia, and herpetic vesicles involving the eardrum, external auditory meatus, or palate (
Ramsay Hunt syndrome). Some patients also exhibit hyperemia of the concha or helix and vestibulocochlear dysfunction, causing
tinnitus, hearing loss, vertigo, and nystagmus. In some instances, the facial paralysis occurs without an associated herpetic rash (
zoster sine herpete).
Lesions Affecting the Facial Nerve within the Facial Canal Between the Exit of the Nerve to the Stapedius
and the Exit of the Chorda Tympani
Lesions within this segment cause facial paralysis and impaired taste on the anterior two-thirds of the tongue. As the lesion is distal
to the nerve to the stapedius, hearing is spared.
Lesions Affecting the Facial Nerve in the Facial Canal Distal to the Exit of the Chorda Tympani
Lesions in this segment result in peripheral facial paralysis without hyperacusis or impaired taste.
Lesions Distal to the Stylomastoid Foramen
Lesions in this segment result in isolated peripheral facial paralysis. At times, individual motor branches are affected, resulting in
isolated paralysis limited to few individual facial muscles. The facial nerve may be involved by inflamed retromandibular lymph
nodes, parotid gland tumors, or infections (e.g., sarcoidosis, infectious mononucleosis). Weakness confined to one or two facial
muscles on the same side of the face may result from facial trauma (obstetric forceps), parotid gland neoplasms, or perineural spread
of skin cancers [137]. Other etiologies listed in Table 10-4 include Lyme disease, leprosy, hepatitis E, acquired immune deficiency
syndrome (AIDS), leukemia, inflammatory pseudotumor of the middle ear, and acupuncture treatment for temporomandibular joint
dysfunction [25 , 30 , 53 , 67 , 98 , 99 , 128 , 154 , 180 , 184 , 224].
Hyperostosis cranialis internal may result in recurrent facial nerve palsies; other CNs such as CNs I, II, and VIII may also be
affected [23].

Peripheral facial nerve paralysis has also been associated with the administration of an intranasal inactivated influenza vaccine [157],
influenza vaccination [51 , 202], hepatitis B vaccination [ 6], interferon-alpha and ribavirin [20], and vaccines against HIV and
tuberculosis using a nontoxic mutant of Escherichia coli[ 131].
Patients with Bell palsy often may have pain in or around the ear few days preceding the onset of unilateral facial weakness,
dysgeusia, hyperacusis, and decreased tearing [2 , 3 , 22 , 48 , 49 , 95 , 215]. The paralysis is rapid in onset (within 72 hours). The facial
paralysis reaches its nadir within a couple of weeks or less. All actions of the facial muscles, whether carried by voluntary, reflex, or
emotional input, are compromised. Thorough otoneurologic examination is usually normal, except for variable impairment of
function of facial nerve-innervated muscles. Transient facial numbness may be experienced by some patients [ 2 , 3]; a small
percentage of patients have dysfunction of other CNs, including the glossopharyngeal, cochleovestibular, and even the contralateral
facial nerve [2 , 3]. A peripheral facial nerve paralysis may result in eating and drinking difficulties, and transient disturbances of
oropharyngeal swallowing have been electrophysiologically demonstrated in two-thirds of patients [ 193].
Patients with Bell palsy and other types of peripheral facial nerve paralysis are at risk of corneal ulcerations because a weakened
orbicularis oculi muscle causes lagophthalmos and, in severe cases, paralytic ectropion. Some patients may have overflow of tears
onto the face (epiphora) or, conversely, a dry eye.
Most patients with Bell palsy have a favorable prognosis. Recovery usually begins within 2 to 4 weeks, reaching its maximum
within 6 months (6 to 12 months). Recurrences are rare [110]. However, some patients have disabling sequelae. Lower rates of
recovery are seen among patients with reactivation of VZV infection or those with loss of the stapedial reflex. Rarely, recovery may
be followed by transient or long-lasting motor synkinesis, myokymia, blepharospasm-like activity, or hemifacial muscle
contractions associated with normal facial movements. In some cases, aberrant regeneration may result in involuntary tearing of the
eye on the involved paretic side (crocodile-tears or Bogorad syndrome) or gustatory sweating (Frey syndrome) when
parasympathetic fibers to the salivary glands are rerouted to the sweat glands [210].
Occult parotid malignancies rarely present with acute-onset unilateral peripheral facial paralysis [173]. In highly endemic areas,
Lyme disease is responsible for one-fourth of cases of peripheral facial paralysis [26 , 44].
Recurrent orofacial swelling involving the lips, face, and eyelids; unilateral or bilateral facial nerve palsy; cheilitis; and fissured
tongue (scrotal tongue or lingua plicata) characterize the Melkersson-Rosenthal syndrome [ 69 , 94]. This rare disorder may be
associated with hyperhidrosis, acroparesthesias, migraines, retrobulbar optic neuritis, ocular muscle paresis, Crohn disease, and

contiguous fasciae and muscles [45]. Rarely, recurrent idiopathic peripheral facial nerve paralysis may be associated with episodic
ophthalmoplegia [123].

Abnormalities of Tear Secretion
Supranuclear lesions resulting in pseudobulbar palsy are often associated with inappropriate bouts of crying, often unaccompanied
by sadness. Unilateral pontine lesions involving the facial nerve nucleus may affect the superior salivatory nucleus, leading to
decreased salivary flow, and the lacrimal nucleus, causing an associated dry eye. However, brainstem lesions may produce facial
paresis with sparing of taste and tearing due to the anatomic separation of the motor fibers from the sensory-parasympathetic
components [151]. As the nervus intermedius is adjacent to the facial nerve and the CVN in the cerebellopontine angle and internal
auditory meatus, lesions in the region may result in a dry eye associated with ipsilateral unilateral peripheral facial nerve paralysis,
impaired taste, hyperacusis, hearing loss, and vestibular symptoms. A peripheral facial nerve palsy associated with ipsilateral
impairment in reflex tearing suggests a lesion at the petrous bone or the cerebellopontine angle or both [145]. Tumors of the internal
auditory canal may cause deficient production of tears ipsilateral to the lesion, often before any evidence of facial paresis or corneal
hypesthesia [171]. Occasionally, vestibular schwannomas may cause excessive lacrimation, at times observed only during
consumption of meals [21 , 171].
Lesions of the floor of the middle fossa near the gasserian ganglion may injure the greater superficial petrosal nerve, impairing tear
secretion [ 145]. Impaired tear secretion ipsilateral to the side of an acquired abducens nerve palsy usually indicates an extradural
lesion in the middle cranial fossa, such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma [145]. Lesions of the sphenopalatine ganglion such as
pterygopalatine fossa tumors result in unilateral decreased tearing and dryness of the nasal mucosa, with associated paresthesias or
hypesthesia in the maxillary distribution of the trigeminal nerve. Unilateral reduction in tear secretion in patients with a known
tumor or infection of the maxillary or sphenoid sinuses indicates disease extension beyond sinuses confines [145]. Trauma or tumors,
especially metastatic carcinoma, may damage the zygomaticotemporal nerve, resulting in reduced reflex tear secretion. Reduced tear
secretion may also occur in cases of diffuse dysautonomias, such as the Riley-Day syndrome, acute pandysautonomia, or multiple
system atrophies [145].
Abnormalities of Eyelid Closure
Volitional eyelid blinking is under bilateral, but mainly contralateral, precentral supranuclear cortical

### [Page 380 begins | PDF 535]

control
and is mediated through the control of the facial nerves of orbicularis oculi muscles functioning. Emotional eyelid blinking and
periodic spontaneous eyelid blinking are probably controlled by extrapyramidal pathways originating from or conveyed through the
putamen and globus pallidus, cingulate gyrus, amygdala, and thalamus. The typical spontaneous, voluntary, or reflex eyelid blink
consists of a rapid down phase followed by a slower return to the open position, with down-phase velocities nearly twice as fast as
the up-phase velocities. The neural firing pattern underlying both the upward and the downward phases of eyelid blinking is
probably similar to that observed in generation of saccadic eye movements, with a down phase resulting from a pulse-type firing
pattern and an up phase resulting from a pulse-step firing pattern (see Chapter 8). The open eyelid position is maintained by the
tonic activity of the levator palpebrae muscles; inhibition of the levator palpebrae and simultaneous contraction of the orbicularis
oculi result in eyelid blink.
A review of eyelid function noted that the role of the upper eyelid as the protector of the eye comprises a number of distinct
functions [191]:


<!-- PDF page 536 -->

1. Tonic eyelid elevation when the eyes are open
2. Voluntary eye closure and eye opening
3. Involuntary adjustment of the eyelid to the vertical globe position, that is to say, lid-eye coordination
4. Periodic and reflex blinking
5. Firm eye closure in protective and expressive acts, for example, sneezing
During the first three listed functions, only the levator palpebrae muscle is active, whereas in the last two functions listed, different
parts of the orbicularis oculi muscle contract, whereas the levator palpebrae is synchronously inhibited. Therefore, in cases of facial
nerve palsy, the position of the upper eyelid, gentle closure of the eye, and lid-eye coordination are unaffected, whereas blinking and
tight closure of the eye are impaired [ 123].
The rate of eyelid blinking reflects central dopaminergic activity. Decreased rate of periodic eyelid blinking may occur with
progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) and other disorders associated with parkinsonism. Increased rate of eyelid blinking may occur
with drug-induced dyskinesias, Tourette syndrome, and schizophrenia [115]. In patients with parkinsonism, the timing and
reciprocity of the levator and orbicularis oculi muscles activity may be disturbed during blinking. Coactivation or a tremor-like
rhythmic reciprocal activity of the levator and orbicularis oculi muscles may be present upon light eyelid closure, whereas eyelid-
eye coordination remains intact [134]. This suggests that in addition to their influence on eyelid blink frequency, the dopaminergic
basal ganglia pathways play a role in the inhibition of the levator during eyelid blinks and eye closure [191]. Loss of spontaneous
blinking has also been described in patients with Balint syndrome [218].
Eyelid Closure Insufficiency
Insufficiency of eyelid closure may be due to supranuclear lesions, brainstem lesions involving the CN VII nucleus, peripheral facial
nerve damage, or disorders of the neuromuscular junction (e.g., myasthenia gravis) or muscles (e.g., myotonic dystrophy). Cortical
or subcortical precentral gyrus lesions may result in paresis of volitional contralateral eyelid closure, with relative sparing of
spontaneous and emotional eyelid closure. Acquired inability to wink (Revilliod sign) may be an early sign of corticobulbar disease.
Nondominant frontal lobe lesions or, more commonly, bilateral frontal lobe lesions may result in compulsive eye opening[ 28 , 152].
These patients are unable to initiate bilateral voluntary eyelid closure despite retention of their ability to comprehend the task and
intact reflex eyelid closure. Apraxia of eyelid closure has been described with Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, amyotrophic lateral
sclerosis, static encephalopathies, and PSP [81 , 89 , 111 , 129]. Occasionally, only one eyelid is affected [28 , 62]. Corpus callosum
damage has been thought as the possible explanation for the unilateral impaired volitional eyelid closure that is observed
contralaterally to an anterior cerebral artery territory infarction [120]. Bilateral hemispheric or unilateral nondominant hemispheric
lesions may result in motor impersistence. When patients are requested to close the eyelids and keep them closed, they are unable to
complete the task [ 79]. With motor impersistence, the eyelids close, often develop a fine tremor, and then almost immediately
reopen. Failure to keep the eyelids closed may occasionally affect only the contralateral eyelid in cases of unilateral hemispheric
lesions; this is more frequently observed with right-sided brain lesions [ 62]. Motor impersistence may also occur with parkinsonism
[134]. As a result of the inability to close the eyes, the condition often turns into eyelid closure impersistence [191]. Because gentle
eyelid closure results from the inhibition of the levator palpebrae muscles, disorders of voluntary eyelid closure are likely caused by
deficits in the cortical inhibition of levator palpebrae muscle function rather than of orbicularis oculi muscle activation [191].
Excessive Eyelid Closure and Blepharospasm
Spasmodic contralateral eyelid closure may occur with focal seizures (frontal epileptiform focus). Excessive eyelid closure is,
however, most often caused

### [Page 381 begins | PDF 536]

by blepharospasm, characterized by repetitive, involuntary, bilateral contraction
of the orbicularis oculi muscles, which may render the patient functionally blind [92]. The clinical manifestations of blepharospasm
have been associated with pathologic changes involving the primary motor cortex, anterior cingulate gyrus, and premotor and
supplementary motor cortices [122]. Positron emission tomography (PET) with (18) fluorodeoxyglucose [PET (18) FDG] has shown
involvement of several cortical and subcortical areas, including the inferior frontal lobes, caudate, thalamus, pons, and cerebellum [
105 , 118]. Functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) studies suggest activation of the striatum, frontal and parietal operculum,
supplementary motor area, primary sensorimotor cortex, various visual areas, and the cerebellum [192].
Blepharospasm is most often idiopathic, also known as essential blepharospasm, and is considered a subtype of focal cranial
dystonia [11 , 97 , 112]. Blepharospasm may be accompanied by spasmodic torticollis [108]. Blepharospasm may also occur with
oromandibular dystonia, as in the Meige syndrome (idiopathic blepharospasm-oromandibular dystonia) [108].
Blepharospasm has also been reported in patients with multiple sclerosis plaques involving the rostral brainstem [113] and with


<!-- PDF page 537 -->

bilateral infarctions of the rostral brainstem, diencephalon, and striatum [60 , 117]. Asymmetric blepharospasm may also follow left
frontal cortical infarctions [ 216]. Bilateral transient blepharospasm may be a feature of nondominant striatal infarctions [ 93].
Blepharospasm may be seen with multiple system atrophy cerebellar type (MSA-C) and may be caused by rostral brainstem lesions
disrupting central dopaminergic and cholinergic pathways, resulting in the disinhibition of brainstem reflexes or denervation
supersensitivity of the facial nuclear complex [ 109]. Blepharospasm also occurs as a consequence of painful ocular disease, such as
conjunctivitis, drug-induced tardive dyskinesia, postencephalitic disorders, Parkinson disease, Wilson disease, Huntington disease,
PSP, and pantothenate kinase-associated neurodegeneration [75 , 106 , 108]. Blepharospasm has also been described with retinal
degeneration and familial apoceruloplasmin deficiency [150] and as a familial syndrome associated with dystonia and pigmentary
retinopathy [ 56]. Very seldom blepharospasm has a functional basis. Myasthenia gravis may at times mimic essential blepharospasm
[181].
Reflex blepharospasm has been observed among patients with hemiplegia after vascular insults, predominantly involving the
nondominant temporoparietal lobes. In these instances, the blepharospasm is usually limited to the nonparalyzed side of the body
and is often elicited when the examiner attempts to hold both eyelids open. Following right middle cerebral artery territory
infarctions, some patients have a tendency to close more tightly the ipsilateral right eye compared to the left eye [163].

Dyskinetic Movements
Oral-facial dyskinesia results in a constellation of involuntary movements of the face, jaw, lip, and tongue, including facial
grimaces, distortions, expressions, and twitches; wide opening, tight closing, up-and-down movements and lateral deviation of the
jaw; puckering, pursing, opening, and closing lip movements; and/or protrusion, writhing, and distorted posturing of the tongue [ 165
].
Orofacial dyskinesias in the elderly may occur spontaneously, but usually result from side effects of neuroleptics, lack of dentures
(edentulous dyskinesia), Huntington chorea, Wilson disease, and neuroacanthocytosis [ 32]. Orofacial dyskinesias may also occur in
drug-naïve patients with schizophrenia, suggesting that abnormal involuntary movements, particularly orofacial dyskinesia, may
represent a neuromotor component of schizophrenia [172].
Dystonic Movements (Blepharospasm and Blepharospasm with Oromandibular Dystonia)
Blepharospasm, a common focal dystonia, is characterized by episodic or sustained involuntary, spasmodic, forceful, bilateral, and
symmetric contractions of the orbicularis oculi muscles. The muscle contractions may spread to involve other facial muscles.
Blepharospasm usually results from Parkinson disease, PSP, neuroleptic-induced tardive syndromes, and lithium intoxication [144].
Blepharospasm has

### [Page 382 begins | PDF 537]

also been observed among patients with bilateral thalamic infarctions [148], putaminal
hemorrhages [212], and lower pontine tegmental lesions [12]. When severe, blepharospasm may interfere with daily activities, such
as driving or walking. Occasionally, sensory tricks or geste antagoniste may decrease the blepharospasm [90].


<!-- PDF page 538 -->

When blepharospasm is associated with oromandibular dystonia, the disorder is known as Meige syndrome, which is typically
manifested as sustained perioral grimacing, jaw clenching, platysma contraction, and sustained neck flexion [108]. The abnormalities
may spread beyond the facial and nuchal musculature to involve one or both arms and the trunk. The cause of Meige syndrome is
unknown, but likely basal ganglia dysfunction may be involved. Brueghel syndrome refers to a rare dystonia of the motor
component of the trigeminal nerve that typically results in involuntary opening of the mouth. Additional features may include
upbeat nystagmus and paroxysmal hyperpnea [85]. Orofacial dystonia has also been associated with the use of bupropion and St.
John wort [147].
Hemifacial Spasm
Hemifacial spasm refers to unilateral, intermittent, involuntary, hyperactive dysfunction of the seventh CN, most commonly due to a
vascular loop abutting the facial nerve at the root exit zone (REZ) [1 , 15 , 76 , 83 , 101 , 151 , 219]. Hemifacial spasm is characterized by an
insidious onset of painless, arrhythmic, tonic, or clonic intermittent spasms usually affecting the orbicularis oculi. The spasms
gradually progress to involve other ipsilateral facial muscles, especially the orbicularis oris, buccinator, and/or platysma [76].
Atypical cases of hemifacial spasm, characterized by spasms starting in the orbicularis oris and buccinator muscles and gradually
spreading to involve the orbicularis oculi muscle, are rather unusual [188]. Involvement of the stapedius muscle may result in
ipsilateral ear clicking. Hemifacial spasm may be exacerbated by emotional stress, anxiety, nervousness, and fatigue [217]. Rarely
painful, the movements may persist during sleep. Ipsilateral facial weakness may occur with chronic cases, as well as in rare
instances following microvascular decompression [76]. When bilateral, these spasms are asynchronous and asymmetric. Bilateral
alternating hemifacial spasm has been observed in cases of multiple sclerosis [211].
Lesions accounting in hemifacial spasm may occur anywhere from the facial motor nucleus to the stylomastoid foramen [ 71].
Lesions located in the ipsilateral cerebellopontine angle (e.g., tumors, vascular malformations, dolichoectatic basilar artery,
aneurysms, arachnoid cysts, neuroglial cysts, lipomas, and bony abnormalities of the skull) that either compress or angulate the
facial nerve at or near the REZ, a transition zone between central and peripheral axonal myelination (synonymous with the
Obersteiner-Redlich zone), are the most common cause [1 , 14 , 17 , 41 , 66 , 100 , 130 , 139 , 164 , 195]. Intrapontine (e.g., multiple sclerosis,
pontine gliomas, and other intramedullary tumors [73 , 162], lacunar infarction [7 , 213]), and intratemporal lesions (e.g., hemangioma
of the geniculate ganglion) have also been implicated [13]. Neurovascular conflict due to arterial loops of the AICA or posterior
inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is frequently found during microvascular decompressive surgical procedures [18 , 187]. The vertebral
artery, internal auditory artery, and veins at the facial nerve REZ have also been incriminated [159]. Hemifacial spasm has also been
associated with tentorial dural arteriovenous fistulas [63], focal temporal bone hyperostosis [149], facial nerve compression within
the parotid space [175], parotid gland tumors [24], schwannomas of the intermediate nerve[ 124], contralateral vestibular
schwannomas causing brainstem displacement and distortion [161], and idiopathic intracranial hypertension [194]. Proximal ephaptic
transmission among facial nerve fibers or a kindling effect due to compression may mediate this disorder [71 , 78]. Structural studies
have shown partial demyelination and axonal degeneration of the facial nerve as a result of neurovascular compression [186]. In
some cases, hemifacial spasm has been associated with Chiari type I malformation [34 , 55 , 77] and multiple cranial neuropathies.
When coexistent with trigeminal neuralgia, the condition is known as tic convulsif[ 72 , 141]. In other instances, hemifacial spasm
followed Bell palsy or traumatic facial nerve injury [65]. Albeit unusual, hemifacial spasm has been associated with diabetic
ketoacidosis [16]. Quite seldom, hemifacial spasm has been observed among young children and adolescents [119], such as the
observation of a 6-year-old child with otitis media with resolving manifestations following insertion of tympanostomy tubes [122].
Rare familial cases suggesting a genetic predisposition have also been reported [217]. An association between rostral left
ventrolateral medullary compression and essential arterial hypertension has been hypothesized [47 , 51 , 158], and in rare instances, it
has been associated with neurogenic sinus bradycardia [225]. Likewise, rare instances of syncope due to neurovascular compression
by vertebral artery ectasia have been described [201].
Postparalytic Spasm and Synkinetic Movements
Following recovery from a peripheral facial nerve palsy (e.g., Bell palsy), various motor overflow

### [Page 383 begins | PDF 538]


phenomena may occur as a result of aberrant nerve regeneration or ephaptic transmission, including postparalytic hemifacial spasm,
the “crocodile-tears” phenomenon (eating provokes lacrimation), facial contractures, and various synkinesias. These synkinesias
result from abnormal synchronization of movements of different muscle groups that normally do not contract together, such as
contraction around the mouth with eyelid blinking and eyelid closure upon jaw opening or lateral jaw movements (Marin Amat
syndrome or inverse Marcus Gunn phenomenon) [166]. Synkinesis seldom occurs in muscles innervated by two different CNs, such
as the rare facial-trigeminal synkinesis [ 185]. With postparalytic facial muscle contractures, the more “relaxed” and normal
contralateral side may appear weak on casual inspection. However, when facial movements are elicited, the pathologic side is
properly revealed. These various abnormalities probably result from faulty fiber regeneration following peripheral facial nerve
lesions or abnormal activity of residual motor units.

Facial Myokymia
Facial myokymia is a rare facial dyskinesia characterized by fine, continuous, rippling, undulating movements of the facial muscles
occasionally associated with facial contracture or facial weakness [96]. In many instances, facial myokymia results from intrinsic
pontine tegmental lesions involving the postnuclear and postgenu segment of the facial nerve [ 8 , 107 , 143 , 160 , 205]. Facial myokymia
has been described with pontine neurocysticercosis [28]; GBS [ 59 , 140], limbic encephalitis [10], autosomal dominant facial
myokymia and dystonic/choreic movements linked to chromosome 3p21-3q21 [176], syringobulbia [174], cerebellar and
cerebellopontine angle tumors, meningeal carcinomatosis or sarcomatosis, radiation therapy [132], basilar invagination, phosgene
poisoning, cardiac arrest [ 46 , 74 , 155], and even in rare instances of brain death [190]. Bilateral facial and limb myokymia may follow
timber rattlesnake envenomation [35]. In a patient with syringobulbia and facial myokymia, neither the facial motor nucleus nor the
facial nerve was pathologically involved [179]; the myokymia in this case was thought to be caused by interruption of aberrant
corticobulbar fibers at the medullary level, accounting for disinhibition of a “rhythmic neural generator” in the facial nucleus. Facial
myokymia has also been described as a false localizing sign in a patient with obstructive hydrocephalus; the myokymia resolved
following shunting [189]. Finally, benign myokymia among healthy subjects is confined to the eyelids and, in most instances, results
from or is exacerbated by fatigue.
Myokymia may be associated with spastic paretic facial contracture [146]. In this disorder, the myokymia begins around the
orbicularis oculi muscle and gradually spreads to unilaterally involve most of the facial muscles. Associated tonic contractures of
the involved muscles develop simultaneously, eventually resulting in decreased voluntary facial movements on the involved side.
The nasolabial groove deepens, the corner of the mouth is drawn laterally, the palpebral fissure narrows, and all facial muscles
weaken. Myokymia with spastic paretic facial contracture results from damage to the dorsal pons at the level of the facial motor
nucleus and is especially observed with brainstem neoplasms [123 , 146 , 200]. Oculofacial or oculomasticatory myorhythmia has been
considered to be pathognomonic of Whipple disease, a multisystem disorder caused by Tropheryma whipplei [ 9]. Patients with
oculomasticatory myorhythmia exhibit pendular vergent ocular oscillations and synchronous contractions of the masticatory
muscles, but not the palatal muscles [178]. Facial “myorhythmia” has been associated with the use of interferon-alpha2a [204].
Focal Cortical Seizures
Clonic movements of the face, usually spreading to involve other muscles, may be associated with epileptogenic foci, affecting the
lower aspect of the precentral gyrus.
Tics and Habit Spasms
Tics and habit spasms are characterized by abrupt, repetitive, stereotyped, simple or complex movements that often involve muscles
outside the distribution of the facial nerve. The movements can be reproduced or inhibited voluntarily. Thought to be of
psychogenic origin, their association with Tourette syndrome (involuntary tic-like movements or vocalizations, sometimes with
associated coprolalia) suggests the possibility of basal ganglia dysfunction.
Fasciculations
Fasciculations of the facial muscles characterized by spontaneous twitches of individual muscle fascicles may occur with any
disorder affecting the facial nerve nucleus or nerve fascicles. Fasciculations of the facial muscles, tongue, and limbs are commonly
observed in the Kennedy syndrome (X-linked bulbospinal neuronopathy), an X-linked recessive disorder resulting from a
trinucleotide repeat expansion in the androgen receptor gene.
Myoclonus
Rhythmic facial movements may occur in association with palatal tremor, a rhythmic, continuous palatal contraction that persists
during sleep and occurs

### [Page 384 begins | PDF 539]

with lesions involving the red nucleus, inferior olive, or dentate nucleus, or their
connecting pathways (Guillain-Mollaret triangle). Sternocleidomastoid and facial asynchronous myoclonus have also been reported
in association with vertebral artery dolichoectasia displacing the medulla oblongata; direct compression of the eleventh and seventh
CNs by the dolichoectatic vessel has been proposed as the plausible mechanism [156]. Isolated unilateral right facial reflex
myoclonus has also been reported to be triggered by speaking and writing, but not by non-linguistic tasks, suggesting a left rolandic
opercular cortical origin [19]. Facial myoclonus has also been described in cases of hypocalcemia [208], serotonin syndrome [222],
sleep-related faciomandibular myoclonus [135], Rasmussen encephalitis [38], topiramate use [125] and with Kufor Rakeb disease,
and autosomal recessive form of levodopa-responsive parkinsonism associated with facial-faucial-finger mini-myoclonus [ 220].
