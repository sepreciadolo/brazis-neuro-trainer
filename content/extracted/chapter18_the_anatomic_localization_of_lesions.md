The Anatomic Localization of Lesions in the Thalamus
Functional Anatomy of the Thalamus
The paired thalamic nuclei are egg-shaped structures of gray matter located on both sides of the third ventricle [148]. They represent
the largest portion of the diencephalon; other diencephalic structures are the epithalamus (pineal and habenular complex), the
subthalamic nucleus, and the hypothalamus. The thalamus plays a major role in cortical activation [165]. It projects to the cortex in a
highly organized manner. Characteristically, thalamic connections are reciprocal, that is, the target of the axonal projection of any
given thalamic nucleus sends back fibers to that nucleus. Nevertheless, thalamocortical projections are often larger than their
corticothalamic counterparts (e.g., the geniculocalcarine projection). Anatomically and functionally, four regions can be
distinguished in the thalamus: anterior, posterior, medial, and lateral, partially separated from each other by white matter laminae [
120 , 203 , 292]. In the core of the thalamus, a fifth region is encased by these laminae, the intralaminar nuclei [300]. Finally, the lateral
aspect of the thalamus is covered by a layer of myelinated axons, the external medullary lamina, housing in its core clusters of cells,
the reticular nucleus of the thalamus [280]. Thalamic nuclei and their connectivity can be assessed by neuroimaging [125 , 282]. A
simplified account of the thalamic nuclei and their connections, pertinent to clinical localization, is given in Table 18-1. Figure 18-1
illustrates the position and main cortical projections of the thalamic nuclei. It is important to note that thalamic nomenclature is not
uniform across authors and is largely derived from the mapping of anatomic subregions in rodents and non-human primates. For this
reason, the anatomic names only loosely correspond to the real position of the thalamic nuclei in man. For instance, the ventrolateral
(VL) nucleus is not only ventral in humans, but extends to the highest level of the thalamus, in this sense being actually not only
ventral but dorsal as well (
Video 18-1).

To facilitate clinical diagnosis, the complex thalamic anatomy [108 , 229] (Video 18-1) can be divided into four main
anatomofunctional regions as follows:
1. The midline, intralaminar, reticular, and some areas of the ventral anterior nuclei mediate general cortical alerting responses
and may be termed nonspecific thalamic nuclei[ 60 , 129]. By contrast, specific thalamic nuclei receive sensory information
from the body, process it, and project the pertinent output to specific areas of the cortex, such as the somatosensory area and
visual cortex. Whereas the synapses of specific cells have narrow and low-threshold action potentials, those from nonspecific
cells tend to have neuromodulating properties [233]. The nonspecific thalamic nuclei receive strong projections from the
midbrain reticular formation, hypothalamus, and the spinothalamic tract as well as from other sensory pathways. Some stimuli
(e.g., auditory, pain) excite this alerting system more easily than others (e.g., visual). These nuclei project back to the
midbrain and to the specific thalamic nuclei. Lesions that involve these structures bilaterally cause impairment of purposeful
alertness [145 , 324], whereas thalamic lesions causing coma extend into the midbrain [121].
2. The medial (dorsomedial [DM]) and anterior thalamic nuclear groups, including the laterodorsal and lateral posterior
nuclei, play an important role in memory, motivation, and emotions [154]. They are connected with the hypothalamus, the
“limbic lobe” (cingulate gyrus, medial temporal region, insula), and the frontal lobe. The DM nucleus mediates olfaction,
emotions, the secondary affect of pain, the sleep-wake cycle, and executive functions [21 , 78 , 96 , 238 , 254 , 263 , 264 , 269 , 289 , 291 , 322
]. Lesions of the anterior nucleus are more consistently associated with memory and executive function loss [52 , 90 , 101 , 112].
3. The VL and basal nuclear groups are concerned with the processing of sensory information and relaying it to the cortex,
and with sensorimotor control [169].
a. Relaying sensory information to the cortex is effected mainly by three nuclear groups, as follows:
i. The ventroposterior nuclear group, where taste and somatosensory information is elaborated and projected to
the somatosensory cortex of the parietal lobe. Within the thalamus, lateral inhibition increases sharpness in spatial
localization. Information from receptors in the head reaches

i. The large VL nucleus has an oral (VL anterior or nucleus ventralis oralis), an intermediate (VL posterior or

the cerebellum and projects to the precentral, primary motor cortex (area 4) [218]. The caudal portion receives
information from musculoskeletal system mechanoreceptors through the lemniscal system; it contributes to the
coordination of finer, distal motor movements with the proximal axial movements that support them.


<!-- PDF page 709 -->

ii. The ventral anterior nucleus may play a role in voluntary attention [201 , 246]. It has strong connections with the
substantia nigra, pallidum, medial thalamic nuclei, and frontal cortex.
iii. The intralaminar nuclei, centromedian and parafascicular, mediate purposeful behavior in response to sensory
stimuli [255]. They receive input from sensory pathways and project mostly to the striatum, but also to globus
pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, and cortex.
The VL and ventral anterior regions of the thalamus constitute the keystone for two large sensorimotor control loops:
(1) the cerebellorubro-thalamo-cortico-pontocerebellar loop and (2) the cortico-striato-pallido-thalamo-cortical loop.
Although their physiologic role is only partially understood [94], it is known that lesions in each loop cause different
syndromes. Obviously, their proximity in the thalamus allows ample possibilities for lesions to affect both loops.
Symptoms and signs derived from cerebellar lesions are discussed in Chapter 16. Those derived from basal ganglia
lesions are discussed in Chapter 19. Motor findings that point to the thalamus as the site of the lesion are given
preferential attention here.
4. The fourth main region of the thalamus comprises the dorsolateral and posterior nuclear groups, particularly the largest
thalamic nucleus, the pulvinar, which comprises 30% of the thalamus. The pulvinar receives input from the superior colliculus
and the visual streams, as well as from the medial geniculate body and the uncrossed dentatorubrothalamic tract, and
modulates occipito-temporo-parietal cortex [17 , 19 , 24 , 232]. Unlike simpler relay nuclei, like the geniculate bodies, which
receive sensory inputs and project to layer 4 of primary visual or auditory cortex, the pulvinar is an association nucleus that
receives input from layer 5 of primary visual or auditory cortex and projects to layer 4 of association cortex. Using both
retinotopic [8] and object-based frames of reference [313], the pulvinar participates in the identification of the behavioral
salience of stimuli, likely by filtering out irrelevant stimuli [191 , 281]. As such, it facilitates visual attention and the cortical
attention needed for language-related sensory tasks in the left hemisphere and visuospatial tasks in the right [59 , 128], as well
as other visually relevant tasks, as head direction while walking [270]. The pulvinar is much better developed in humans than
in lower mammals. During ontogenesis, it is the last thalamic region to reach adult morphology.

Localization of Ischemic Thalamic Lesions
In localizing ischemic lesions of the thalamus, several points should be kept in mind:
1. The arterial supply for most of the thalamus arises from the vertebrobasilar system, in some cases with a small contribution
from the posterior communicating artery [45]. Only the lateral portion and the hilium of the lateral geniculate body are usually
fed by the anterior choroidal artery, a branch of the internal carotid artery.
2. Except for the lateral geniculate body, the middle cerebral and anterior choroidal arteries do not supply the thalamus to such
an extent that thalamic infarction would result from occlusion of these vessels [226]. Some internal capsular dysfunction,
however, may result from occlusion of thalamic vessels [199]. In rare cases, the anterior cerebral artery may contribute to the
supply of the posterolateral thalamus through a posterior pericallosal vessel, which is anastomotic with the posterolateral
choroidal artery.
3. The paramedian thalamic vessels often arise from a single pedicle, often called artery of Percheron, which originates in one
of the basilar communicating arteries (see Fig. 18-2). Thus, unilateral posterior cerebral artery occlusions may result in
bilateral paramedian thalamic infarcts [ 45] (Video 18-1).
4. The individual pattern in vessel distribution and size will ultimately dictate the presence of one of the syndromes described
earlier.

Clinical Manifestations of Lesions in the Thalamus
The following considerations facilitate the understanding of the clinical manifestations of thalamic lesions:
1. Due to the smallness of the thalamus, several of the nuclei and even several of the functional regions outlined earlier are
usually affected simultaneously, even by discrete lesions such as infarcts. Because arteriolar vascular territories cross the
nuclear boundaries, as a rule, ischemic disease affects several nuclei, often partially [45]. In addition, many lesions are not
restricted to the thalamus, but involve neighboring areas of the brain as well. Paramedian thalamic vascular lesions tend to
affect also the midbrain, with a resultant decrease in the level of alertness to the point of coma [ 45 , 121 , 324]. Thus, other motor
or sensory findings that would point to thalamic involvement cannot be elicited. Laterally located lesions may disrupt the
internal capsule, thereby causing motor and sensory deficits [104] that mask the deficits characteristically present with
thalamic involvement. For instance, “thalamic neglect,” the lack of use, despite intact volitional strength, of a limb
contralateral to a lesioned VL nucleus is not manifest if capsular involvement has resulted in a hemiplegia. Lesions extending
inferiorly may yield hemiballismus, for which the subthalamic lesion is probably primarily responsible. Lesions in the
territory of the lateral posterior choroidal artery may cause memory loss through involvement of the parahippocampal gyrus [
210].
2. Except for sensory deficits, unilateral thalamic lesions result in transient deficits. By contrast, bilateral lesions or unilateral
lesions, such as hemorrhages or tumors, that press against the contralateral thalamus or impinge on the midbrain may render
the patient comatose or akinetic and mute.
3. Timing has a particular impact on the clinical expression of thalamic lesions. As the effects of an acute lesion recede,
neglect may disappear, inability to walk may yield to mild ataxia, and hemisensory loss diminishes. Other findings, however,
particularly the so-called positive symptoms (tremor, pain), usually become more pronounced within a few weeks after the
injury.
Although neuroimaging techniques and particularly MRI are extremely helpful to localize and categorize thalamic lesions (see, for
instance, [2 , 140 , 194]) imaging findings related to the thalamus fall rather under the scope of imaging publications (for instance, [180
]).
Disturbances of Alertness
In propofol anesthesia, thalamocortical connectivity is decreased [172]. Sudden bilateral paramedian thalamic lesions, such as
infarcts, may cause a decreased level of alertness ranging from somnolence to coma [45 , 99 , 324], generally transient. Prolonged coma
may result if the lesion extends to the midbrain tegmentum [121]. These patients often have oculomotor paresis [45]. By contrast,
patients with pure thalamic involvement have very small reactive pupils (“diencephalic pupil”), and their extraocular movements,
elicited by the doll's head maneuver, are full [234]. Akinetic mutism, discussed in Chapter 23, may follow bilateral paramedian
thalamic strokes [45 , 324], but more often extensive traumatic [167] or anoxic thalamic lesions, as in the noted Karen Quinlan case [
145].
The intralaminar, reticular, and ventral anterior nuclei seem to play the greatest role in mediating normal alertness [ 110 , 280].
Electrical stimulation of this region induces arousal from sleep [318], whereas lesions here cause drowsiness [115]. Insomnia from
bilateral electrical stimulation of the VL nuclei [36] may also be related to intralaminar stimulation. In patients with paramedian
thalamic lesions and daytime hypersomnia, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep is normal, but wakefulness, sleep spindling, and
stages of deep sleep are all reduced, suggesting that the medial thalamus is the “final common pathway” for both maintenance of
wakefulness and promotion of non-REM sleep [ 21 , 96 , 268]. Other reported disturbances include inversion of the nycthemeral rhythm
and dissociation of sleep stages, detected by electroencephalography, between the two hemispheres: the hemisphere affected by a
thalamic tumor showed earlier onset of deeper sleep stages [132]. Normally, thalamic deactivation at sleep onset precedes that of the
cerebral cortex [170].
Fatal familial insomnia, a prion disease related to mutations in the PRNP gene on chromosome 20, is mostly confined to the anterior
and DM thalamic

Autonomic Disturbances
Kleine-Levin syndrome, which is characterized by episodes of somnolence, hyperphagia, impaired recent memory, and hypersexual
behavior, traditionally believed to be related to hypothalamic disease, may have a paramedian thalamic component [44 , 71 , 138].
Subjective and objective hyperhidrosis contralateral to an anterior thalamic infarction has been described [143]. Deep brain
stimulation (DBS) with electrodes in the centromedian-DM thalamic region caused a change in penile erection, facilitating it in a
patient and inhibiting it in another [288]. Erectile dysfunction has been associated with strokes involving the most caudal portion of
the right ventroposterolateral nucleus of the thalamus [321]. Patients with Parkinson disease and autonomic dysfunction have
decreased hypothalamic-striatal-thalamic connectivity [63].
Disturbances of Mood and Affect
Disturbances of mood and affect are considered next because they are mediated by thalamic regions closely linked to the
hypothalamus and limbic lobe. These thalamic regions, however, interact strongly with the frontal lobe, and their damage often
impairs not only mood and affect but executive function as well. The thalamus is an important hub for the integration of emotional
stimuli. For instance, acute psychosocial stress alters thalamic network centrality and other connectivity [89 , 242]. Social touch,
which facilitates the feeling of friendliness, increases endogenous μ-opioid system activity in the thalamus [ 213]. Not surprisingly,
these mechanisms mature during ontogenesis. For instance, testosterone during puberty shifts emotional control from pulvinar to
anterior prefrontal cortex [297].
Apathy, disinterest, and a lack of drive have been reported with lesions of the paramedian region of the thalamus [29 , 45 , 99 , 139],
which is involved in reward learning [ 249]. Less often, such lesions may cause agitation, dysphoria, or an acute confusional state [29
, 93] and even undue joviality, accompanied by confabulation [45]. Similar manifestations of bilateral medial thalamic damage may
be interpreted as a partial Klüver-Bucy syndrome, described in a patient with chronic amnesia, distractibility, hyperorality, affective
dyscontrol, and a socially inappropriate behavior [206]. A manic-like state with disinhibition affecting speech (with logorrhea,
delirium, joking, laughing, inappropriate comments, and confabulation) has been described with right thalamic lesions [29 , 150]. A
patient mentioned having lost the pleasure involved in reading after a left anterior thalamic infarct [55]; in this case, the ipsilateral
cingulate gyrus was hypometabolic. Apathy is associated with thalamic volume loss in Huntington chorea [12]. In schizophrenia, a
disorder with altered affect and executive function, neuronal loss has been found in the DM and anterior nuclei of the thalamus by
some [ 235 , 323], but not by others [73].
Memory Disturbances
The thalamus plays an important role in memory formation and consolidation [283 , 316]. Memory for recent events may be
transiently or permanently impaired by lesions of the anterior or medial thalamic nuclear region [45 , 62 , 100 , 101 , 123 , 196 , 306] (

The proposed anatomic basis for a permanent amnestic syndrome after bilateral anterior thalamic infarctions is combined damage to
hippocampal-thalamic pathways via the mammillothalamic tract and medial temporal-thalamic pathways via the inferior thalamic
pedicle [62 , 101 , 111 , 171 , 301 , 308]. These pathways

### [Page 511 begins | PDF 716]

are closely adjacent in the anterior thalamus, and bilateral
lesions that cause amnesia are found in this region, whereas bilateral medial thalamic lesions that do not cause amnesia are located
more posteriorly [101 , 171 , 193 , 308]. Korsakoff amnesia correlates with neuronal loss in the anterior thalamic, but not DM, nuclei [112
]. Pure amnesia has also been described after a unilateral left polar thalamic infarct affecting the anterior thalamic nuclei and
adjacent mammillothalamic tract [55]. Lesions involving the left thalamus affect mainly verbal memory [ 90 , 204], whereas those in
the nondominant paramedian thalamic region impair memory related to visuospatial tasks (nonverbal memory) [29 , 230 , 276 , 278 , 315].
Thalamic amnesia as normally tested in the clinic is characterized by deficits in episodic memory, having to do with the recollection
of events or new information, while procedural memory is preserved [101]. Patients who are alert and active usually perform
adequately in tests of immediate memory, such as digit span. Characteristically, the amnesia is most profound for events taking
place after the injury (anterograde amnesia or recent memory loss), although, sometimes, it includes information acquired from days
to years previously (retrograde amnesia) [90 , 134 , 187 , 325]. Disorientation to time is common. With thalamic lesions, the content of
recall is not as affected as the temporal order of the items stored in memory, be they verbal or nonverbal items. Thus, the patients
may retrieve facts, but in a disorganized manner and out of context [90 , 272]. Some patients are aware of their deficit [325], and
others are not [306]. Whether this awareness can be accounted for by differences in the site or extent of the lesion remains to be
determined.
Some unusual patterns of memory loss and recovery have been described with thalamic lesions. A patient with bilateral medial
thalamic lesions recognized by their voices relatives whom he had failed to identify visually (prosopagnosia) [187]. Another patient's
retrograde amnesia improved suddenly 1 year after a left anterior thalamic infarction, when he was exposed to an event that
triggered the recall of a flood of previously forgotten autobiographical detail [166]. The resemblance with Proust's recollection
triggered by the taste of aunt Leonie's “petite madeleines” has led to the term petite madeleines phenomenon for this remarkable
presentation [166].
Confabulation, or falsification of memory occurring in clear consciousness, is frequently present with thalamic amnesia [29 , 90 , 217 ,
258 , 259]. Patients may confabulate spontaneously or when asked to recall some facts [258 , 259]. Particularly those that confabulate
spontaneously seem to have an impaired ability to order in time facts retained in memory [90 , 259]. Part of the problem has to do
with identifying the relevance of items stored in memory to the current context. Items of memory inappropriate for the here and now
find their way into the patient's verbal output or are manifested by behavior that relates to past experiences, but not to what is now
appropriate.
Thalamic abnormalities present in many disorders characterized by memory impairment, including Korsakoff amnestic syndrome [
112 , 265], traumatic brain injury [ 168], some of the frontotemporal dementias [156], and Alzheimer [ 4] and Creutzfeldt-Jakob diseases
[14 , 248 , 284].
Impaired Time Perception
Perhaps underlying some forms of thalamic amnesia, but not necessarily associated with clinical episodic memory loss, is abnormal
estimation of time observed in patients with lesions in the anterior or medial thalamic nuclear groups [90 , 151 , 157]. This disorder,
which has been called thalamic chronotaraxis[ 151], has been described with lesions of the DM nucleus of the thalamus. This
nucleus projects to prefrontal lobe, a time-keeping structure [ 88]. Thus, it is attractive to postulate that DM thalamic lesions are
responsible for this problem [ 151 , 157]. However, the lesions described so far tend to affect the mammillothalamic tract as it courses
from the mammillary bodies to the anterior nucleus of the thalamus [151 , 157 , 179]. The cortical metabolic defect, recorded in one
case [157], was not in the prefrontal cortex, but in the cingulate gyrus, cortical projection territory of the anterior nucleus of the
thalamus [ 179]. Thus, it is possible that isolated impaired time perception is the manifestation of partial lesions, while amnesia
results from larger, bilateral lesions in the same thalamic structures.

Paresthesias and Pain
Clinically, small lesions in the ventroposterolateral nucleus of the thalamus may yield only contralateral paresthesias that lack
“objective” sensory loss when tested at the bedside [80]. Such paresthesias tend to occur on one side of the face, particularly around
the mouth, and in the distal portion of the limbs. Occasionally, this cheiro-oral or distal distribution of the paresthesias may suggest
a more distal lesion (e.g., radiculopathy) [155]. These areas of the body have the largest representation in the thalamic sensory
nuclei. When the trunk is also numb, the subjective feeling of numbness may stop abruptly in the midline, although, on objective
testing, the sensory loss often fades toward

### [Page 512 begins | PDF 717]

the midline [199]. Such a “thalamic midline split,” which is absent
with parietal lesions, has been thought to have some clinical value in identifying the site of the lesion [198]. The numb areas of the
body may feel swollen, enlarged, shortened, twisted, or torn, or they may tingle. Objects held with the limb contralateral to the
lesion may feel abnormally heavy. Finally, the patient may be unaware of a profound sensory loss.
The thalamus modulates pain perception [7 , 294]. Pain, referred to as thalamic pain, is perhaps the best known component of
Dejerine and Roussy thalamic syndrome, described earlier [185 , 320]. In the stroke literature, this syndrome is called the central
poststroke pain syndrome, because lesions outside the thalamus may be cause a similar syndrome. The unpleasant or excruciatingly
painful sensation on the side of the body contralateral to a thalamic lesion (an infarct is most common) may appear at the time of the
injury [80] or when the sensory loss begins to improve (
Videos 18-2 and 18-4). The pain feels localized to the skin. Cutaneous
stimuli trigger paroxysmal exacerbations of the pain, which persists after the stimulus has been removed. The latency between the
stimulus and pain perception is prolonged, suggesting that the pathways conveying it are polysynaptic. Because the perception of
epicritic pain, such as that induced with a pin-prick, is reduced in the painful areas, this symptom has been termed anesthesia
dolorosa, or painful anesthesia. A similar symptom may follow damage of the posterior root ganglia (herpes zoster) or trigeminal
nerve or nucleus (trigeminal neuralgia with anesthesia). Ventroposterior thalamic nuclear lesions, particularly those in the border
with the pulvinar, coinciding with the ventrocaudalis portae nucleus, are more likely to produce half-body pain than lesions
elsewhere in the sensory pathways [35 , 146 , 277 , 302]. These lesions are generally accompanied by hypesthesia (aka hypoesthesia) to
cold, but not to heat [141]. Metabolic studies have shown enhanced activation of somatosensory cortex with stimulation [141] or pain
relief correlating with increased metabolic rates in prefrontal and anterior insular cortices, hypothalamus, and periaqueductal gray [
152], all of the structures felt to play an important role in pain perception. Patients with trigeminal neuropathic pain, but not those
with trigeminal neuralgia or temporomandibular pain, had a reduction in thalamic volume and thalamic N-acetylaspartate-to-creatine
ratio [106]. Some patients with localized neuropathic pain can be relieved by stimulation of the basal ventroposteromedial region of
the thalamus or by lesioning the sensorimotor cortex [141].

Thalamic pain seldom occurs with tumors. It has been described most often with vascular lesions, some of which involve not only
the thalamus but also the deep parietal white matter [5]. Besides, delayed pain may follow cortical parietal infarcts, particularly
those in the bank of the Sylvian fissure, affecting the second somatosensory area (pseudothalamic syndrome) [25 , 257].
Loss of Sensory Modalities
All somatosensory modalities are processed in the ventroposterior nucleus of the thalamus contralateral to the side of the body


<!-- PDF page 718 -->

where they are perceived. Within the nucleus, there is a definite topographic distribution: the head is represented
anteroinferomedially, whereas the leg is represented posterosuperolaterally; the arm is represented in an intermediate position. A
larger volume of the nucleus is dedicated to the mouth, tongue, and distal portion of the extremities; their thalamic representation is
almost completely crossed. The large oral thalamic and cortical representation in humans may well be related to language functions
[49]. The face, proximal portion of the limbs, and trunk are represented in a smaller volume of thalamic tissue, mainly contralateral
but partially ipsilateral [37]. Thalamic sensory loss tends to occur maximally in the distal portion of the limbs and often spares the
face [144]. Such sparing may be related to the bilateral representation of the upper face or to the different vascular supply of the
medial ventroposterior region (paramedian territory) because discrete infarction of this region can cause isolated numbness of the
lower face [245].
In regard to the thalamic topography of various sensory modalities, physiologic experimental studies have shown that cells
concerned with deep pressure and movements of the limbs are preferentially located in the rostral and caudal ends of the
ventroposterolateral nucleus. The central part of the nucleus contains neurons that respond to cutaneous stimuli. In humans,
however, lesions large enough to produce any sensory loss most often involve several modalities. No existent clinicopathologic
studies allow precise identification of thalamic areas for touch versus joint position or vibration. Pain sensation has been obtained by
stimulation of the basal part of the nucleus [37].
Because the perception of pin-prick, temperature, touch, or vibration is altered more often after thalamic than after cortical lesions,
these sensory modalities have been termed primary or thalamic. By contrast, conscious joint position identification, two-point
discrimination, stereognosis, and graphesthesia tend to be more impaired after cortical parietal lesions and are thus termed
secondary or cortical sensory modalities. Nevertheless, parietal lesions often cause some impairment of thalamic modalities and
vice versa. Occasionally, a lesion in the thalamus may disturb mainly the so-called cortical sensory modalities [84 , 312].
Anesthesia and impaired temperature perception tend to occur with basal lesions near the medial geniculate body [ 35 , 37]. Because
vibration sense remains

### [Page 513 begins | PDF 718]

unaltered after surgical removal of the parietal cortex, it has been assumed that
hemispheric lesions causing loss of vibratory sense necessarily implicate the thalamus or the thalamocortical projections [250].
Decreased thalamic perfusion has been observed with functional, nonorganic hemisensory loss [309]. This abnormality reverted
when the patients improved.
Disturbances of vision, hearing, and vestibular function are discussed in Chapters 7 and 11. Visual field defects caused by thalamic
lesions frequently involve the superior quadrant bilaterally. An intolerance to light (“central dazzle”) has been ascribed to a thalamic
lesion [61 , 69]. Paramedian thalamic infarction may cause the sudden onset of vivid, formed visual hallucinations (suggesting
peduncular hallucinosis) associated with agitation and sleep disturbance (MRI revealed no abnormality of the midbrain or cerebral
peduncles) [77]. Vivid visual hallucinations, suggesting peduncular hallucinosis, with left hemiparesis and left paresthesias have
been described with a right posterior thalamic infarct [267]. Auditory and visual experiential hallucinations may occur with unilateral
thalamic lesions affecting the intralaminar and DM nuclei [ 175 , 212]. Auditory illusions of hyperacusis and palinacousis may occur
with a lesion in the medial geniculate body [ 86]. Certain sounds produced an intense somatosensory tingling sensation (auditory-
tactile synesthesia) in a patient with a lesion centered in the VL nucleus [23], whereas emotional synesthesia, a strong emotional
response to certain auditory stimuli, has been described with a posterolateral thalamic lesion [262]. Unilateral visual sensory neglect
may occur with lesions of the right pulvinar [136 , 299]. Vestibular dysfunction may contribute to thalamic postural syndromes
described in the section “Motor Disturbances” [15].
Normal detection, but altered identification, of taste (dysgeusia) has been associated with thalamic lesions (
Video 18-5); in
several patients, odors and taste were perceived either in a neutral way, their pleasant character having disappeared, or as unpleasant
[251 , 266]. DM and ventroposteromedial thalamic nuclei gate the intensity of taste sensation [ 305]. Taste sensations have been elicited
in humans by stimulation of a portion of the ventroposteromedial nucleus [ 161]. A decrease in taste perception, hypogeusia, is a
common side effect of therapeutic thalamic DBS [252].

Motor Disturbances
Just as the sensory disturbances described in the previous section are often related to lesions in the ventroposterior nucleus of the
thalamus, motor disturbances are related to lesions of the VL nucleus and the adjacent subthalamic region.
Postural Disturbances
Following an acute thalamic lesion, even a unilateral lesion, patients may be transiently unable to stand or even sit, despite normal
strength of the limbs when tested against resistance (thalamic astasia) [181 , 182 , 303]. The lesions, including infarction, hemorrhage,
or tumor, primarily involved the superoposterolateral thalamus and spared the rubral region. Although alert, with normal or near-
normal strength and a variable degree of sensory loss, patients with thalamic astasia cannot stand and often cannot even sit up
unassisted (Video 1-23). They fall backward or toward the side contralateral to the lesion and have a deficit of overlearned motor
activity of an axial and postural nature [182]. Sudden falling to one side while sitting, standing, or walking has also been described
with basal ganglia lesions contralateral to the side of the fall [153]. Some patients with posterolateral thalamic lesions push actively
to the side contralateral to the lesion (pusher syndrome) [137]. The postural impairment caused by thalamic lesions may be
accompanied by vestibular dysfunction and a disturbance in the patient's perception of the vertical axis [15 , 64 , 65 , 135]. However, the
impaired perception of the vertical does not explain the entire syndrome.
Postural abnormalities in patients with lesions in the VL nucleus or its connections with the medial frontal region, in the
suprathalamic white matter, go beyond simple disequilibrium [ 181 , 182]. Volitional movements, such as trying to overcome the
strength of the examiner during isometric testing, are normal (Video 1-23). Yet, the patient does not use the same strong limbs, and
particularly the axial muscles, in tasks that are normally performed automatically or without much thinking, such as shifting in bed.
Proximal movements that normally support distal ones, such as abduction of the shoulder when trying to pick up a cup, are
restricted, even though the patient is perfectly able to abduct his or her shoulder on command. This syndrome occurs with lesions of
either thalamus and is different from the sensory hemineglect described predominantly with nondominant thalamic lesions [136 , 182].
It is best compared to an impairment of the automatic pilot that underlies much motor activity. Consciously, we decide to walk, but
the complex motor synergies involved in walking proceed without our attending to them [186]. The remarkable syndrome caused by
VL thalamic lesions has been called motor neglect[ 173] or unilateral akinesia, but the patients do not neglect the volitional use of
the contralateral limbs. However, in movements that include a significant “automatic pilot” component, such as shifting in bed, the
failure to use the limbs contralateral to the lesion may convey

### [Page 514 begins | PDF 719]

to the examiner the false impression that the
patient is hemiplegic [18 , 29 , 31 , 225 , 314 , 315] (Video 1-23). Certainly large infarcts, lacunes [198], hematomas [312], and tumors may
involve the neighboring internal capsule, causing a more or less profound hemiplegia. However, purely thalamic involvement does
not result in hemiparesis.
Other Motor Manifestations
Lesions in the VL nucleus of the thalamus cause contralateral hypotonia, reduction of emotional expression, and transient neglect [
115 , 173]. This syndrome may occur even with lesions that are discrete enough to spare all sensory modalities and the early (thalamic)
components of the somatosensory evoked response. Such lesions, which are circumscribed to the VL nucleus, spare the
ventroposterior nucleus. Because the late components of the somatosensory evoked response are abolished, it has been postulated
that the VL nucleus plays a key role in the activation of the frontal cortex. Unilateral thalamic lesions cause akinesia, either as a
result of sensory inattention or as a consequence of impaired activation of axial, automatic synergies, as described earlier [31 , 181 , 303
, 315].
Lesions of the thalamus may cause emotional facial paresis (i.e., weakness of emotionally evoked facial movements, such as
smiling, with normal volitional activation) [122]. Contralateral emotional facial paresis has been described with lesions of the
thalamus and subthalamus, anterolateral thalamus and insula, posterior thalamus and operculum, and posterior thalamus [30 , 31 , 100 ,
122].

Damage to the dentatorubrothalamic projection to the VL nucleus by a lesion rostral to the decussation of the superior cerebellar
peduncle or damage to the VL nucleus itself results in hemiataxia (coarse ataxia, with action tremor, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia,
and rebound) of the contralateral limbs [176]. These structures are near the corticospinal pathways and the ventroposterior nucleus of
the thalamus, explaining why hemiataxia is often associated with some degree of hemiparesis or hypesthesia in this type of infarct [
190]. Isolated hemiataxia and ipsilateral sensory loss (the hemiataxia-hypesthesia syndrome) may be a manifestation of thalamic
infarction in the thalamogeniculate territory, causing damage to the ventroposterior nucleus and VL nucleus [ 189]. The cerebellar
syndrome is not as severe as with involvement of the superior cerebellar peduncle or dentate nucleus. Regarding hand movements,
pinching may be involved in both cases, but reaching tends to be spared with thalamic lesions [22]. Some weeks after the injury
(which is most often ischemic), tremor at a rate of between 3 and 5 cycles per second (Holmes tremor) may appear in the affected
extremities [130 , 197 , 240]. It is mainly distal and increases greatly during the performance of any movement. This tremor may be
improved by dopaminergic medication, DBS, or by a surgical lesion of the VL nucleus of the thalamus [240 , 261]. If the central
tegmental tract is also involved, tremor with a similar rate may affect the eyelids, eyes, or palate (palatal tremor). Central tegmental
tract lesions are in the territory of the thalamopeduncular paramedian vessels and are often related to occlusion of the top of the
basilar artery [ 45]. Contralateral cerebellar ataxia and proprioceptive sensory loss may occur with lesions of the ventroposterior
thalamus, likely due to interruption of cerebellar outflow pathways in the thalamus rather than to sensory deafferentation [107].
Dysfunction of thalamocerebellar connectivity may underlie orthostatic tremor[ 260] (Video 1-29). Essential tremor is related to
dysfunction of the inferoposterior portion of the VL nucleus [223]. DBS of this region, or the nearby posterior subthalamic area, is
effective in reducing essential tremor [ 16 , 116]. Although the term stimulation is used for this procedure, it is likely that the electrical
fields generated by the electrodes may cause a functional lesion: unilateral thalamotomy of the same region, most conveniently
performed with focused ultrasound, has similar effects to DBS [34 , 109]. Severe essential tremor can be accompanied by gait ataxia,
which, like the tremor, but independent of it, improves with stimulation in the VL nucleus [ 76]. It is likely that the thalamus
mediates the increase in essential tremor amplitude when the patient's eyes are open [9]. Tremor in Parkinson disease is controlled
by dopamine inhibiting the cerebellar thalamus [66 , 67] and by DBS or thalamotomy in areas similar to those targeted in essential
tremor [307].
Lesions that are slightly more rostral, involving the subthalamic region and the pallidothalamic projections to the VL nucleus, may
cause transient contralateral hemiballismus[ 150]. After some days or weeks, the amplitude of the movement decreases and either
disappears or adopts a choreic or athetotic pattern. Paroxysmal episodes of chorea in the neck, arms, and trunk associated with
drowsiness, triggered by fever or hot weather, and bilateral thalamic MRI signal change may occur in GABA transaminase
deficiency [202].
Dystonia may be secondary to a necrotizing lesion or degeneration, as with the familial dystonias. Messenger RNA for torsinA, a
protein encoded by the gene abnormal in early-onset torsion dystonia, is abundantly present in the thalamus [10]. Thalamic infarcts
in the intermediate and caudal portions of the VL nucleus may cause myoclonic dystonia in the

### [Page 515 begins | PDF 720]

contralateral
limbs [91 , 149 , 158 , 163]. The myoclonic nature of the deficit, often accompanied by action tremor or chorea, differs from the dystonia
with tonic spasms more characteristic of striatopallidal lesions [149 , 158] (Video 1-21). The onset of the dystonic movements often
lags by months or years after the acute insult [91 , 92]. Dystonic tremor with chronic MRI evidence of infarction in the anterior
nucleus of the thalamus may had resulted from a more posteroventral lesion causing atrophy but no cavitary lesions in the involved
nuclei [53]. More subtle thalamic changes occur in dystonia. The sensory fields of thalamic neurons are enlarged [160 , 162]. Dystonia
is associated with changes in thalamic activity and connectivity [85]; further, thalamic activity may be normalized by botulinum
toxin injection in the dystonic muscles [38], highlighting the feed forward component of this disorder. Patients with secondary
dystonia or hemiballismus of basal ganglionic or thalamic origin tend to improve with lesions or stimulation of the VL nucleus of
the thalamus [43 , 207 , 222]. Focal hand dystonia has improved after ventrooral thalamotomy [124].
Abnormal posturing of the hand, often termed thalamic hand, may appear 2 weeks or more after the occurrence of a vascular lesion
of the same region. The hand assumes a posture with flexion at the wrist and metacarpophalangeal joints, whereas the
interphalangeal joints are hyperextended. Flexion of the metacarpophalangeal joints increases from the second digit, which may
actually be extended, to the fifth digit, which is markedly flexed. The fingers may be forcibly abducted. The thumb is either
abducted or pushed against the palm [177].
Other abnormal movements described with thalamic lesions include action myoclonus[ 11], ideomotor apraxia[ 209], and asterixis [ 39
, 68 , 182 , 279]. Asterixis is more common with VL nucleus lesions [286]. Hyperekplexia, the sudden loss of postural tone caused by
startling stimuli, may be exacerbated with thalamic lesions [74]. Imitation synkineses, also called mirror movements, are common
after thalamic lesions [178]. In such cases, the distal portion of the limb contralateral to the thalamic lesion tends to imitate the
movement performed by the healthy side. When the patient forcibly makes a fist with the sound hand, the fingers of the other hand
curl up into the palm, and the patient ends by making a fist with both hands. Loss of position sense or loss of cortical activation by
the thalamus may underlie these abnormal movements, which can be lessen, like the choreic movements described earlier, by having
the patient concentrate his attention on avoiding them.
Discrete lesions in various regions of the thalamus [ 70], or DBS through implanted electrodes, are used for the treatment of

parkinsonian, dystonic and essential tremor [75 , 220 , 222 , 307], dystonia [147], pain [ 57], epilepsy [253], and the manifestations of
Gilles de la Tourette syndrome [3 , 127 , 236 , 288]. Tremor treatment is the most extensively used and best understood DBS thalamic
procedure.
A decreased corneal reflex may be present in patients with hemiparesis and hemisensory loss due to a cerebral hemispheric lesion [
79]. Loss of parietal excitatory influence on the lower brainstem seems to be responsible for this finding [215]. Pure thalamic lesions,
even those that cause a marked hemisensory loss, do not depress the corneal reflex [216]. However, a patient with an acute
thalamocapsular lesion had bilaterally depressed late components of the blink reflex when the side contralateral to the lesion was
stimulated [51]. It is possible that damage of corticopontine fibers traveling in the internal capsule may be responsible for the deficit.
Disturbances of Ocular Motility
Lesions restricted to the thalamus cause only subtle changes in ocular motility [219]. Visual information from the superior colliculus,
relayed by the pulvinar to the parietal lobe (“second visual system”), contributes to the detection and localization of visual events in
space and the production of saccadic eye movements that allow the “first” (geniculostriate) visual system to identify such events.
Lesions in the pulvinar have been said to cause (1) a decrease in the critical flicker frequency and neglect of visual objects in the
periphery of the contralateral visual field, (2) prolonged latency of visually evoked saccadic eye movements, and (3) a paucity of
spontaneous eye movements directed toward the contralateral hemifield [326].
Much more striking, and more obvious at the bedside, are eye movement abnormalities that occur when a lesion involves the
midbrain and thalamus [47]; this often happens with paramedian thalamopeduncular infarcts [45]. In such cases, impairment of
ocular motor function results in abnormal pupils, ptosis, and restriction of vertical eye movements and of adduction. Selective
upward gaze or downward gaze or combined dysfunction may occur [31], as may blepharospasm [237], bilateral internuclear
ophthalmoplegia with ptosis [31], and a pseudo-sixth nerve palsy [40 , 183] (see Chapter 8). Thalamic lesions may be associated with
vertical gaze palsies, not due to thalamic injury per se but due to extension of the lesions into the upper midbrain [273]. A “vertical
one-and-a-half syndrome” (vertical palsy in one eye, upward palsy in the other eye) may occur [30 , 31 , 285]. Bilateral medial thalamic
lesions may cause purely vertical saccadic apraxia, affecting

### [Page 516 begins | PDF 721]

volitional vertical saccades, but not saccades
elicited by external stimuli [ 196]. Acute thalamic esotropia, with impaired upward gaze, has been described with infarction of the
contralateral posterior thalamus in the basilar communicating artery territory [95]. Tonic activation of the medial rectus in this case
could result from damage to direct inhibitory projections from the thalamus or impairment of inputs to midbrain neurons involved
with vergence control [95].
Large thalamic hemorrhages may impinge on the midbrain or impair its function by causing raised intracranial pressure [93]. The
eyes then become tonically deviated down and slightly adducted, as if peering at the tip of the nose [312]. In some instances, the eyes
may be tonically deviated to the side of the hemiparesis, opposite to a thalamic hemorrhage (“wrong-way eyes”) [82 , 290]. This
finding has also been reported in extrathalamic supratentorial lesions [231 , 290].
DBS for the treatment of epilepsy, with electrodes placed at the mesodiencephalic junction, just inferior to the centromedian nucleus
of the thalamus, may cause nystagmus with constant velocity slow phases, beating to the right when the left thalamus is stimulated
and vice versa [287].
Depression of the reticular activating system (most often metabolic in nature) or involvement of both thalami results in small pupils
(1 mm in diameter) that react well to light (diencephalic pupils) [234]. Anisocoria is occasionally present, with the smaller pupil
ipsilateral to the thalamic lesion.
Disturbances of Complex Sensorimotor Functions
The thalamus modulates the association cortex involved in the processing of language and other “higher” cortical functions.
Although unilateral thalamic lesions do not impair these functions as much as large cortical lesions, the pattern of impairment has
some localizing value. Mention has already been made of the contralateral motor neglect caused by lesions in the VL nucleus of the
thalamus and of the contralateral visual inattention that results from lesions in the pulvinar.
Patients with right thalamic lesions may have constructional apraxia and display marked neglect of the left hemifield [314 , 315]. This
hemineglect may be associated with anosognosia and asomatognosia, thus mimicking a parietal lobe lesion [29 , 314], and is likely
due to damage to the intralaminar and VL or ventral anterior nuclei on the right [ 54 , 315]. Right thalamic lesions can also cause
impairment in the identification of emotional facial expressions with preserved discrimination of facial identity (prosopoaffective
agnosia) [310]. Alexia related to impaired visuospatial perception has been described after right thalamooccipital infarction [118].
Optic ataxia or visuomotor apraxia has been described in a patient with computed tomography (CT) evidence of bilateral lesions in
the pulvinar, the one on the left in the acute stage when the patient was studied [56].


<!-- PDF page 722 -->

Dominant hemisphere thalamic lesions may cause a transient language disturbance (thalamic aphasia) [31 , 98 , 126 , 296], characterized
by (1) reduced spontaneous speech with paraphasic errors and perseveration, (2) varying degrees of auditory comprehension
impairment, (3) preserved repetition and reading, (4) defective spontaneous writing and writing to dictation but normal copying, (5)
word-production anomia but spared word selection and word symbolism, and (6) distractibility. This deficit, which would be
classified as a mixed transcortical aphasia, tends to improve in a few weeks [6 , 42 , 126]. The language deficit may have more
semantic components when the lesion is posterior [241] and more motor components when the lesion affects the anterior nucleus [ 90
]. Electrical stimulation of the VL thalamus produces an acceleration of speaking. The patient feels urged to speak faster [115].
Stimulation also enhances later recall of objects presented to the patient. The left thalamus is involved in attention mechanisms that
gate storage and retrieval of both long-term and short-term verbal memory [ 128]. Derangement of a specific cortical attention
mechanism because of the thalamic lesion results in a lack of drive to speak and perseveration of apparently unrelated verbal
material [243]. Some patients have hypophonia and dysarthria [90]. Such language impairment resembles the language impairment
that results from left medial frontal lesions [33 , 184]. Other DM thalamic lesions cause a bizarre language pattern, with some
characteristics of dysfunction in the prefrontal cortex. It contains intrusions (segments of speech out of context) and other evidence
of temporal gating impairment, such as giving biographical information while working on a calculation test [ 50 , 90]. Apraxic
agraphia has been described with an MRI, showing a DM thalamic lesion [ 214]. In developmental dyslexia, structural connectivity
between the left auditory thalamus and the planum temporale is reduced [ 293]. Focused ultrasound thalamotomy in Parkinson
disease may impair Stroop color naming and verbal fluency. Verbal perseveration has been elicited by electrical stimulation of the
anterior thalamic radiations [ 174].
The language impairment noted with left thalamic lesions and the visual neglect noted with right thalamic lesions are associated
with decreased activity of ipsilateral frontal or temporoparietal association cortex on positron emission tomography (PET) or single-
photon emission computed tomography

(SPECT) [18 , 195 , 214 , 225]. Deafferentation of an otherwise intact
cortex (diaschisis) due to the thalamic lesion may explain these “cortical” syndromes with thalamic lesions. For example, thalamic
aphasia may be due to decreased cortical activation secondary to the subcortical lesion [18 , 195 , 225]. Some authors ascribe to
thalamic lesions or thalamocortical disconnection the cases of striatocapsular aphasia reported in the literature [208].
A stuttering-like repetitive speech disorder may occur with infarction in the paramedian thalami and midbrain [1]. Although the
speech disorder seems like stuttering, the compulsive repetitions, constant rate, and monotonous tone seen with these infarctions are
not associated with ordinary stuttering. The repetitive speech disorder in patients with infarcts in the supplementary motor area has
similar clinical features [1].
Disturbances of Executive Function
The ability to harness sensory inputs, social needs, memories, ethical principles, and vegetative drives to choose an appropriate
course of action may be called executive function, for which the thalamus is key [32 , 221 , 244 , 322]. Thalamic lesions are associated
with executive function impairment in ischemic disease [27 , 48] and traumatic brain injury [ 168].
Paramedian thalamic infarction may cause a lack of initiative, bradyphrenia, akinesia or apathy [46], and a “frontal lobe” utilization
behavior (see Chapter 20), suggesting a thalamofrontal component to environmental interactions that require inhibition, self-
monitoring, and cognitive flexibility [72 , 90 , 114]. Treated with bromocriptine, one such patient overcame her akinesia, but became
hypersexual [46]. The patients' language may appear bizarre because of the presence of intrusions (segments of speech out of
context) and the super-imposition of mental activities normally processed sequentially, for instance, giving biographical information
while working on a calculation test [50 , 90]. Failure of goal-directed regulation of behavior has been observed with a lesion affecting
the right anteroventral thalamic region [ 211]. A left-sided discrete infarction of the medial thalamus may cause severe impairment of
complex executive behaviors, probably due to dysfunction of thalamofrontal linkages that help modulate complex human behavior [
254]. Repetitive movements (clonic perseveration) may be a manifestation of executive function disorder with thalamic lesions [87].
Infarction of the DM nucleus, intralaminar nuclei, and medial part of the VL nuclei is often associated with marked hypoperfusion
of the overlying frontal region cortex on PET or SPECT [50 , 114]. It is thought that the “frontal lobe-like” syndrome with thalamic
lesions may be related to impaired activation of the frontal lobe by the damaged DM and anterior thalamic nuclei [29 , 301], or their
projections, for instance, with traumatic brain injury [ 164]. These nuclei provide an important pathway for information from
vegetative centers to reach the frontal lobe.
Topographic Localization of Thalamic Lesions
Following the detailed and referenced account of the localization of thalamic symptoms and signs, a brief synopsis follows
organized by the anatomic regions of the thalamus.
Anterior Thalamic Region


<!-- PDF page 723 -->

Discrete lesions may be silent or cause language disturbances when they affect the dominant hemisphere. They may also cause
inattention, which results more often when the right hemisphere is involved. Bilateral lesions may cause akinesia, amnesia, and
attentional disturbances. Lesions extending to the subthalamic area may cause athetosis, chorea, or postural abnormalities (thalamic
hand).
Medial Thalamic Region
Lesions in this location may pass unnoticed when they are small and unilateral. Large or bilateral lesions cause impairment of recent
memory, executive function impairment, apathy or agitation, attention derangements, and somnolence or coma. Lesions that extend
to the midbrain-diencephalic junction may cause contralateral tremor and vertical gaze palsy, affecting particularly downward gaze.
VL Thalamic Region
Sensory loss, paroxysmal pains, and hemiataxia in the contralateral side of the body are the most striking sequelae of lesions in the
posterior portion of this region. More anterior lesions cause postural abnormalities, such as disequilibrium and restriction of axial
supportive movements or delayed tremor. Hemineglect and language disturbances may appear transiently.
Posterior Region
Basal lesions in this region may cause hemianesthesia, “thalamic pain,” and visual field defects. Dorsal lesions give rise to
attentional disorders of the ipsilateral hemisphere, resulting in transient aphasia when the dominant hemisphere is involved. Some
patients may have myoclonic dystonia.
