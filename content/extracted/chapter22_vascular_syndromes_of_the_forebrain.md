Arterial Blood Supply
Three major vessels originate from the aortic arch: the brachiocephalic trunk (innominate artery), the left common carotid artery,
and the left subclavian artery. The brachiocephalic trunk branches to form the right subclavian artery and the right common carotid
artery. The paired common carotid arteries travel upward on each side of the trachea to the level of the thyroid cartilage, where each
common carotid artery bifurcates into the external and internal carotid arteries (ICAs) (Fig. 22-1). The paired vertebral arteries
(VAs) originate from the subclavian arteries medial to the anterior scalene muscles and join at the medullo-pontine junction to form
the basilar artery. After its origin (V1 or first segment) from the subclavian artery to the transverse foramen of C6, the VA travels
through the transverse foramina of C6-C2 (V2 or second segment), loops around the arch of C1 (V3 or third segment), pierces the
dura passing through the foramen magnum to become intracranial (V4 or fourth segment) and join the fellow VA to constitute the
basilar artery. The basilar artery begins at or slightly below the pontomedullary junction and stretches the length of the pons,
tapering to its termination at the pontine-midbrain junction, where it bifurcates into its two terminal branches, the right and left
posterior cerebral arteries (PCAs), at the level of the interpeduncular cistern. The basilar artery, divided into three segments
(proximal, middle, and distal), gives off perforating arteries along its course [38 , 113 , 378 , 410].
By convention, the ICA territories are referred to as the anterior circulation, whereas the VAs and basilar artery (vertebrobasilar)
and their branches are termed the posterior circulation. Both ICAs supply four-fifths of the brain by volume, whereas the
vertebrobasilar circulation accounts for one-fifth of the total. After reaching the ventral surface of the brain, the paired carotid
arteries and the paired VAs supply the brain according to three distinct vascular patterns: median and paramedian arteries, short
circumferential arteries, and long circumferential arteries. Three main arteries supply the cerebrum: anterior cerebral artery
(ACA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), and PCA. Likewise, three main arteries supply the cerebellum: the posterior inferior
cerebellar artery (PICA); the anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA); and the superior cerebellar artery (SCA). Also, three
groups of perforators supply the striatum and thalamus: medial striate arteries from the proximal ACA; lateral striate arteries from
the MCA stem; and posterior striate arteries from the PCAs. The brainstem, labyrinth, cochlea, cerebellum, subthalamus, and parts
of the thalamus, temporal, and occipital lobes receive their supply from the vertebrobasilar circulation. The blood supply to the
brainstem is often divided into four distinct territories: anteromedial, anterolateral, lateral, and posterior. The carotid and
vertebrobasilar circulations join at the base of the brain to form the anastomotic circle of Willis [101 , 102].
The Internal Carotid Artery
The ICAs supply the rostral aspect of the brain and include the cerebral cortex, deep white matter, basal ganglia, and diencephalon.
The ICAs may be divided into four segments: cervical segment (C1); petrous segment (C2); cavernous segment (C3); and
supraclinoid or intracranial segment (C4). According to another classification, proposed by Borthillier, the ICAs are divided into
seven segments: C1 or cervical; C2 or petrous; C3 or lacerum; C4 or cavernous; C5 or clinoid; C6 or ophthalmic; and C7 or
communicating (terminal) [103]. The cervical segment (C1) has no branches. It ascends vertically in the neck, extending from the
common carotid artery bifurcation to the skull base. The petrous segment (C2) travels within the carotid canal to end when it enters
the cavernous sinus, in proximity to the oculomotor, trochlear, and abducens nerves (CNs III, IV, and VI). A caroticotympanic
branch to the tympanic membrane and a vidian branch (artery to the pterygoid canal) originate from this segment. The cavernous
ICA (C3) segment travels within the cavernous sinus and gives off three named arteries: (1) the meningohypophyseal trunk, which
also has three branches (tentorial artery of Bernasconi and Cassinari, inferior hypophyseal artery, and dorsal meningeal artery); (2)
the artery of the inferior cavernous sinus; and (3) the capsular artery. Finally, the ICA pierces the dura mater medial to the anterior
clinoid process of the sphenoid bone to become the supraclinoid or intracranial segment (C4).

projects anteriorly into the back of the orbit and travels through the optic canal alongside the optic nerve. Along its course, the

After the OA origin, the ICA gives off the posterior communicating artery (PComA) and the anterior choroidal artery (AChA). The
PComAs join the PCAs to form the posterolateral segment of the circle of Willis, providing a link between the anterior and posterior
circulations and between both cerebral hemispheres. Penetrating branches from the PComA supply the anterior and posterior
hypothalamus, the optic tracts and posterior segments of the optic chiasm, and the anterior and ventral thalamic nuclei.
The AChAs originate from the posterior wall of the ICAs distal to the PComAs and proximal to the ICAs terminus. The AChA is
divided into an intra-cisternal and intraventricular segment. The AChAs course posteriorly and laterally to reach the optic tract. The
AChAs supply the choroid plexus of the temporal horn, hippocampus, dentate gyri, amygdaloid nucleus, piriform cortex, uncus of
the temporal lobe, lateral geniculate body, optic tract, origin of the optic radiations, genu and inferior and medial segments of the
posterior limb of the internal capsule, globus pallidus, tail of the caudate nucleus, the middle one-third of the cerebral peduncle, and
the substantia nigra. The AChAs end in the lateral geniculate body, where they anastomose with lateral posterior choroidal artery
branches of the PCAs. Finally, the ICA branches into the ACA and MCA.
Anterior Cerebral Artery
The ACA originates below the anterior perforated substance. It courses anteromedially to join the fellow ACA by way of the
anterior communicating artery (AComA), closing the rostral segment of the circle of Willis. The ACA may be divided into five
segments: proximal (A1), ascending (A2 and A3), and horizontal segments (A4 and A5). The ACAs supply the medial surface of
the cerebrum and the upper regions of the frontal and parietal lobes [41]. The ACA gives off (a) medial lenticulostriate branches,
(b) pericallosal branches, and (c) hemispheric branches. The medial lenticulostriate branches include basal branches that supply the
dorsal aspect of the optic chiasm and the hypothalamus and the recurrent artery of Heubner that supplies the anteroinferior limb of

the internal capsule, anterior aspect of the putamen and caudate nucleus, and the tip of the globus pallidus externa. Callosal branches
of the ACA arise from the pericallosal artery, defined as the ACA segment distal to the AComA. The ACA and the pericallosal
arteries supply the septum pellucidum and fornix. Hemispheric ACA branches supply the medial surface of the hemispheres and
include: orbitofrontal, frontopolar, internal frontal (anterior, middle, and posterior), paracentral, and internal parietal (superior
and inferior) branches.
Middle Cerebral Artery
The MCA originates below the anterior perforated substance, lateral to the optic chiasm. The MCA supplies most of the lateral
surface of the cerebral hemisphere and deep frontal and parietal lobe structures [33]. The MCA may be divided into four segments:
M1 or sphenoidal, M2 or insular, M3 or opercular, and M4 or cortical. Penetrating lenticulostriate arteries supplying the adjacent
corona radiata, external capsule, claustrum, putamen, part of the globus pallidus, body of the caudate nucleus, and superior aspect of
the anterior and posterior limbs of the internal capsule originate from the M1 segment. Other M1 segment branches are the
orbitofrontal and anterior temporal arteries.
The MCA stem division falls into one of three anatomic patterns: (a) bifurcation (78%), (b) trifurcation (12%), or (c) ramification
into multiple trunks (10%). Shortly after the origin of the anterior temporal artery, the MCA trunk most often bifurcates; one branch
gives off the anterior or proximal group of arteries, and the other gives off the posterior or distal group. The anterior group includes
the orbitofrontal, precentral, central, and anterior parietal arteries; the posterior group includes the posterior parietal, posterior
temporal, and the angular or terminal arteries.

Posterior Cerebral Artery
The PCAs arise from the rostral basilar artery within the interpeduncular cistern. Embryologically, the PCA belongs to the carotid
circulation. Approximately one in five individuals have a fetal PCA origin from the ICA. The PCAs supply the occipital lobes,
inferior and medial surface of the temporal lobes, parts of the midbrain and thalamus, choroid plexus, and walls of the lateral and
third ventricles. Four named PCA segments are identified and labeled P1 to P4: the precommunical segment, also known as the
“mesencephalic artery” (P1); the ambient segment (P2); the quadrigeminal segment (P3); and the calcarine segment (P4). PCA
branches are further divided into three groups: (a) penetrating arteries to the brainstem and thalamus, (b) dorsal callosal (splenial)
artery, and (c) cortical branches [ 152]. Numerous perforating branches originate from the proximal PCA. Mesencephalic branches
include the interpeduncular perforators and short and long circumferential arteries. The thalamic blood supply originates from the
PComAs and the perimesencephalic segment of the PCAs. The dorsal callosal artery anastomoses with distal ACA branches. The
four main PCA cortical branches include the anterior temporal, the posterior temporal, the parieto-occipital, and the calcarine
branches. The primary visual cortex (calcarine cortex, striate cortex, or V1) is supplied mainly by the calcarine artery, but it can also
receive its supply from the parieto-occipital and posterior temporal arteries.

Collateral Circulation
Main sources of brain collateral circulation include (a) the circle of Willis, connecting the carotid and vertebrobasilar arterial
systems, (b) anastomoses between extracranial and intracranial arteries, and (c) leptomeningeal anastomoses between terminal
branches of the main cerebral and cerebellar arteries. The most important intracranial anastomoses are those of the circle of Willis.
Variations in its configuration are common with a complete circle of Willis, present only in approximately 25% of individuals [351].
Variant anatomic communications between the anterior and posterior circulation include the primitive carotid-vertebrobasilar
anastomoses such as (a) the persistent trigeminal artery, (b) the persistent acoustic (otic) artery, (c) the persistent hypoglossal
artery, and (d) the persistent proatlantal artery. A persistent trigeminal artery [ 121] is by far the most common of these carotid-
vertebrobasilar anastomoses [121 , 267].

Syndromes of the Cerebral Arteries
Strokes result from either ischemia or hemorrhage. Approximately 85% to 87% of strokes are ischemic, 10% result from
intracerebral hemorrhages (ICHs), and the rest result from aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhages (aSAHs) [281].
Ischemic strokes may involve the cerebral cortex, subcortical areas, brainstem, cerebellum, or spinal cord and may be caused by
atherothromboembolism, cardiogenic embolism, small vessel ischemic disease, nonatherosclerotic vasculopathies, thrombophilic
states, decreased global perfusion, or a combination thereof. Intracranial hemorrhages may be epidural, subdural, subarachnoid,
intraparenchymal, or intraventricular and result from various causes, including trauma, hypertensive cerebrovascular disease,
ruptured saccular brain aneurysms, ruptured brain arteriovenous malformations (AVMs), blood dyscrasias, systemic vasculitis,
sympathomimetic drug use, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), or primary or metastatic neoplasms. Hypertensive vasculopathy is
most often associated with nonlobar ICHs, whereas lobar hematomas, particularly in the elderly, are often caused by CAA.
Cerebral atherothromboembolism predominantly involves the MCA territory, followed by the PCA territory. The arterial territories
of the ACA and the basilar artery are less frequently involved. Common sources of cardioembolism include nonvalvular atrial
fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction, dilated or other type of cardiomyopathies, sick sinus syndrome, rheumatic valvular heart
disease, prosthetic heart valves, congenital heart disease, infective and nonbacterial thrombotic (marantic) endocarditis, and cardiac
tumors.
Transient Ischemic Attacks
Transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) are characterized by short-lived episodes of acute, focal, nonconvulsive neurologic dysfunction
due to reversible retinal or brain ischemia. Symptom onset is sudden and often unprovoked, reaching maximum severity almost
immediately. Symptoms typically last less than 1 hour, often no longer than 2 to 30 minutes. In general, patients do not have
residual manifestations by the time they receive medical attention [ 372]. However, as TIAs are often associated with variable rates of
restriction on diffusion-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (DW-MRI) [25], a “tissue-based definition” is currently commonly
used: TIAs are brief episodes of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal retinal or brain ischemia with symptoms typically lasting <
60 minutes, and without evidence of acute infarction[ 4].
Carotid circulation TIAs need to be distinguished from those TIAs affecting the vertebrobasilar circulation. TIAs often result from
thromboembolism

The Carotid Artery Syndrome
Amaurosis (“to darken or obscure”) fugax (“fleeting”), or transient monocular blindness, is the hallmark clinical feature that helps
distinguish the ICA syndrome from the MCA syndrome. Amaurosis fugax results from retinal, choroidal, or optic nerve
hypoperfusion and is characterized by sudden, temporary, and painless monocular visual loss [167]. Patients often describe a
temporary visual blackout, dimming, blurring, graying, fogging, or an altitudinal visual loss [359]. Scintillations are seldom reported.
Most attacks of amaurosis fugax occur spontaneously and are unrelated to postural changes. Duration of visual loss is approximately
1 to 5 minutes; rarely, these episodes last 20 to 30 minutes.

Atherothrombotic carotid artery disease has a predilection for involvement of the common carotid artery bifurcation and the
proximal ICA. Carotid artery atherosclerotic steno-occlusive disease may result in recurrent TIAs, an apoplectic or stepwise stroke
onset, or a slowly progressive ischemic neurologic deficit. Cervical ICA occlusion may be asymptomatic in the presence of adequate
collateral circulation, particularly if the arterial occlusive process develops slowly. Different patterns of infarction result from ICA
occlusion [312]. An ipsilateral hemispheric infarction may result when the collateral circulation is inadequate. Occlusion of the
carotid artery terminus (carotid T) often results in large infarctions involving the arterial territories of the MCA and ACA, including
involvement of deep structures supplied by the lenticulostriate arteries. In cases of fetal origin of the PCAs, the ischemic damage
may extend to involve the PCA territory. Decreased level of alertness and severe leg weakness are useful clinical clues to
differentiate a carotid T occlusion from a stem of the MCA stroke.
MCA strokes may involve the entire territory of the MCA (total) or be restricted to areas closest to the ICA or MCA (proximal), the
border zone areas between the ACA and MCA (watershed), or only the white matter supplied by peripheral branches of the MCA
(terminal). Contralateral hemiplegia, hemianesthesia, homonymous hemianopia, and aphasia (if the dominant hemisphere is
compromised), or apractagnosia (if the nondominant hemisphere is involved) may ensue. The association of amaurosis fugax or
ischemic optic neuropathy with contralateral hemiplegia (optico-cerebral syndrome) is seldom observed [62]. Acute ICA occlusion
rarely results in concurrent transient ophthalmoparesis with permanent monocular blindness [398]. There may be an ipsilateral
Horner syndrome due to involvement of oculosympathetic fibers along the ICA. Ipsilateral optic atrophy is seldom present.
Ischemic oculopathy, also known as ocular ischemic syndrome, is another manifestation of carotid artery steno-occlusive disease [
405]. This condition may result in ocular or orbital pain often relieved in the supine position, impaired vision, and “bright-light”
amaurosis. There may be engorgement of conjunctival and episcleral vessels, corneal edema, ischemic pseudo-inflammatory uveitis,
rubeosis iridis, and anterior chamber cells and flare [270]. The intraocular pressure may be low in early stages or abnormally high in
late stages. Funduscopy demonstrates asymmetric hypertensive retinal changes. A corneal arcus senilis may be less prominent on
the hypoperfused side [350]. High-grade carotid artery stenosis or occlusion may result in venous stasis (hypotensive) retinopathy,
characterized by decreased or absent venous pulsations, dilated and tortuous retinal veins, midperipheral retinal microaneurysms,
blossom-shaped hemorrhages in the midperipheral retina, and retinal nerve fiber layer splinter hemorrhages. Hypotensive
retinopathy may also result in retinal arteriolar narrowing, macular edema, and posterior pole neovascularization.

Patients with carotid artery steno-occlusive disease may present with proportionate hemiparesis (equal involvement of face,
shoulder, hand, hip, and foot) or with predominant faciobrachial weakness. Small cortical infarctions may cause weakness confined
to a few digits, particularly the index finger [201 , 206], or the finger extensors in cases of cortical infarctions involving the
contralateral middle-lower precentral gyrus [27 , 75 , 408]. Small cortical infarctions may also result in pure sensory stroke [401],
isolated dysarthria [210], asterixis [198], or upper-limb monoataxia [291].
The neurovascular examination may demonstrate a well-localized bruit in the mid- or upper cervical region. Cervical bruits have
many causes and do not always indicate underlying carotid artery steno-occlusive disease. Radiated cardiac murmurs, hyperdynamic
states, nonatherosclerotic carotid arterial disorders such as fibromuscular dysplasia, cervical artery dissections, radiation
vasculopathy, and also venous hums may result in cervical murmurs. Thus, the lack of a cervical bruit has little diagnostic value
because the bruit may disappear when the carotid artery stenosis is >90%; conversely, a cervical bruit may result from flow
augmentation due to a contralateral carotid artery occlusion.
Severe ICA steno-occlusive disease may also result in progressive or episodic weakness of the contralateral lower extremity, often
aggravated or triggered by standing or walking, as a result of hypoperfusion in the border zone areas between the ACA and the
MCA [403]. Episodic carotid territory ischemia may on rare occasions result in repetitive, brief, coarse, irregular or rhythmic,
wavering or trembling involuntary movements, affecting the contralateral arm and hand or arm, hand, and leg, mimicking “focal
motor seizures” [ 29 , 402]. These movements, precipitated by standing, walking, or neck hyperextension, are usually promptly
relieved in the supine or sitting positions. Other unusual manifestations of carotid artery territory ischemia include transient
anosognosia, or transient loss of pitch perception [120].

Infarctions involving the genu of the internal capsule result in contralateral facial and lingual paresis with dysarthria [58]. A cluster
of TIAs (capsular warning syndrome) involving the face, arm, and leg without associated cortical symptoms, due to ischemia of a
single lenticulostriate artery territory, may occur hours to days before a stroke [111]. The capsular genu syndrome has also been
associated with unilateral mastication-palatal-pharyngeal weakness, ipsilateral vocal cord paresis, and mild hand weakness. This
faciolingual syndrome suggests that most of the corticopontine and corticobulbar fibers to the facial and hypoglossal nuclei are
located in the capsular genu. The lack of sternocleidomastoid involvement or sensory changes suggests that corticofugal fibers to
CN XI nucleus and thalamocortical fibers corresponding to buccofacial sensation do not travel in the capsular genu. The
inconsistent presence of mastication, pharyngeal, palatal, and laryngeal weakness suggests bilateral (predominantly unilateral),
corticofugal projections to the motor nuclei of CN V and CN IX and the nucleus ambiguus or control of these functions by
extracapsular fibers [58]. Faciolingual hemiparesis, whether associated with masseter, palatal, pharyngeal, laryngeal, or hand
weakness, is highly suggestive of a genu of the internal capsule stroke. Pure dysarthria, accompanied by contralateral facial
weakness, may occur with striatocapsular infarctions, infarctions of the superior aspect of the anterior limb of the internal capsule or
adjacent corona radiata, superior aspect of the genu or adjacent corona radiata, or vertebrobasilar territory infarction [110 , 160 , 169].
Capsular genu strokes may result in behavioral changes; damage to the adjacent anterior nucleus of the thalamus, may account for
these findings [364]. Acutely, patients may have fluctuating levels of alertness, inattention, memory loss, apathy, abulia, and
psychomotor retardation. There may be mild contralateral hemiparesis and dysarthria. Bilateral capsular genu lesions may also result
in trismus [19].
With left-sided infarctions, there may be severe verbal memory loss, occasionally associated with dementia. Right-sided infarctions
cause transient visuospatial memory impairment. A plausible explanation is that capsular genu infarctions result in functional
deactivation of the ipsilateral frontal cortex, resulting in a thalamocortical disconnection [58 , 364].
The AChA Syndrome
As described by Foix and colleagues in 1925, infarctions in the AChA territory result in hemiparesis, hemianesthesia, and
homonymous hemianopia [129]. Damage to corticospinal fibers in the posterior limb of the internal capsule accounts for the
hemiparesis; a variable hemisensory loss for light touch and pinprick sensation results from involvement of the superior thalamic
radiations; and the homonymous hemianopia sparing the horizontal meridian or quadruple sectoranopias result from involvement of
the optic tract, lateral geniculate body, or optic radiations [ 68 , 100 , 166]. A relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) may be present.
Syndromes associated with AChA infarction include the pure motor hemiparesis (PMH) syndrome, the sensorimotor syndrome, and
the ataxic hemiparesis (AH) syndrome [ 166]. Homonymous visual field defects involving the upper and lower visual fields with
sparing of the horizontal meridian result from lateral geniculate

ACA Syndrome
Isolated ACA territory infarctions account for less than 3% of all ischemic strokes [13 , 57 , 134 , 161 , 185 , 192 , 220 , 229 , 335]. ACA strokes
may damage the medial aspect of the frontal and parietal lobes, cingulate gyrus, basal aspects of the frontal lobes, rostrum, genu,
trunk, and splenium of the corpus callosum, rostral diencephalon, head of the caudate nucleus, and anterior limb of the internal
capsule.
Manifestations of ACA infarctions vary according to the topography of the arterial occlusion and the patency of the collateral
circulation [57 , 94 , 323 , 334]. Involvement of hemispheric ACA branches often results in contralateral weakness mainly involving the
leg. There may be lack of initiative or abulia. Paratonia (gegenhalten) and a grasp reflex may be present. Bilateral damage to
mesiofrontal structures results in frontal lobe release signs (sucking, rooting reflex), akinetic mutism, paraparesis, urinary
incontinence, and amnesia with apathy [57 , 271]. Paraparesis due to ACA infarctions has often been associated with hypoplasia or
absence of the A1 segment. Involvement of the rostral corpus callosum may cause left arm apraxia (anterior disconnection
syndrome) or hyperlexia [361]. There may be contralateral tactile sensory loss primarily involving the leg. Impaired articulation and
a soft whispering voice may occur. Left ACA infarctions may result in transcortical motor aphasia. There may be memory and
emotional disturbances, impaired planning, gait apraxia, and postural impairment. Lesions of the medial frontal micturition center
(especially if bilateral) may result in urinary incontinence [400].
Damage to the dominant medial frontal region, including the supplementary motor cortex, may result in impaired bimanual
coordination, the alien-hand sign, and features of intermanual conflict [262]. Other manifestations include forced gaze deviation,

ACA territory infarctions account for only one-fourth of all strokes associated with a pattern of leg-predominant weakness. More
often, this clinical pattern results from either corona radiata or internal capsule involvement in the territories of the AChA or
perforators or even in the brainstem. Furthermore, a leg-predominant pattern of weakness may also result from MCA territory
ischemic strokes or thalamic hemorrhages [337]. Strokes restricted to the precentral gyrus aspect of the paracentral lobule result in
contralateral predominantly distal leg weakness. If, in addition, there is involvement of the premotor cortex and supplementary
motor area, there is distal leg and proximal arm weakness. Strokes involving the medial aspect of the premotor cortex and
supplementary motor area, although sparing the precentral gyrus, result in a pattern of contralateral crural predominant hemiparesis.
Internal capsule or brainstem strokes result in proportional leg weakness, suggesting a somatotopic organization of the corticospinal
tracts, with fibers destined to the leg situated dorsolaterally and arm fibers situated ventromedially [337].
Superficial ACA territory infarctions involving the paracentral region may also cause homolateral ataxia and crural paresis [55].
Medial lenticulostriate artery (artery of Heubner) territory infarctions result in contralateral face and arm weakness without
associated sensory loss. Thus, proximal ACA infarctions cause a contralateral hemiplegia, with face, tongue, and arm paralysis as a
result of involvement of the anterior limb of the internal capsule, as well as leg paralysis due to paracentral lobule involvement.
Infarction of basal ACA branches results in transient memory disorders, anxiety, and agitation. Occlusion of the pericallosal
branches results in apraxia, agraphia, and tactile anomia of the left hand. Distal ACA occlusion may cause infarctions of the caudate
nucleus, at times extending to the anterior limb of the internal capsule and anterior putamen, resulting in transient contralateral
hemiparesis, dysarthria, behavioral and cognitive deficits, and language impairment [ 74].
ACA infarctions may rarely cause movement disorders. Small anterior frontal lobe infarctions may result in asterixis.
Hemiparkinsonism may follow strokes involving the supplementary motor area or cingulate gyrus. ACA infarctions may also result
in micrographia [200 , 220]. The observation of involuntary masturbation due to a callosal alien-hand syndrome, and right-sided
hemiballism, further highlight the intriguing semiology of ACA territory ischemic strokes [36].

MCA Syndrome
MCA territory infarctions are among the most common manifestations of cerebrovascular disease. The clinical picture is variable
and depends on whether the topography of the arterial occlusion involves the MCA stem, the superior MCA division, the inferior
MCA division, or rather the lenticulostriate MCA branches, as well as the status of collateral circulation. MCA territory infarctions
may be categorized as superficial or deep [154 , 268 , 321 , 390].
Contralateral faciobrachial weakness and, to a lesser extent, leg weakness is a common manifestation of MCA strokes. Contralateral
sensory impairment involving the face, arm, and, to a lesser extent, the leg is also frequent. Perioral and distal upper-limb sensory
dysfunction (cheiro-oral syndrome) may result from postcentral gyrus involvement [53]. A cheiro-oral syndrome may also follow
ischemia of the corona radiata [294], thalamus [190], or brainstem [ 10 , 150 , 258 , 295]. AH with cheiro-oral syndrome may also occur
with posterior capsular infarction [93].
MCA territory infarctions may cause paresis and apraxia of conjugate gaze to the contralateral side and transient tonic deviation of
the eyes and head toward the side of the stroke. Left hemispheric infarctions may result in Broca, Wernicke, conduction, or global
aphasia. Alexia with agraphia results from involvement of the left angular gyrus. Variable combinations of finger agnosia,
dyscalculia, right-left disorientation, and agraphia (Gerstmann syndrome) may be present. Nondominant hemispheric infarctions
may result in inattention, neglect, apractic syndromes, lack of emotion or empathy to others, and impaired prosody. Rarely,
nondominant hemispheric strokes cause an acute confusional state, acute agitated delirium, delusions, or hallucinations [ 276].
Nondominant temporoparietal infarctions may also result in visual agnosia, prosopagnosia, spatial disorientation, and inability to
comprehend nonverbal communication or to recognize familiar sounds [181]. Infarctions of either hemisphere may result in
contralateral homonymous hemianopia or inferior homonymous quadrantanopia. Cataleptic posturing is an unusual manifestation of
MCA infarctions [ 332].
Occlusion of the superior division of the MCA results in contralateral hemiparesis with predominant involvement of the upper
extremity and face, hemisensory loss, and conjugate gaze deviation. The visual fields tend to be preserved. Strokes involving the
superior division of the dominant MCA account for nonfluent aphasia. A confusional state, aprosodia, contralateral hemi-
inattention, and anosognosia are prevailing features of superior division right MCA strokes. Left frontal cortical infarctions have
been associated with transcortical sensory aphasia [196 , 341], or AH [ 128]. Parietal lobe infarctions may result in the posterior variant
of the alien-hand syndrome[ 194 , 217]. Cortical strokes affecting the omega- or epsilon-shaped precentral “hand knob” area may
cause a predominant deficit of the thumb-index pinch (flexor pollicis longus and flexor digitorum profundus of the second finger),


<!-- PDF page 930 -->

mimicking an anterior interosseous neuropathy [5 , 145 , 250 , 309].
Wernicke aphasia results from strokes involving the inferior division of the left MCA. Inferior MCA division infarctions of either
hemisphere result in contralateral homonymous hemianopia or quadrantanopia. Right inferior MCA division infarctions may also
cause left visual neglect. Temporal lobe involvement can cause an agitated and confused state.
Central MCA branches supply the insula: the anterior aspect of the insula by branches of the superior MCA trunk and the posterior
aspect of the insula from branches of the inferior MCA trunk. Strokes restricted to the insular cortex may cause somatosensory
deficits; gustatory disorders; vestibular manifestations; cardiovascular disorders, including arterial hypertension and arrhythmias;
language and neuropsychological disorders, including aphasia, dysarthria, and somatoparaphrenia [76 , 123 , 383]. Insular strokes have
been associated with increased risk of myocardial injury, cardiac arrhythmias, and sudden death [24 , 82].
Occlusion of the lateral striate branches of the MCA result in striatocapsular infarctions with involvement of the rostral aspect of the
head of the caudate nucleus, anterior limb of the internal capsule and putamen [110]. Clinical manifestations include hemiparesis,
affecting mainly the upper limb, and “cortical” abnormalities (aphasia, neglect, and dyspraxia). Less frequently, a PMH with
minimal cortical signs may be present. Isolated dysarthria or upper-limb clumsiness may seldom occur. Striatocapsular infarctions
often result from cardioembolism or occlusive atherosclerotic cerebrovascular disease [110].
The centrum semiovale contains the core of the hemispheric white matter and is supplied by the superficial (pial) MCAs through
perforating medullary branches. Limited centrum semiovale infarctions without associated lenticulostriate territory involvement are
often associated with acute neurologic-neuropsychological impairment not different from those observed with large MCA territory
infarctions [59].
Double (multiple) MCA territory infarctions involving the dominant hemisphere may result in global aphasia without hemiparesis [
375], hemianopic hemiplegia without sensory impairment [52], or conduction aphasia with hemiparesis [52]. Bilateral supranuclear
facio-pharyngo-glosso-masticatory paresis with automatic-voluntary dissociation (Foix-Chavany-Marie

syndrome) results from bilateral anterior opercular infarctions [330]. These patients have anarthria, severe dysarthria, and dysphagia

About 10% to 15% of all ischemic brain infarctions may behave as a space-occupying edematous lesion. Malignant MCA infarction
results from space-occupying subtotal (>50% of the MCA territory) or complete MCA territorial infarction. Malignant MCA

Vertebrobasilar Artery Syndromes of the Brainstem and Cerebellum
The main arterial territories of the brainstem and cerebellum have been elegantly delineated by Tatu and colleagues [365]. The main
arterial trunks supplying the brainstem include the VA, anterior spinal artery, PICA, basilar artery, AICA, SCA, PCA, PComA, and
AChA. The cerebellar arterial supply on its lower half originates from the PICAs and the AICAs, whereas the superior aspect of the
cerebellum is supplied by the SCAs.
Connected to the brainstem by three pairs of cerebellar peduncles, the main symptoms of cerebellar infarction include vertigo,
dizziness, nausea, vomiting, gait unsteadiness, limb clumsiness, headache, dysarthria, diplopia, and impaired alertness. Most
prominent signs include limb and gait ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus, and altered mental status [ 46].
The cerebellar areas supplied by the PICAs are variable. The PICAs supply the inferior vermis and the inferior and posterior aspects
of the cerebellar hemispheres. Several patterns of PICA territory infarctions are recognized. If the medial branch of PICA is
compromised, involving the vermis and vestibulocerebellum, frequent manifestations include severe vertigo, ataxia, and nystagmus.
If the lateral cerebellar hemisphere is involved, patients have nausea, vomiting, vertigo, dysarthria, limb dysmetria, conjugate or
dysconjugate gaze palsies, miosis, and gait ataxia. With large edematous cerebellar infarctions, lethargy, acute obstructive
hydrocephalus, and posterior fossa herniations develop. With a cerebellar pressure cone (tonsillar herniation), there is downward
displacement of the tonsils through the foramen magnum, resulting in hemorrhagic necrosis of the cerebellar tonsils and grooving of
the ventral surface of the medulla oblongata. Clinical manifestations include neck stiffness, cardiac and respiratory disturbances, and
apnea. With ascending transtentorial cerebellar herniation (upward herniation syndrome), there is an upward shift of the superior

The AICAs supply the anterior surface of the simple, superior, and inferior semilunar lobules, flocculus, middle cerebellar peduncle,
and caudal pontine tegmentum. AICA territory infarctions result in the ventral cerebellar syndrome. Typical manifestations of AICA

and thermoanesthesia with corneal hypesthesia due to involvement of the trigeminal spinal nucleus and tract. Ipsilateral deafness
and facial paralysis result from involvement of the lateral pontomedullary tegmentum. An ipsilateral Horner syndrome reflects
compromise of the descending oculosympathetic fibers. Contralateral trunk and extremity hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia result
from involvement of the lateral spinothalamic tract. Ipsilateral ataxia and asynergia are due to involvement of the cerebellar
peduncle and cerebellum. AICA infarctions may be forerunners of an impending basilar artery occlusion [7].

PComAs and the perimesencephalic segments of the PCA. Thalamic infarctions characteristically involve one of four vascular
territories (Fig. 22-4): posterolateral, anterior, paramedian, or dorsal[ 61 , 273 , 336 , 342]. Occlusion of thalamogeniculate branches
arising from the PCA (P2 segment) causes a pure sensory stroke, a sensorimotor stroke, or the thalamic pain syndrome (Dejerine-
Roussy syndrome). Damage to the ventroposterolateral thalamus causes contralateral hemibody sensory loss to all modalities, with
occasional facial sparing as a result of the more medial location of the nucleus ventralis posteromedialis. Patients present with
severe dysesthesias, “burning,” “shooting,” or “stabbing” pains, allodynia, and hyperalgesia. There may be vasomotor disturbances,
transient contralateral hemiparesis, and choreoathetoid or ballistic movements. Onset of symptoms is variable, ranging from weeks
to months to years after stroke, most frequently within the first 6 months.
Anterior thalamic infarctions result from occlusion of the polar or tuberothalamic artery. Main clinical manifestations consist of
fluctuating levels of alertness, abulia, apathy, disorientation, lack of insight, personality changes, contralateral emotional-facial
paresis, occasional hemiparesis, and visual field deficits. Left-sided infarctions may result in aphasia, dysprosody, dysarthria, or
selective semantic memory impairment [340]. Right-sided infarctions may cause hemineglect, alien-hand syndrome, or visual spatial
deficits [254 , 296]. Paramedian thalamic infarctions resulting from occlusion of the paramedian or thalamic and subthalamic arteries
(thalamoperforating pedicle) may be unilateral or bilateral. Main clinical manifestations include hypersomnolence, transient loss of
consciousness, memory loss, dementia, akinetic mutism, lexical semantic deficits, vertical gaze abnormalities, bilateral eyelid
tremors upon voluntary eye closure, hypersexuality, and body core temperature abnormalities [47 , 107 , 183 , 224 , 274 , 282].
The artery of Percheron, a single dominant arterial trunk arising from one of the proximal segments of the PCA, supplies the
paramedian thalami and the rostral midbrain. Occlusion of the artery of Percheron results in bilateral paramedian thalamic
infarctions with or without midbrain involvement [224 , 257 , 306 , 324 , 367 , 371].
Dorsal thalamic infarctions resulting from occlusion of the posterior choroidal arteries cause homonymous quadrantanopia or
homonymous horizontal sectoranopias and hemibody (face and arm) sensory loss. An asymmetric optokinetic response may be
present. Aphasia may result from left pulvinar lesions.
Postthalamic stroke movement disorders may be acute or delayed and primarily include dystonia, chorea, tremor, vascular
parkinsonism, asterixis, athetosis, myoclonus, and ballism [139 , 147 , 182 , 198 , 199 , 241 , 265 , 298].

Border Zone Ischemia
During or following cardiac surgery or episodes of severe arterial hypotension, ischemia may involve the border zone or watershed
areas between two main arterial territories. Border zone ischemia, resulting from the combination of hypoperfusion and embolism,
may result in several distinct syndromes [37 , 163 , 252]:
1. Ischemia in the border zone of all three major arterial systems (ACA, MCA, and PCA) may result in bilateral parieto-
occipital infarctions. Clinical manifestations include bilateral lower altitudinal visual field defects; difficulty in judging size,
distance, and movement; impaired smooth ocular pursuit; optic ataxia or cortical blindness.


### [Page 668 begins | PDF 936]


2. Bilateral border zone ischemia between the ACAs and MCAs results in bibrachial cortical sensorimotor impairment often
confined to the forearms and hands, and impaired volitional saccades.
3. Bilateral border zone ischemia between the MCAs and PCAs results in cortical blindness, alexia, dyscalculia, dysgraphia,
and memory impairment for verbal and nonverbal material.

Unilateral watershed infarcts may result from episodes of systemic arterial hypotension associated with preexisting steno-occlusive
cervicocephalic arterial disease, causing focal hypoperfusion in the most distal arterial territory [60]. A review of 51 patients with
symptomatic unilateral watershed infarcts [60] noted the following three subtypes:
1. Watershed infarctions in the border zone between the superficial MCAs and ACA territories (anterior watershed
infarctions) observed in 22 patients caused crural predominant hemiparesis with facial sparing, at times associated with
impaired sensation. Left-sided lesions resulted in transcortical motor aphasia preceded by mutism, or isolated word-finding
difficulties. Nondominant hemispheric lesions resulted in apathy or euphoria.
2. Watershed infarctions in the border zone between the superficial territories of the MCA and the PCA (posterior watershed
infarctions) were found in 20 patients. A noncongruent homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing and a cortical type of
hemihypesthesia (two-point discrimination, stereognosis) were common. Limb weakness was rare. Left-sided lesions resulted
in isolated anomia, transcortical sensory aphasia, and rarely Wernicke aphasia. Hemispatial neglect and anosognosia were
common with right-sided lesions.
3. Watershed infarctions involving the border zone between superficial and deep territories of the MCA (subcortical
watershed infarcts) occurred in nine patients. Hemiparesis was common, and approximately one-half of these patients had
hemisensory loss. Broca aphasia, global aphasia, or transcortical motor aphasia was common with dominant hemispheric
lesions. Hemineglect was noted with nondominant hemispheric infarctions.
Infarctions involving the boundary zones between the ACA, MCA, and PCA, ipsilateral to a severe ICA stenosis or occlusion may
result in reduced blood flow to the white matter of the angular gyrus and evolving hand motor deficits [368].
Other watershed areas are those in the cerebellum at the borders between PICA, AICA, SCA, and their branches [252].
Lacunar Infarctions
Ischemic strokes result from (a) large artery steno-occlusive atherosclerotic disease, (b) small vessel or penetrating artery disease
(lacunes), (c) cardio-aortic embolism, (d) nonatherosclerotic vasculopathies, (e) hypercoagulable disorders, and (f) undetermined
cause.
Lacuna refers to small necrotic/cystic lesions of the brain or brainstem often associated with chronic arterial hypertension. As such,
lacuna is a pathologic term. A lacunar syndrome is the clinical picture due to a lacuna or lacune. However, lacunar syndromes may
result from nonlacunar mechanisms of infarction, as well as subcortical and brainstem hemorrhages.
Lacunes are small ischemic infarctions in the deep regions of the brain or brainstem, less than 15 mm in size, resulting from
occlusion of a single penetrating artery (AChA, MCA, PCA, or basilar artery). Lacunar infarctions often result from lipohyalinosis
of small penetrating arteries or branches related to long-standing arterial hypertension. Chronic arterial hypertension induces
segmental arteriolar disorganization, hypertrophy of the media, and fibrinoid deposition on the vessel wall, eventually leading to
narrowing or occlusion. Another mechanism of lacunar infarction is microatheroma at the origin of the penetrating arteries from a
parent major cerebral artery. Less common mechanisms include embolism from arterial or cardiac sources or arteriolar or capillary
endothelial dysfunction. Besides the common sporadic form of small vessel disease related to arterial hypertension, a minority of
cerebral small vessel disease has a monogenic cause, most often resulting from cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with
subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) [31 , 125 , 269 , 272 , 292 , 310 , 318].
Most frequently, lacunar infarctions involve the putamen, basis pontis, thalamus, posterior limb of the internal capsule, and caudate
nucleus in that order. Lacunar infarctions may also involve the anterior limb of the internal capsule, subcortical cerebral white
matter, cerebellar white matter, and corpus callosum. Lacunar syndromes are the clinical manifestations of lacunar infarctions. At
least 20 lacunar syndromes have been described [83]. Atypical presentations are not uncommon [15]. The five classical lacunar
syndromes are as follows:
1. PMH or pure motor stroke results from an internal capsule, corona radiata, or basis pontis lacunar stroke. PMH is the most
common lacunar syndrome, between one-half to two-thirds of cases depending on the series. PMH is characterized by
hemiparesis or hemiplegia involving the face, arm, and leg. There may be mild dysarthria,

particularly
at stroke onset [289]. There should not be associated aphasia, apraxia, agnosia, sensory, visual, or higher cortical disturbances.
Preceding or crescendo TIAs (capsular warning syndrome) are frequently observed. Distinguishing capsular from pontine
PMH is clinically challenging. Dysarthria and a history of previous transient episodes of gait imbalance or vertigo favor a
pontine topography [ 289 , 332]. Other implicated topographies include the cerebral cortex, centrum semiovale, basal ganglia,
mesencephalon, and medullary pyramid [ 17 , 18 , 186]. A pure motor monoparesis seldom results from a lacunar infarction.


<!-- PDF page 938 -->

Although uncommon, PMH may also result from putaminal hemorrhages [176].
2. Pure sensory stroke (PSS), also known as pure paresthetic stroke or pure hemisensory stroke, is often the result of a lacunar
stroke involving the ventroposterolateral nucleus of the thalamus. PSS is characterized by numbness, paresthesias, and a
hemisensory deficit involving the face, arm, trunk, and leg. Sensory manifestations are primarily distal with a cheiro-oral,
cheiro-pedal, or cheiro-oral-pedal topographic pattern [126 , 203 , 211]. Seldom, the sensory manifestations are restricted to
proximal body segments [204 , 205 , 208]. Although thalamic lacunar strokes are the most frequent cause of PSS, nonthalamic
strokes involving the internal capsule/corona radiata, subthalamus, midbrain, parietal cortex, and paramedian dorsal pons have
been reported to produce a PSS [14 , 186 , 344 , 345 , 346]. Differentiation of a pontine PSS syndrome from a thalamic PSS
syndrome may be challenging. Brainstem PSS often shows a discrepancy between superficial and deep sensory modalities. In
cases of pontine PSS, vibration and position sense (medial lemniscal modalities) are often impaired on the paresthetic side,
whereas sensation to pinprick and temperature (spinothalamic modalities) is preserved. Conversely, in cases of PSS involving
the thalamus, internal capsule, or corona radiata, both spinothalamic and medial lemniscal modalities are compromised [344 ,
346]. Likewise, ipsilateral impairment of smooth pursuit and vestibulo-ocular reflex may result from a pontine topography of
the stroke [178]. Focal hemorrhages in the cerebral cortex, thalamus, midbrain, pons, an internal capsule may also result in
PSS syndromes [6 , 26 , 345].
3. In a report of 21 patients with PSS, 11 had thalamic strokes (pansensory or restricted sensory loss), 7 had lacunes or
hemorrhages in the lenticulocapsular region or corona radiata (impairment in spinothalamic tract sensory modalities), 2 had
pontine tegmental strokes (medial lemniscal type of selective sensory loss), and 1 had a small cortical infarct (cortical sensory
loss) [203]. Hemisensory deficits to all modalities are usually associated with relatively large lacunar strokes or hemorrhages
involving the lateral thalamus, whereas tract-specific or restricted sensory changes suggest very small strokes involving the
sensory pathway between the pons and the parietal cortex [203].
4. AH, the second most common lacunar syndrome, usually occurs with a lacunar stroke involving the posterior limb of the
internal capsule, basis pontis, or corona radiata [140 , 143 , 186 , 370]. However, less than half of the cases are attributed to small
vessel disease, with a substantial number of cases attributed to cardioembolism and large artery atherosclerosis [ 143 , 280]. AH
is characterized by mild to moderate hemiparesis, predominantly affecting the foot and leg more than it does the hand and
arm, and homolateral cerebellar ataxia out of proportion to the motor deficit. There is usually an extensor plantar response.
Facial involvement is rare. Cortical signs, visual field deficits, and dysarthria are absent. AH may also result from lesions in
the contralateral thalamocapsular region, red nucleus, corona radiata, and lentiform nucleus. Likewise, AH may result from
SCA territory infarctions and superficial ACA territory infarctions.
The clinical constellation associated with AH has been expanded to include hemiataxia-hypesthesia syndrome, painful AH,
hypesthesic AH, AH accompanied by contralateral sensorimotor or motor trigeminal weakness, dysarthria-hemiataxia, and
quadrataxic hemiparesis [8 , 143 , 187 , 265 , 280 , 328].
5. DCHS, the least common of the lacunar syndromes. DCHS is the result of a lacune involving the anterior limb or genu of
the internal capsule or the depth of the basis pontis between its upper third and lower two-thirds. The syndrome is
characterized by supranuclear facial weakness, tongue deviation, dysarthria, dysphagia, loss of fine hand motor control, and
an extensor plantar response. Lacunar infarctions in the corona radiata, basal ganglia, thalamus, and cerebral peduncle may
also result in this rare syndrome [12 , 186]. Small hemorrhages involving the putamen or genu of the internal capsule may also
result in DCHS [290]. Dysarthria is also a major finding in other lacunar syndromes such as dysarthria-PMH, pure dysarthria,
dysarthria-facial paresis, and dysarthria-facial-lingual paresis (capsular genu syndrome) [379 , 380 , 381].
6. Sensorimotor stroke is often the result of a lacune involving the posteroventral thalamus and adjacent posterior limb of the
internal capsule, or lateral pons. Sensorimotor stroke due to a lacunar infarction causes hemibody (face, arm, and leg)

sensorimotor deficits. Selective involvement of the face and arm, sparing the leg, suggests a non-lacunar
mechanism [ 51].
Cerebral Hemorrhage Syndromes
ICH accounts for 10% of all strokes. The most common topographies of ICH are the putamen (lenticulostriate branches of the
MCA), thalamus (thalamogeniculate branches of the PCA), cerebral lobes (penetrating cortical branches of major brain vessels),
pons (paramedian branches of the basilar artery), and the dentate nucleus region of the cerebellar hemisphere (branches of the SCA)
[1 , 136 , 146 , 260 , 391].
Arterial hypertension is the most common cause of spontaneous (nontraumatic) ICH. Approximately 80% of hypertensive ICHs are
supratentorial, and the remainder are infratentorial. CAA presenting as lobar ICH is a major cause of ICH in the elderly. Besides
chronic arterial hypertension (hypertensive cerebral vascular disease) and CAA, ICH may also result from ruptured intracranial


<!-- PDF page 939 -->

aneurysms, ruptured brain AVMs, or other CNS vascular malformations (cavernous malformations, dural arteriovenous fistulae,
etc), bleeding diatheses, primary or metastatic brain tumors, vasculitis, anticoagulant/antiplatelet/thrombolytic therapy, or use of
illicit “recreational” drugs. ICH resulting from cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, infectious disorders, or following surgical
procedures, hyperperfusion syndrome, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS), or autonomic dysreflexia, is less
frequent (Table 22-3) [48 , 116 , 323 , 331 , 343].
Intracerebral hematomas associated with ruptured intracranial aneurysms most often result from ruptured MCA, AComA, or ICA
aneurysms. Ruptured MCA aneurysms often result in anterior temporal lobe or insular hematomas [288]. Hematomas resulting from
ruptured AComA complex or more distal branches of the ACA involve the septum and inferior frontal lobes. Ruptured distal ACA
aneurysms result in hematomas of the anterior superior aspect of the corpus callosum. Hematomas arising from ruptured ICA
bifurcation aneurysms involve the anterior temporal lobes or temporal horns. Ruptured intracranial aneurysms may also result in
intraventricular and/or subdural hemorrhages. Brain AVMs are a leading cause of ICH in children and young adults and are
classified according to the Spetzler-Martin grade based on their functional localization (eloquent area: Yes/No), size, and patterns of
venous drainage [354].
General Features
Clinical features of ICH often resemble those of an ischemic stroke. Severe headaches, vomiting, elevated blood pressure, impaired
level of alertness, and underlying bleeding diatheses, favor a presumptive diagnosis of ICH. Neurologic deterioration is primarily
due to hematoma expansion, observed during the first 6 hours after onset of hemorrhage, and worsening brain edema [67 , 149 , 389].
The damage caused by an ICH depends on the location of the intracranial bleeding. Neurologic manifestations vary according to
hematoma topography, hematoma volume, direction of spread, and rate of hematoma expansion [124]. Approximately half of the
patients present with headaches, nausea, or vomiting, and variable level of alertness. Seizures are common with lobar hemorrhages.
Meningeal irritation signs are present with bleeding extension into the subarachnoid space. Ophthalmoscopy may show retinal
hemorrhages, vitreous hemorrhages, and/or changes consistent with hypertensive retinopathy.
The most commonly involved areas are the putamen, the lobar subcortical white matter, thalamus, cerebellum, pons, caudate
nucleus, and ventricles.

Putaminal Hemorrhage
Putaminal hemorrhages account for one-third of all hypertensive ICHs. The bleeding may remain localized to the putamen or
expand to involve the internal capsule, corona radiata, centrum semiovale, external capsule, claustrum, temporal lobe or may rupture
into the ventricles [355]. Putaminal hemorrhages may result in contralateral hemiparesis or hemiplegia and conjugate gaze
preference to the side of the parenchymal hematoma [159]. Pupillary size and reactivity are usually spared. There may be
contralateral hemisensory loss. Left putaminal hemorrhages may cause aphasia. Right putaminal hemorrhages may result in
apractagnosia, left visual field neglect, and constructional apraxia. Right putaminal hemorrhages may also result in alloesthesia
(mislocation of sensory stimuli), where a noxious stimulus on the side of the hemisensory disturbance is perceived (after a half-
second delay) to be at the corresponding area of the opposite (normal) side [ 191]. Alloesthesia frequently involves the trunk and
proximal limbs. Low Glasgow Score at presentation, hydrocephalus, intraventricular blood, and large hematoma volume often
predict poor functional outcome or death [109 , 376 , 377]. The ICH Score is a valuable and widely adopted severity of illness scoring
system for ICH patients [158].

Lobar Hemorrhage
Lobar ICHs account for 7% to 32% of spontaneous intraparenchymal bleeds [355]. Lobar ICHs originate at the gray-white matter
junction, extend into the adjacent white matter, and are usually situated within a single lobe. Hemorrhage volumes of lobar ICHs
have been shown to be consistently larger than those in deep hemispheric locations [117]. Lobar ICHs result from a variety of
etiologies, including CAA, ruptured brain AVMs, ruptured intracranial aneurysms, bleeding diatheses, coagulopathies, primary or
metastatic brain tumors, cortical or dural venous sinus thrombosis, CNS infections, side effects of sympathomimetic drugs,
anticoagulants, or thrombolytic agents, and hypertensive cerebrovascular disease [151 , 260].
Frontal lobe ICHs cause headaches, vomiting, contralateral hemiparesis, abulia, and conjugate deviation of the eyes toward the side
of the hematoma [325].
Parietal lobe ICHs cause pain on the ipsilateral temple or above the ear, contralateral hemisensory loss, and neglect of the opposite
visual field. Parietal lobe hematomas may also cause variable degrees of contralateral inferior homonymous hemianopia, mild
hemiparesis, and anosognosia. Left parietal lobe hematomas may cause Gerstmann syndrome [325].
Left temporal lobe ICHs cause Wernicke aphasia. Hematomas affecting the left temporoparietal area result in conduction or global
aphasia. Temporal lobe hematomas may also cause headaches around or anterior to the ipsilateral ear, contralateral superior
homonymous quadrantanopia (colloquially referred as “pie in the sky”) with damage to the inferior optic radiations, or homonymous
hemianopia. Occasionally, patients present with agitated delirium [325].
Occipital lobe ICHs cause ipsilateral orbital pain and result in contralateral homonymous hemianopsia [325].
Focal seizures with secondary generalization may develop during the acute phase of a lobar hematoma; large and expanding
hematomas may also result in hydrocephalus [303 , 385].
Thalamic Hemorrhage
Thalamic hemorrhages are largely attributed to hypertensive cerebrovascular disease and rupture of small intracerebral arterioles [
151 , 228]. The bleeding may be confined to the thalamus or extend laterally to involve the internal capsule, inferomedially to
compromise the subthalamus and midbrain, or medially to involve the third ventricle [232 , 406].
Clinical features vary according to the location of the bleed and the bleeding source [ 87]. Typically, there is contralateral pansensory
loss and oculomotor abnormalities, including impaired vertical gaze

internal capsule results in contralateral hemiparesis. Left thalamic hematomas may cause transient aphasia, whereas right thalamic

(pseudo-sixth nerve palsy). The eyes may become tonically deviated downward and slightly abducted, away from the hematoma

Small thalamic hemorrhages (<2 cm) have been divided into four subtypes [ 189]:
1. Anterolateral type. Hematomas in this location may produce mild “prefrontal lobe” signs (impaired verbal memory and
inattention) and mild sensory and motor impairment.
2. Posterolateral type. These hematomas may result in severe motor and sensory disability and ocular abnormalities (miosis,
impaired light reflex, upward gaze palsy). These hematomas have the poorest prognosis, with residual hemiparesis or sensory
loss in most.
3. Medial type. These hematomas may account for an impaired level of consciousness in the acute stage, followed by longer
lasting “prefrontal lobe” signs, including decreased spontaneity and memory impairment.
4. Dorsal type. These hematomas may produce “parieto-occipital” signs, including aphasia with left-sided lesions, or
topographic memory disturbances with right-sided lesions.
The syndrome of posterior thalamic hemorrhage may result in the following signs or symptoms: Saccadic hypometria away from
the side of the lesion, defective smooth ocular pursuit toward the lesion with corresponding opticokinetic abnormalities, mild
ipsilateral eyelid ptosis and miosis, unilateral sensory “thalamic” neglect, contralateral sensorimotor hemiparesis, and forced
horizontal gaze deviation toward the lesion [160].
Thalamic hemorrhages have also been divided into four regional types and one global type [87]:
1. Anterior type (7%). These hematomas situated in the territory of the tuberothalamic arteries often ruptured into the anterior
horn of the lateral ventricle. Main findings were acute onset of behavioral abnormalities. The clinical course was usually
benign.
2. Posteromedial type (14%). Localized within the territory of the thalamic-subthalamic paramedian arteries, these hematomas
often rupture into the third ventricle, resulting in hydrocephalus. Outcome was poor with medial and caudal extension into the
mesencephalon.
3. Posterolateral type (44%). Situated in the territory of the thalamogeniculate arteries, these large hematomas often rupture
into the posterior horn of the lateral ventricles, extending into the posterior limb of the internal capsule. There is marked
sensorimotor impairment, hemineglect if the right thalamus is affected, or language abnormalities if the hematoma involves
the left thalamus. Case fatality was high, frequently resulting in permanent neurologic sequelae.
4. Dorsal type (18%). These hematomas, situated in the territory of the posterior choroidal arteries, cause a combination of
sensory and motor signs and are associated with an excellent prognosis.
5. Global type (18%). These types of hematomas involve the whole thalamus. Clinical manifestations often resemble the
posterolateral variant, with severe sensory and motor deficits, stupor or coma, decerebrate posturing, upward gaze paralysis,
and small but reactive pupils. Global type of thalamic ICHs are associated with hydrocephalus and with poor prognosis and
high fatality rate.
Cerebellar Hemorrhage
Cerebellar hemorrhages warrant prompt neurosurgical assessment [219]. Cerebellar hemorrhages account for about 10% of
spontaneous ICH and for approximately 10% of all cerebellar strokes [64 , 153 , 320 , 358]. Variations in site, size, and development of
cerebellar hemorrhage, brainstem compression, fourth ventricular rupture, and development of acute obstructive hydrocephalus
account for the heterogeneous presentation of these hematomas. Large cerebellar hemorrhages behave as space-occupying mass
lesions, leading to tonsillar herniation, upward tentorial herniation, or brainstem compression. The most common cause of
spontaneous (nontraumatic) cerebellar hemorrhage is hypertensive vasculopathy. Hypertensive cerebellar hemorrhages situated in
the region of the dentate nucleus result from rupture of distal SCA branches. Less often, the epicenter of the cerebellar hemorrhage
is in the vermis. Cerebellar hemorrhages may also result from trauma, ruptured CNS malformations, coagulopathies, side effects of
anticoagulant therapy, side effects of thrombolytic therapy, hemorrhagic metastases, CAA, complications of
cervical/thoracic/lumbar spinal surgery, ventriculoperitoneal/lumboperitoneal shunts, and supratentorial surgical

interventions. Cerebellar hematomas may also occur in extremely low-birth-weight infants [ 21 , 42 , 63 , 78 , 122 , 165 , 174 , 219 , 233 , 249 ,
251 , 277 , 278 , 284 , 302 , 333].

vomiting, slurred speech, inability to stand or walk, truncal or limb ataxia, ipsilateral gaze palsy, skew deviation, ocular bobbing,
small reactive pupils, horizontal gaze paresis, paretic nystagmus, facial weakness, and a decreased corneal reflex. Horizontal gaze

in vomiting, gait instability, or limb ataxia without associated headaches.
Pontine Hemorrhage
Pontine hemorrhages account for approximately 10% of spontaneous ICHs. The most common causes are arterial hypertension and
ruptured CNS vascular malformations such as cavernous malformations [89]. Primary pontine hemorrhages result from ruptured
paramedian pontine arterioles at the midpontine level, near the junction of the basis pontis and tegmentum. Clinical manifestations
depend on size, site, and presence or absence of ventricular rupture or hydrocephalus [396]. Goto et al. [144] divided pontine
hemorrhages into tegmental and tegmentobasilar. Massive pontine hemorrhages, involving the whole area of the pons, compress the
fourth ventricle, causing acute obstructive hydrocephalus, resulting in coma, decerebrate rigidity, quadriparesis, hyperthermia,
tachycardia, absent corneal reflexes, ocular bobbing, absent horizontal eye movements, and miotic (2 to 3 mm) but reactive pupils [
144]. A locked-in syndrome is seldom present, because these hematomas symmetrically dissect the pons, destroying the more dorsal
structures. Bilateral deafness due to involvement of the ventral acoustic striae decussating in the trapezoid body is an uncommon
finding [114]. Coma, absence of oculocephalic reflexes, absence of corneal reflexes, no motor responses, hypertension, tachycardia,
hyperthermia (core temperature ≥ 39°C), acute obstructive hydrocephalus, and presence of intraventricular blood predict a very poor
outcome [35 , 396].
Primary pontine hemorrhages have been divided into three clinical types [231]:
1. Classic type (60%). Associated with severe and extensive pontine damage of both pontine basis and tegmentum bilaterally
and results in quadriparesis, coma, hyperthermia, tachycardia, and death.
2. Hemipontine syndrome (20%). Associated with unilateral involvement of the basis pontis and pontine tegmentum,
hemiparesis, preserved level of consciousness, skew deviation, unilateral absent corneal reflex, dysarthria, facial nerve palsy,
contralateral extremity, and ipsilateral facial sensory changes. These hemorrhages have been associated with good functional
recovery.
3. Dorsolateral tegmental syndrome (20%). Results in gaze paresis or ipsilateral CN VI palsy (or both), skew deviation,
unilateral absent corneal reflex, unilateral facial nerve palsy, contralateral extremity and ipsilateral facial sensory loss,
dysarthria, motor sparing, preserved consciousness, and gait or limb ataxia, and survival with good functional recovery.
Caudate Hemorrhage
Hemorrhages within the caudate nucleus result from rupture of the artery of Heubner or branches of the medial anterior and lateral
lenticulostriate arteries. The least common of the hypertensive ICHs, caudate hemorrhages cause headache, vomiting, neck stiffness,
confusion, and behavioral changes with impairment in tests of short-term and long-term memory. Variable findings include a
transient contralateral conjugate gaze paresis, contralateral hemiparesis, transient hemisensory deficits, and, rarely, an ipsilateral
Horner syndrome when the hematoma extends into the hypothalamus [243 , 356].
Mesencephalic Hemorrhage
Isolated spontaneous mesencephalic hemorrhages are rare and often the result of a superior extension of a pontine or cerebellar
hemorrhage or an inferior extension from a thalamic or putaminal hemorrhage. Hemorrhages within the mesencephalon [ 392] often
present with headaches, vomiting, and impaired consciousness. Unequal pupils unreactive to light but retaining the near reflex are
common, as is impairment of conjugate upgaze. Partial dorsal mesencephalic hemorrhages may result in a Parinaud syndrome
(with rostral tectal plate bleeds), vertical gaze palsy, skew deviation, and bilateral Horner syndrome (with unilateral superior
colliculus bleed), as well as bilateral trochlear nerve palsies, unilateral Horner syndrome, and ataxia (with hemorrhages involving
the caudal tectal plate) [255 , 287 , 329 , 392].

Medullary Hemorrhage
Primary medullary hemorrhages are extremely rare. Most frequent manifestations include headache, vertigo, sensory disturbances in
the face or extremities, dysarthria, dysphagia, palatal weakness, nystagmus, hypoglossal nerve palsy, cerebellar ataxia, and limb
weakness [32 , 44 , 239].
Internal Capsular Hemorrhage
Small hemorrhages arising within the genu or posterior limb of the internal capsule may cause a PMH or a mixed sensorimotor
syndrome. In rare instances, paraparesis results from bilateral hemorrhages involving the posterior limb of the internal capsule [212 ,
362].
Intraventricular Hemorrhages
Intraventricular hemorrhages in adults most commonly result from ventricular extension of a parenchymal hematoma. Primary
intraventricular hemorrhages in adults may result from ruptured AComA aneurysms, AVMs adjacent to the ventricles or choroid
plexus, choroid plexus papillomas, bleeding diatheses, coagulopathies, thrombolytic agents, or moyamoya disease. Clinical features
include headaches, nausea, vomiting, impaired consciousness, memory disturbances, and nuchal rigidity. Among very low-birth-
weight infants, the majority of intraventricular hemorrhages originate in the subependymal germinal matrix [ 40 , 355].
Syndromes Related to Cerebral Aneurysms
Unruptured intracranial aneurysms are a relatively common autopsy finding. An estimated 3% of the general population harbors an
unruptured intracranial saccular aneurysm [173 , 266 , 300]. Unruptured, asymptomatic intracranial saccular aneurysms have been found
in approximately 5% of autopsies [173]. Brain aneurysms may be saccular, fusiform, traumatic, dissecting, inflammatory/infective
(mycotic), oncotic, flow related, or associated with a systemic or CNS vasculitic disorders. Approximately 90% of intracranial
saccular aneurysms involve the carotid circulation and 10% the vertebrobasilar system. Brain aneurysms are multiple in
approximately 10% to 30% of patients.
Saccular brain aneurysms occur at bifurcation points of major intracranial arteries, most commonly in the circle of Willis. Most
brain aneurysms are found at the junction of the AComA with the ACA, junction of the ICA and PComA, the MCA
bifurcation/trifurcation in the Sylvian fissure, the ICA bifurcation, and the pericallosal/callosomarginal artery bifurcation. Most
vertebrobasilar aneurysms are found at the basilar artery apex, and the remaining at the PICA, VA, AICA, or SCA.
Intracranial aneurysms are rare in infants or children. Pediatric intracranial aneurysms are located mostly at the distal ICA or
vertebrobasilar circulation.
Unruptured intracranial aneurysms may present with headaches, cranial nerve deficits, brain ischemia, or seizures, as a result of
mass effect or distal clot embolization. Once brain aneurysms reach a diameter of 2.5 cm or greater (giant aneurysms), they behave
like a space-occupying lesion [172 , 293 , 315 , 360]. A brief discussion of selective brain aneurysm-related syndromes follows.

Subarachnoid Hemorrhage
Extravasation of blood within the subarachnoid space between the arachnoid membrane and the pia mater constitutes an SAH. SAH

receiving medical attention. Population-based studies suggest that the incidence of aneurysmal SAH ranges between 2 and 16 per

The most common presenting symptom of an aneurysmal SAH is a severe, abrupt, and excruciating headache located mainly in the
occipital-nuchal area. Some patients present with neck stiffness, nausea, vomiting, and photophobia. Diplopia is also common. A
sudden rise in intracranial pressure may result in transient loss of consciousness. Some patients have backaches and/or leg pains.
The clinical spectrum is variable. Misdiagnosis is not infrequent, particularly among patients with mild symptoms [225 , 244 , 319 , 384].
Pain response to commonly used therapies in the management of acute headaches, including triptans, should not exclude a diagnosis
of SAH. Focal or generalized seizures may be present in ˜2% of patients. Meningismus with nuchal rigidity, Kernig sign, or
Brudzinski sign is common but not invariable.
Ophthalmoscopic findings may include vitreous, subhyaloid, or preretinal hemorrhages. The syndrome of vitreous hemorrhage in
association with any forms of intracranial hemorrhage or SAH is known as Terson syndrome[ 338]. Eyelid ptosis or diplopia is most
frequently caused by CN III palsy due to an ICA-PComA junction aneurysm. CN III palsy may also result from aneurysms located
at the ICA bifurcation, PCA, basilar artery tip, or SCA or result from uncal herniation. Unilateral or bilateral CN VI palsies may
reflect raised intracranial pressure and therefore lack localizing valve. Visual field defects or sudden loss of vision may result from
aneurysmal rupture adjacent to the visual pathways. Focal neurologic findings, such as aphasia, hemiparesis, sensory impairment,
and abnormal reflexes, may reflect the location of the ruptured aneurysm, delayed cerebral ischemia, or simultaneous occurrence of
a parenchymal ICH.

cardiac arrhythmias, including prolonged QTc interval, myocardial infarction, severe hypertension, neurogenic stunned
myocardium, Takotsubo cardiomyopathy (broken-heart syndrome), neurogenic pulmonary edema, or hyponatremia due to central
salt-wasting syndrome or inappropriate secretion of antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) [131 , 240 , 283]. Patients with central salt

Increased intracranial pressure may result from extensive SAH, a large parenchymal hematoma, delayed cerebral ischemia
secondary to cerebral vasospasm, brain edema, or hydrocephalus. Clinical deterioration following SAH may reflect rebleeding,
development of hydrocephalus, delayed cerebral ischemia secondary to cerebral vasospasm, or systemic complications. Rebleeding
occurs within 2 weeks of the initial SAH and peaks on the day of the initial SAH. Rebleeding is to be suspected when there is
further alteration of alertness, increasing headaches, worsening neurologic deficits, or unexplained fever or hypertension. Overall
risk of rebleeding is approximately 50% within the first 6 months of presentation and then about 3% per year [399]. Delayed cerebral
ischemia from cerebral vasospasm is now the leading cause of death and disability among patients with aneurysmal SAH.
Angiographic studies demonstrate vasospasm in up to 70% of patients. Clinically significant cerebral vasospasm occurs in one-third
of patients with aneurysmal SAH. Cerebral vasospasm develops gradually, peaks around days 6 through 10, and usually subsides in
3 weeks following aneurysmal SAH [ 45]. Cerebral vasospasm accounts for increasing headaches, meningismus, low-grade fever,
decreased level of alertness, and focal neurologic deficits corresponding to brain regions supplied by the involved arteries. The most
reliable predictor of the development of cerebral vasospasm is the amount and distribution of blood on a head CT scan after SAH.
Thick, localized clots or diffuse SAH predict poor neurologic status [2].
Persistent or recurrent slow bleeding into the subpial layers of the brain and spinal cord may lead to superficial siderosis of the CNS.
Two subtypes of superficial siderosis of the CNS have been recognized: infratentorial, or classic, and cortical superficial CNS
siderosis. Infratentorial superficial siderosis of the CNS is characterized by progressive bilateral sensorineural hearing loss,
cerebellar ataxia, and myelopathy. Other manifestations include anosmia, dementia, hydrocephalus, spasticity, neurogenic bladder,
bowel disturbances, anisocoria, extraocular motor palsies, optic neuropathy, tinnitus, nystagmus, dysarthria, ageusia, neck or
backache, sensory signs, bilateral sciatica, lumbosacral radiculopathies, and lower motor neuron signs. Other vascular lesions
associated with superficial siderosis of the CNS include AVMs, unruptured intracranial aneurysms, cavernous malformations, and
telangiectasias. Superficial siderosis of the CNS can also result from chronic bleeding from brain or spinal cord tumors—most
commonly, cauda equina myxopapillary ependymomas, posterior fossa tumors, hemispherectomy, radiotherapy, brachial plexus
injuries, traumatic cervical nerve root avulsion with subsequent pseudo-meningocele formation, cervical or lumbar meningoceles,
subdural hematoma, and trauma associated with warfarin use. Other etiologies of superficial siderosis of the CNS include CAA,
meningiomas, oligodendrogliomas, pineocytomas, paragangliomas, repair of occipital encephalocele, and neonatal intraventricular
bleeding [141 , 177 , 245].
Cortical superficial siderosis of the CNS, often the result of CAA, is characterized by stereotypical episodes of transient focal
neurologic deficits, including positive and negative phenomena (“amyloid spells”), cognitive impairment, headaches, seizures, and
increased risk for ICH. Almost one-third to one-half of cases are idiopathic [164 , 193 , 227].
