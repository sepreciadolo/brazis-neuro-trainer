CHAPTER 16
The Cerebellum
Anatomy of the Cerebellum
The neuron-rich cerebellum (Fig. 16-1A,B) derives from the hindbrain vesicle (rhomboencephalon) [12 , 128 , 179]. The
rhomboencephalon includes the cerebellum, pons, and medulla oblongata. Remarkable morphologic features of the cerebellum are
its foliation, laminar structure, unique afferent and efferent connectivity, and molecular organization [ 47 , 77 , 145 , 160 , 170]. The
cerebellum participates in numerous motor functions and is primarily responsible for the modulation of muscle tone, posture, and
equilibrium. The cerebellum plays a major role in the automatic excitation of antagonist muscles at the end of a movement while
simultaneously inhibiting the agonist muscles that initiated the movement. Furthermore, the cerebellum has a critical role in
cognition, emotion, and behavior [ 135].
Located in the posterior fossa, dorsal to the pons and medulla oblongata, the cerebellum forms the roof of the fourth ventricle and is
separated from the occipital lobes by the tentorium cerebelli. On axial and coronal planes, a midline region, the vermis, and two
lateral regions, the cerebellar hemispheres are recognized. The falx cerebelli, a dural partition, partially separates the two cerebellar
hemispheres. Sagittal and coronal planes best display the three major cerebellar regions: the anterior lobe, the posterior lobe, and
the flocculonodular lobe. The anterior lobe is separated from the posterior lobe by the primary fissure, whereas the flocculonodular
lobe is separated from the posterior lobe by the posterolateral fissure. The anterior lobe comprises Larsell lobules I to V, the
posterior lobe comprises Larsell lobules VI to IX, and the flocculonodular lobe comprises lobule X. The medioventral border of the
posterior lobe of each cerebellar hemisphere is formed by the cerebellar tonsil [43].
Physiologic studies highlight multiple somatotopic maps with the following homunculi organization. The hand is represented in the
ipsilateral anterior lobe corresponding to Larsell lobules IV to V. The foot is represented in the ipsilateral central lobule,
corresponding to Larsell's lobules II to III. The tongue is mapped posterior and lateral to the hand area, extending in part to Larsell
lobules VI to VII [ 114].
The cerebellum consists of a superficial cortex surrounding the deep cerebellar white matter and three pairs of deep nuclei. The
cerebellar cortex is folded into the cerebellar folia. Histologically, the cerebellar cortex has three layers—an outer molecular layer, a
middle Purkinje cell layer, and an inner granular cell layer. Five cell types are recognized in these three cerebellar cortical layers:
outer stellate cells and inner basket cells in the molecular layer; Purkinje cells, arranged in a single row in the Purkinje cell layer;
and granule cells and Golgi cells, in the granular cell layer. All cells except the Purkinje cells, whose axons project outside the
cerebellum, are intrinsic neurons establishing synaptic connections only within the cerebellum (Fig. 16-2).
The cerebellar white matter is made up of three groups of fibers: intrinsic fibers, which do not leave the cerebellum; afferent fibers,
which enter the cerebellum mainly through the inferior and middle cerebellar peduncles; and efferent fibers, which are the Purkinje
cells axons. Afferent fibers to the cerebellum segregate into one of three fiber systems: climbing, mossy, and multilayered. Climbing
fibers are the terminal fibers of the olivocerebellar tracts and establish multiple synapsis with a single Purkinje cell. The mossy fiber
system includes all cerebellar afferent tracts except those that contribute to the climbing fibers and the multilayered system. In
contrast to the climbing fiber system, the mossy fiber system is diffuse; a single mossy fiber stimulates thousands of Purkinje cells
through the granule cells. The multilayered fiber system includes afferents from the hypothalamus, raphe nuclei, and locus ceruleus
[2].
Functional subdivisions of the cerebellum include the vestibulocerebellum, spinocerebelum, and cerebrocerebellum. The
vestibulocerebellum corresponds to the flocculonodular lobe and has rich connections with the vestibular system and with brain
areas concerned with eye movements [ 25 , 141 , 142]. The vestibulocerebellum has a major role in the modulation of body equilibrium
and eye movements. The spinocerebellum consists of the vermis of the anterior lobe, the pyramis, the uvula and the paraflocculus.
The spinocerebellum receives input mainly from the spinal cord and plays a major role in the modulation of muscle tone and axial
and limb movements. The cerebrocerebellum consists of the middle region of the

Three pairs of deep nuclei on each side of the midline within the white matter core of the cerebellum receive input from the
cerebellar cortex and incoming afferents. From medial to lateral, these nuclei are the fastigial nucleus, the nucleus interpositus
(formed by the emboliform and globose nuclei), and the dentate nucleus. These nuclei are the main source of cerebellar efferents.
All afferent projections from these nuclei are excitatory, except those to the inferior olive, which are inhibitory. Based on these


<!-- PDF page 648 -->

nuclear connections, the cerebellum is longitudinally subdivided into a midline (vermal) zone containing neurons projecting to the
fastigial nucleus; an intermediate (paravermal) zone containing neurons projecting to the nucleus interpositus; and a lateral
(hemispheric) zone containing neurons projecting to the dentate nucleus. Each cerebellar nucleus has a somatotopic arrangement,
with caudal body regions mapped anteriorly, rostral body regions mapped posteriorly, the trunk mapped laterally, and the limbs
medially [59 , 163].
The fastigial nucleus is involved with stance and gait, and modulates muscles are involved in activities such as sitting, standing or
walking. Lesions of this nucleus result in abasia. The nucleus interpositus is involved with segmental reflexes concerned with
stability. It also hastens the initiation of movements triggered by somatosensory cues, ceases unwanted oscillations while promoting
needed oscillations. Lesions of the nucleus interpositus result in delayed check responses, truncal titubation, impaired rapid
alternating movements, action tremor, oscillation of the outstretched limbs, and finger-nose-finger and heel-knee-shin ataxia. The
dentate nucleus is involved with tasks requiring fine dexterity. Lesions involving this nucleus or its projections cause delays in the
initiation of movements requiring the activation of multiple joints and impaired special coordination of hand and finger movements.
The cerebellum is joined to the brainstem by three pairs of cerebellar peduncles: the inferior cerebellar peduncle (restiform body),
the middle cerebellar peduncle (brachium pontis), and the superior cerebellar peduncle (brachium conjunctivum). The inferior and
superior cerebellar peduncles carry afferent and efferent tracts; the middle cerebellar peduncle is exclusively afferent. Most afferent
fibers projecting to the cerebellum do so via the inferior and middle cerebellar peduncles, whereas efferent fibers from the
cerebellum travel in the superior and the inferior cerebellar peduncles.
The inferior cerebellar peduncle, which consists of the restiform body and the juxtarestiform body, joins the cerebellum to the
superior half of the medulla oblongata and carries afferent and efferent fibers. Afferent fibers of clinical relevance include the
following:
1. The dorsal spinocerebellar tract, which originates in the dorsal nucleus of Clarke (T1-L2), carries unconscious
proprioception and exteroceptive information mainly from the trunk and ipsilateral lower extremity.
2. The cuneocerebellar tract, originating in the external arcuate nucleus, carries unconscious proprioceptive information from
the upper extremity and neck.
3. The olivocerebellar tract carries somatosensory information from the contralateral inferior olivary nucleus.
4. The vestibulocerebellar tract conveys impulses from vestibular receptors on both sides of the body.
5. The reticulocerebellar tract, which arises in the lateral reticular and paramedian nuclei of the medulla.
6. The arcuatocerebellar tract, which arises from the arcuate nuclei of the medulla oblongata.
7. The trigeminocerebellar tract, which arises from the spinal cord and main sensory nuclei of the trigeminal nerve.
Efferents in the inferior cerebellar peduncle include cerebellovestibular, cerebelloreticular, and cerebello-olivary pathways. The
fiber bundles of the juxtarestiform body located within the lateral and spinal vestibular nuclei have rich intrinsic vestibular
connections.
The middle cerebellar peduncle, the largest peduncle, originates from the contralateral pontine nuclei, is purely afferent, and
connects the cerebellum to the pons. It carries afferents from the pontocerebellar (corticopontocerebellar) tract, which terminates as
mossy fibers.
The superior cerebellar peduncle connects the cerebellum to the lower part of the midbrain. It carries mainly efferent fibers and also
a few afferent fibers. Afferent fibers include the following:
1. The ventral spinocerebellar tract, which transmits proprioceptive and exteroceptive information from below midthoracic
cord levels.
2. The tectocerebellar tract, arising in the superior and inferior colliculi, which carries auditory and visual information.
3. The trigeminocerebellar tract, which conveys proprioceptive fibers from the mesencephalon and

tactile information from the chief sensory nucleus of the trigeminal nerve.
4. The cerulocerebellar tract, which carries fibers from the nucleus ceruleus.

Vascular Supply of the Cerebellum
The cerebellum receives its blood supply from the paired inferior cerebellar arteries (PICAs), the anterior inferior cerebellar arteries
(AICAs), and the superior cerebellar arteries (SCAs) (Fig. 16-3) [4 , 134]. Branches of these three arteries anastomose with branches
from the corresponding vessels on the opposite side to provide a rich anastomotic network.
1. The PICA arises from the intracranial vertebral artery and supplies the half of the cerebellum below the plane of the great
horizontal fissure, encompassing the lateral medullary tegmentum, inferior cerebellar peduncle, ipsilateral aspect of the
inferior vermis, and inferior surface of the cerebellar hemisphere. The vermian or medial branch of PICA supplies the medial
aspect of the cerebellum and the dorsolateral medulla oblongata; the tonsillar-hemispheric or lateral branch supplies the
infero-posterolateral aspect of the cerebellum.
FIGURE 16-3. Course of the cerebellar arteries. SCA, superior cerebellar artery; AICA, anterior inferior
cerebellar artery; PICA, posterior inferior cerebellar artery. (Adapted from Amarenco P. The spectrum of
cerebellar infarctions. Neurology. 1991;41:973-979, with permission.)
2. The AICA, which often arises about 1 cm above the origin of the basilar artery, most frequently as a single vessel, supplies
the anterior petrosal surface of the cerebellar hemisphere, the flocculus, lower portion of the middle cerebellar peduncle, and
lateral pontomedullary tegmentum. Typically, the AICA gives origin to the internal auditory artery that enters the internal
acoustic meatus.
3. The SCA arises near the distal segment of the basilar artery just below the terminal bifurcation into the paired posterior
cerebral arteries (PCAs) and supplies the upper surface of the cerebellar hemisphere, ipsilateral portion of the superior vermis,
most of the dentate nucleus, upper portion of the middle cerebellar peduncle, superior cerebellar peduncle, and lateral pontine
tegmentum. The oculomotor nerve (CN III) and the trochlear nerve (CN IV) run between the SCA and the PCA.

Clinical Manifestations of Cerebellar Dysfunction


<!-- PDF page 650 -->

Since the seminal contributions by Gordon Holmes in patients with wound injuries to the cerebellum [73 , 74], many new cerebellar
disorders have been identified. Cardinal features of cerebellar dysfunction include hypotonia, limb ataxia, dysmetria, dyssynergia,
intention end-point or kinetic tremor, dysdiadochokinesia, gait ataxia, and nystagmus [75].
Hypotonia
Hypotonia may result from acute cerebellar hemispheric lesions. The hypotonia is ipsilateral to the side of cerebellar damage and
more noticeable in proximal segments of the upper limbs. The hypotonic extremities often exhibit pendular muscle stretch reflexes.
The hypotonia is probably the result of decreased fusimotor activity secondary to the cerebellar injury, especially to the dentate
nucleus.
Cerebellar Ataxia
Cerebellar lesions result in defective timing of the sequential contractions of agonist and antagonist muscles [60]. Ataxia, regarded
as the “cerebellar sign par excellence,” refers to a disturbance in the smooth performance of voluntary motor acts causing muscular
incoordination or impaired balance [26]. The movements flaw in rate, range, timing, direction, and force. In the absence of
cerebellar inhibitory and modulating influences, skilled movements originating in the cerebral motor cortex become inaccurate and
poorly controlled. Ataxia may affect the limbs, the trunk, or gait. The clinical presentation may be acute (e.g., cerebellar
hemorrhage, cerebellar infarction, biotinidase deficiency, phenytoin intoxication, acute cerebellar ataxia following chicken pox).
The ataxia may be episodic, as observed in autosomal-dominant episodic ataxias (EA), which include the most prevalent EA-1
associated with mutations in the potassium channel, voltage-gated shaker related subfamily A, member 1 (KCNA1), and the EA-2
calcium channel, voltage-dependent, P/Q type, alpha 1A subunit (CACNA1A) genes. Myokymia is a typical interictal finding of
EA-1, whereas gaze-evoked or downbeat nystagmus is a characteristic interictal finding of EA-2 [54 , 84]. Other causes of episodic
ataxia include migraine with brainstem aura, urea cycle defects, X-linked ataxia with lactic acidosis, intermittent maple syrup urine
disease, and Hartnup disease. Finally, the ataxia may be chronic progressive or nonprogressive (e.g., cerebellar malformations,
cerebellar tumors, spinocerebellar ataxias [SCA], Friedreich ataxia, ataxia telangiectasia, hypothyroidism, gluten-related disorders,
celiac disease (gluten-sensitive enteropathy), autoimmune mediated cerebellar ataxias, superficial central nervous system siderosis,
paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration, central nervous system Whipple disease, and cerebellar ataxia, neuropathy, and vestibular
areflexia syndrome (CANVAS) (Tables 16-1, 16-2, 16-3) [19 , 27 , 30 , 36 , 50 , 54 , 57 , 65 , 68 , 69 , 70 , 71 , 76 , 78 , 85 , 93 , 101 , 102 , 106 , 107 , 108 , 109 ,
110 , 111 , 122 , 124 , 127 , 131 , 133 , 151 , 153 , 155 , 156 , 158 , 159 , 180 , 182].

The inherited ataxias comprise a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by variable combinations of progressive cerebellar
ataxia associated with a variable array of upper motor neuron signs, parkinsonism, tremor, myoclonus, dystonia, chorea, oculomotor
dysfunction, seizures, or signs of peripheral nervous system involvement.
These inherited cerebellar ataxias are further classified into autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, and X-linked forms.
Autosomal-dominant cerebellar ataxias typically present in adulthood and currently include spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) 1 to 48
according to the specific gene or chromosomal locus associated with these disorders [22 , 54 , 110 , 139]. The relative frequency of SCAs
phenotype differs between geographic world regions. Three of these SCAs are briefly described. Suggestive clinical features
suggestive of SCA 3 (Machado-Joseph disease), most commonly observed in Portugal, include progressive cerebellar ataxia,
nystagmus, ophthalmoplegia, facial fasciculations or myokymia, facial dystonia, eyelid retraction with bulging eyes, and
parkinsonism [140]. Features suggestive of dentatorubropallidoluysian atrophy (DRPLA), more prevalent in Asia, include cerebellar
ataxia, myoclonus, choreoathetosis, dystonia, parkinsonism, progressive myoclonic epilepsy, and dementia [112]. SCA-10, an
autosomal-dominant disorder characterized by a pentanucleotide ATTCT repeat intronic expansion (intron 9) in the SCA gene, is
more common in Mexican and Brazilian populations. The disorder is characterized by slowly progressive cerebellar ataxia (gait
ataxia, dysarthria, nystagmus) with or without epilepsy. The typical phenotype observed in Mexico is characterized by the
association of cerebellar ataxia with epilepsy (generalized or partial seizures). In Brazil, the SCA-2 phenotype is that of pure
cerebellar ataxia. Additional clinical features include polyneuropathy, upper motor neuron signs, reduced cognitive abilities, and
neuropsychiatric manifestations such as depression.
Autosomal recessive ataxias encompass a heterogeneous group of unusual disorders that typically result in early-onset cerebellar
ataxia associated with various neurologic, ophthalmologic, and systemic manifestations [57]. Pathologically characterized by the
degeneration of the spinocerebellar tracts, dorsal columns, and, to a lesser extent, the corticospinal tracts, Friedreich ataxia is the
most common of the autosomal recessive ataxias. Age at onset is typically 5 to 25 years. Friedreich ataxia is caused by a deficiency
of the mitochondrial protein frataxin. The vast majority of patients have an unstable GAA triplet-repeat expansion on the gene
encoding frataxin on chromosome 9q13. Friedreich ataxia is characterized by slowly progressive limb and gait ataxia, dysarthria,
rhythmic head tremor, abnormal eye movements (fixation instability, saccadic dysmetria, disruptive pursuit, and vestibular
abnormalities), optic atrophy, areflexia of knee and ankle jerks, loss of vibration and position sense, axonal polyneuropathy,
extensor plantar responses, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, diabetes mellitus, pes cavus, and scoliosis [39 , 48 , 49 , 56].
Cerebellar ataxia characteristically persists in spite of visual cues (unlike sensory ataxia, which is more pronounced with eyes
closed). The term ataxia includes other abnormalities, such as asynergia, characterized by the lack of synergy in performing more
complex movements so that they become disjointed and clumsy; dysmetria; and dysdiadochokinesia, characterized by the inability
to smoothly perform rapidly alternating movements.

Gait disturbance is among the most disabling manifestations of cerebellar disease. Typically, patients have a wide-based stance,
increased trunk sway, and irregular stepping with tendency to stagger and display impaired tandem walking with inappropriate
timing of foot placement [61 , 149]. Truncal instability may result in patients falling in any direction. Truncal ataxia and titubation
suggest midline cerebellar lesions.
An unusual gait disorder, known as quadrupedal palmigrade locomotion, in humans has been associated with cerebellar hypoplasia
and midline clefting of the cerebellar vermus and mapped to chromosome 17p [168]. More recently, mutations in the very-low
density lipoprotein receptor VLDLR have been reported to cause nonprogressive cerebellar hypoplasia [23 , 64]. Hypoplasia of the
cerebellum is also observed in a number of conditions, such as Joubert syndrome, Dandy-Walker syndrome, Tay-Sachs disease,
Menkes kinky hair disease, and a variety of congenital and sporadic cases of unknown cause [ 54].
Cerebellar Dysarthria
Dysarthria due to cerebellar disease is characterized by abnormalities in articulation and prosody [ 35 , 62]. Speech output is labored
with occasional excessive facial grimacing. Comprehension remains intact. Cerebellar dysarthria results in scanning, slurred,
staccato, explosive, hesitant, and garbled speech [81 , 129]. Agrammatic speech following right cerebellar strokes supports the notion
that the cerebellum provides temporal interplay among neural structures underlying processes responsible for sentence production [
146].
Functional MRI studies among healthy volunteers have shown that the cerebellar representation of the tongue and orofacial muscles
corresponds to the affected areas involved in patients with cerebellar dysarthria. In a series of 162 cases of focal cerebellar disease,
31 patients had dysarthria, of whom 22 had predominantly or exclusively left hemispheric lesions [90]. Speech disorders were more
common with hemispheric rather than with vermis lesions. Dysarthria was also more common with lesions extending into the
superior paravermal segment of the left cerebellar hemisphere. Because cerebrocerebellar connections are predominantly
contralateral and the nondominant cerebral hemisphere is concerned with prosody [90 , 129], the authors posited a dominance of the
left cerebellar hemisphere in speech regulation (melody and continuity). Confirming the relevance of the superior cerebellum in
voice control, other authors have found dysarthria to be as common with right-sided as with left-sided cerebellar lesions [2]. Silveri
et al. reported a patient with agrammatical speech after a PICA territory infarction, suggesting a right cerebellar hemispheric
dominance for language function [ 146]. Isolated cerebellar dysarthria, without any other deficit, has also been reported with a small
infarction in the left paravermal zone of the rostral cerebellum (lobulus simplex and semilunaris superior) in the territory of the
medial branch of the SCA [5], with right paravermal segment infarctions, with lesions in the upper paravermal area of the right
cerebellar hemisphere [171], as well as with an intact dentate nucleus [1].
Clinical and neuroimaging studies suggest that the cerebellum is also involved in language tasks [ 46]. Transient mutism following
excision of midline cerebellar tumors (or posterior fossa syndrome) is increasingly recognized [45]. Whether this condition is tumor
specific remains debatable. Symptoms often develop one to three days following posterior fossa surgery. In many instances, there is
associated oropharyngeal apraxia, impaired initiation of voluntary activity, including eye opening, and urinary retention [120]. It has
been speculated that transient bilateral involvement of the dentatorubrothalamic pathway results in this state of mutism and
subsequent dysarthria [21 , 28 , 38 , 53 , 121 , 125 , 172 , 173].
Tremor
Cerebellar lesions, especially those involving the dentate nucleus, result in a kinetic (intention) tremor. A static (postural) tremor
may also occur. The tremor results from interruption of the rubroolivocerebellar circuits [ 88]. Holmes tremor resulting from lesions
in the red nucleus, dentatothalamic pathways, and inferior olives, is characterized by a combination of resting, postural, and kinetic
tremor [33 , 87] (
Videos 16-1 and 16-2). Patients with Wilson disease may display tremor or ataxic dysmetria [116 , 178].

Ocular Motor Dysfunction
Nystagmus is frequently observed with cerebellar disorders. Gaze-evoked, upbeat, rebound, and abnormal optokinetic nystagmus
may be seen with midline cerebellar lesions [52 , 62]. Periodic alternating nystagmus is observed with lesions of the uvula and
nodulus or with lesions involving their connections with the brainstem vestibular nuclei. Downbeat nystagmus results from posterior
midline cerebellar lesions involving the vestibulocerebellum [95]. Positional nystagmus may occur among patients with posterior
fossa tumors. Ocular dysmetria, a conjugate overshoot or undershoot of a given target with voluntary saccades, may result from
midline or lateral cerebellar lesions [34 , 123 , 141]. Other ocular signs observed with cerebellar disorders include irregular tracking,
saccadic breakdown of pursuit eye movements, ocular flutter, opsoclonus, ocular bobbing, paresis of conjugate gaze, square wave
jerks at rest, skew deviation, ocular tilt reaction, and failure to suppress the vestibulo-ocular reflex [13 , 63 , 164].

Cerebellar Fits
Cerebellar disease may be associated with episodes of altered level of consciousness and extensor posturing, which have been
reported with large midline cerebellar lesions and among some children with Chiari 1 malformation [ 112 , 178].
Miscellaneous
CANVAS refers to a rare, progressive, multisystem disorder of uncertain pathophysiology, although it has been attributed to a
focal dorsal root ganglionopathy damaging Scarpa ganglion but sparing the spiral ganglion. Other common associated
findings include postural hypotension, dysphagia, and chronic cough [127 , 155 , 156 , 180].
Nonmotor Manifestations
The role of the cerebellum in the modulation of complex nonmotor behaviors, including various aspects of cognition, learning,
memory, and linguistic functioning, are being unraveled [41 , 42 , 44 , 66 , 75 , 86 , 96 , 97 , 100 , 103 , 132 , 143 , 169 , 176 , 177]. Accumulating
evidence also supports the idea that the cerebellum may be altered by a wide range of psychiatric disorders, including schizophrenia,
bipolar disease, as well as addictive behaviors [14 , 167]. Moreover, a growing body of data also gives credence to the contribution of
the cerebellum to the modulation of a variety of behavioral processes such as cognitive and affective functions [150], dyslexia [113],
schizophrenia [ 11], and autism spectrum disorder [3 , 55 , 144]. Subjects with dyslexia have been found to have MRI voxel differences
on the right cerebellar declive and right lentiform nucleus [118]. In autism spectrum disorder, postmortem histopathologic studies
have shown marked cerebellar vermis hypoplasia and variable loss of Purkinje cells primarily in the posterolateral neocerebellar
cortex and adjacent archicerebellum. Abnormalities have also been found in the fastigial and interposed nuclei, whereas the anterior
lobe of the cerebellum and vermis have been spared [18 , 37 , 100]. Bipolar disease has also been noted in 3 of 15 subjects after focal


<!-- PDF page 659 -->

cerebellar lesions; lesions included right cerebellar hypoplasia, bilateral cerebellar atrophy, and left midbrain pathology [ 89]. A
cerebellar cognitive affective syndrome, characterized by executive dysfunction, personality changes associated with blunted affect
or disinhibited and inappropriate behavior, visuospatial disorganization, impaired visual-spatial memory, mild anomia,

### [Page 470 begins | PDF 659]

agrammatism, and dysprosody, has been reported [138]. This neurobehavioral presentation has been more evident
among those patients with pancerebellar disease and among those with acute onset of cerebellar dysfunction. Lesions of the
posterior cerebellar lobe were particularly responsible for the disturbed cognitive behaviors, whereas the cerebellar vermis was more
consistently involved among patients with a more pronounced affective symptomatology. The anterior lobe of the cerebellum has
not been implicated to the same extent in the generation of these cognitive/behavioral manifestations [135 , 137 , 138]. A disruption of
the cerebellar modulation of neural circuits linking prefrontal, posterior parietal, superior temporal, and limbic cortices with the
cerebellum has been proposed as a plausible mechanism for this syndrome. More recently, lesions in the cerebro-ponto-cerebellar
pathways have been proposed as another plausible anatomic site for the occurrence of pathologic laughter and crying [117].
Furthermore, contralateral cerebellar hypometabolism has been noted among aphasic patients with left cerebral infarctions or
hemorrhages [72]. Macrographia and spatial dysgraphia, with omission and repetition of strokes and letters, has been described in a
patient with marked cerebellar atrophy [147].
Cerebellar Syndromes
The exquisite high-resolution capabilities of structural MRI studies has fueled renewed interest in human cerebellar lesion studies [
165]. In general, disorders mainly involving the midline cerebellum affect the truncal musculature and equilibrium. In contrast,
disorders affecting primarily the cerebellar hemispheres are associated with ipsilateral impairment of voluntary movements of the
fingers and legs as their most salient deficits. Cerebellar syndromes are divided as follows:
1. Rostral vermis syndrome (anterior lobe)
2. Caudal vermis syndrome (flocculonodular and posterior lobe)
3. Hemispheric syndrome (posterior lobe, variably anterior lobe)
4. Pancerebellar syndrome (all lobes)
Rostral Vermis Syndrome
Clinical features include:
1. Wide-based stance and titubating gait
2. Gait ataxia, with proportionally minimal ataxia on heel-to-shin maneuver with the patient supine
3. Normal or only slightly impaired coordination of the upper extremities
4. Infrequent hypotonia, nystagmus, or dysarthria
The rostral vermis syndrome is best exemplified by the restricted form of alcoholic cerebellar cortical degeneration [174]. Affected
patients have selective atrophy of the anterior and superior vermis, particularly the superficial folia, with lesser involvement of the
caudal vermis, anterior parts of lateral lobes, flocculus, and paraflocculus. Morphometric studies have further demonstrated marked
Purkinje cell loss, especially in lobules I to IV, IX, and X of the vermis [29]. The following temporal sequence has been observed:
anterior superior vermis, anterior superior hemisphere, anterior inferior hemisphere, and, finally, anterior inferior vermis [181].
Patients present with gait incoordination with little involvement of the upper limbs, speech, or ocular motility.
Caudal Vermis Syndrome
Clinical characteristics include:
1. Axial disequilibrium and staggering gait
2. Mild or no limb ataxia
3. Occasional spontaneous nystagmus and rotated head postures
This caudal vermis syndrome is typically observed with flocculonodular lobe damage, especially with medulloblastomas in children.
As these tumors grow, a hemispheric cerebellar syndrome may be superimposed. Surgical transection of the posterior inferior


<!-- PDF page 660 -->

cerebellar vermis may result in marked impairment of tandem gait with minimal impairment of regular gait, standing, and hopping
on one foot. Visually guided limb movements and speech remain unaffected. Surgical disruption of the parallel fibers crossing the
midline cerebellar cortex may be a critical variable accounting for the clinical features known as the posterior vermal split syndrome
[ 17].
Hemispheric Syndrome
Patients with this syndrome typically show incoordination of ipsilateral appendicular movements, particularly when the tasks require
fine motor coordination. Thus, the manifestations affect mainly muscles closely controlled by the precentral cortex, such as those
involved in speech and finger movements. Most likely etiologies of cerebellar hemispheric syndromes include cerebellar strokes,
neoplasms, or abscesses [62].
Pancerebellar Syndrome
This syndrome, a combination of all other cerebellar syndromes, results in bilateral signs of cerebellar dysfunction affecting the
trunk, limbs, and cranial musculature. A pancerebellar syndrome is observed with a number of infectious and parainfectious
processes, hypoglycemia, hyperthermia, paraneoplastic cerebellar degeneration associated with small cell lung cancer (anti-Hu
antibodies, anti-Zic 4 antibodies, anti-VGCC antibodies), breast and gynecologic (ovaries, uterus) carcinomas (anti-Yo antibodies,
anti-Ri

antibodies), and lymphomas, particularly Hodgkin disease (mGluR1 antibodies, anti-Tr antibodies),
and other toxic-metabolic disorders [ 40 , 175].
For the purpose of clinical localization, the cerebellum can be conceptualized as a sagittally oriented anatomic structure containing
three zones: midline, intermediate, and lateral [61 , 62]. The midline zone encompasses the anterior and posterior parts of the vermis,
the fastigial nucleus, and the associated input and output projections. Many of these projections are concerned with posture,
locomotion, head position in relation to the trunk, and control of extraocular movements. Thus, cerebellar signs resulting from
midline cerebellar disease are characterized by disorders of stance and gait, truncal titubation, rotated postures of the head, and
disturbances of eye movements. The intermediate zone includes the paravermal region of the cerebellar cortex and the interposed
nuclei on each side. This region is involved in the modulation of movements, velocity, force, and pattern of muscle activity, but
clinical disorders related to damage of this zone have not been clearly delineated. The lateral zone consists of the cerebellar
hemisphere and the dentate nucleus of each side. Neural units in this zone are involved in the planning of movements in connection
with neurons in the precentral region of the cerebral cortex. Thus, lesions of the lateral zone result in hypotonia, dysarthria,
dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, excessive rebound, impaired check response, kinetic and static tremors, decomposition of
movements, past-pointing, and eye movement abnormalities [ 61 , 62].
Syndromes of Cerebellar Infarction
Cerebellar infarctions result from thrombotic or embolic occlusion of a cerebellar vessel. Clinical manifestations and resulting
deficits reflect the specific vessel involved and the compromise of the collateral circulation. Main symptoms include headache,
vertigo, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, unsteady gait, limb clumsiness, dysarthria, and decreased level of alertness. Diagnosis relies on
detailed attention to eye movements, coordination, and gait [51]. Most prominent signs include limb and gait ataxia, dysarthria,
nystagmus, ocular tilt reaction, and altered mental status [13]. Motor symptoms may be minimal or completely lacking, particularly
among patients with infarctions involving lobule VI of the cerebellum [136]. According to their topographic distribution, four types
of cerebellar infarction have been recognized corresponding to the arterial territories of the PICA (40%), AICA (5%), the SCA
(35%), or the cortical watershed and deep cerebellar white matter border zone regions (20%) [16 , 82 , 166]. Two distinct clinical
syndromes can be recognized: space-occupying cerebellar infarctions with fourth ventricular and brainstem compression and
cerebellar infarctions without fourth ventricular or brainstem compression.
Space-occupying cerebellar infarcts likely to cause brainstem compression may present with sudden onset of occipital headache,
vertigo, nausea, vomiting, gait unsteadiness, and dysarthria [4]. Patients often display gait and truncal ataxia, ipsilateral axial
lateropulsion, or both, which usually prevent them from standing upright [ 4]. They may also have nystagmus, ipsilateral limb
dysmetria, dysarthria, and impaired consciousness [162]. The edematous cerebellum may compress the aqueduct of Sylvius or the
fourth ventricle, causing acute obstructive hydrocephalus, or may compress the brainstem, resulting in increasing headaches,
decreased level of alertness, and occasionally a head tilt [4 , 94].
Two distinct syndromes of cerebellar tissue herniation have been recognized: descending or tonsillar herniation syndrome and
upward or transtentorial herniation. With a cerebellar pressure cone, there is downward displacement of the cerebellar tonsils
through the foramen magnum, causing compression of the medulla oblongata. Clinical manifestations of tonsillar herniation include
neck stiffness, bilateral shoulder paresthesias, opisthotonus, and cardiac and respiratory rhythm disturbances, leading to apnea and
possible death. With upward transtentorial herniation, there is rostral displacement of the superior aspect of the cerebellar


<!-- PDF page 661 -->

hemisphere through the free edge of the tentorial incisura, resulting in midbrain compression. Clinical manifestations include
lethargy or coma, paralysis or upward gaze, midposition and unreactive pupils, and abnormal extensor posturing.
Large cerebellar infarctions most often involve the territory of the PICA, the territory of the SCA, or both [4 , 7]. Impaired level of
alertness may occur hours to days after the onset of symptoms, either in isolation or in association with worsening of other
neurologic signs [4 , 7]. Emergency surgery is often required.
The other end of the clinical spectrum includes very small (border zone) cerebellar infarctions [8 , 71]. These small cerebellar
infarctions have been associated with global hypoperfusion following cardiac arrest, small vessel or end (pial) artery disease due to
intracranial atheroma or hypercoagulable states, focal cerebellar hypoperfusion due to large artery vertebrobasilar steno-occlusive
disease or embolism. In many patients, the mechanism of infarction remains unknown. Physical findings were either lacking or
included a wide-based gait, lateropulsion, discrete ipsilateral dysmetria, dysarthria, and dysdiadochokinesia [8]. Small or border
zone cerebellar infarctions may simulate acute vestibular neuritis, including canal paresis [82].
Acute onset of isolated hemiataxia is, in most cases, due to an ipsilateral cerebellar stroke. However,

a
contralateral supratentorial stroke (e.g., thalamic stroke extending into the posterior limb of the internal capsule or stroke restricted
to the posterior limb of the internal capsule) may also result in isolated hemiataxia due to interruption of the cerebellar pathways at
the level of the internal capsule [99].
Inferior Cerebellar Infarction (Posterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery)
Most symptomatic cerebellar infarctions involve the territory of the PICA. Infarctions resulting from PICA occlusion are further
described in Chapter 22. Most often, infarctions in this arterial territory result from occlusion of the intracranial vertebral artery
before the PICA origin. PICA or medial PICA territory infarctions cause acute onset vertigo and truncal ataxia, whereas lateral
PICA territory infarctions result in unsteadiness, limb ataxia, and dysmetria without associated dysarthria. Unilateral limb ataxia
without dysarthria or vestibular manifestations should suggest an infarction isolated to the territory of the lateral branch of PICA [15
]. Bilateral cerebellar infarctions in the territory of medial branches of PICA may result in vertigo, dysarthria, disequilibrium,
bilateral gaze-evoked nystagmus, and gait ataxia without brainstem signs [157]. Vertigo and “upside-down” vision may also result
from infarction of the cerebellar flocculus and nodulus due to involvement of the medial branch of PICA [31]. Seldom, patients may
develop failure of automatic breathing when sleeping (Ondine's curse) [98]. Patients may also present with isolated vertigo
resembling acute vestibular neuritis [ 91]. Cardiac embolism, atherothrombotic occlusions, and vertebral artery dissection are the
main causes of large PICA territory infarctions [16].
Ventral Cerebellar Infarction (Anterior Inferior Cerebellar Artery)
Infarctions in the distribution of the AICA territory involve the ventrolateral pons and the anterolateral cerebellum, particularly the
middle cerebellar peduncle, flocculus, and the anterior part of the cerebellar lobules, with the exception of the lobulus anterior [4].
Due to common anatomic variations, its territory is reciprocal in size with the territories of the PICA and SCA. Not uncommonly,
AICA infarctions spare the cerebellum. Clinical manifestations of an AICA infarction often consist of the following [4 , 5 , 6 , 9 , 115 , 154
, 166]:
1. Prominent vertigo, nausea, vomiting, and nystagmus (due to involvement of the vestibular nuclei).
2. Ipsilateral facial hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia and corneal hypesthesia (due to involvement of the trigeminal spinal
nucleus and tract). Facial sensation is spared at times due to the presence of an independent vessel arising from the basilar
artery supplying the spinal tract and nucleus of the trigeminal nerve.
3. Ipsilateral Horner syndrome (due to interruption of the descending pupillodilator oculosympathetic fibers in the lateral
portion of the pons and medulla). Occasional skew deviation [5].
4. Contralateral trunk and extremity hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia (due to involvement of the lateral spinothalamic tract).
5. Ipsilateral ataxia and asynergia (due to involvement of the middle cerebellar peduncle and cerebellum).
6. Ipsilateral deafness and facial paralysis (due to involvement of the lateral pontomedullary tegmentum).
The AICA syndrome may at times be mistakenly attributed to a lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome because of shared signs
such as dysmetria, vestibular signs, Horner syndrome, and facial sensory impairment with contralateral trunk and extremity pain,
and temperature sensory loss [5]. However, facial palsy and deafness due to involvement of cranial nerves VII and VIII or their
nuclei, lateral gaze palsy, and multimodal sensory impairment of the face should suggest an AICA syndrome [5].

Infarctions in the AICA territory result in a combined loss of both auditory and vestibular functions [92]. Moreover, a labyrinthine
infarction may be a precursor of impending ponto-cerebellar infarctions in the AICA territory [83]. AICA territory infarctions may
also result in isolated vestibular manifestations or isolated cerebellar signs [119 , 130]. Two patients have been described with clinical
features of infarction in the distribution of the AICA who had isolated vertigo for several months before infarction [115]. Both
patients had cerebrovascular risk factors and had experienced other episodes of transient neurologic symptoms not associated with
vertigo. At the time of infarction, they developed vertigo, unilateral hearing loss, tinnitus, facial numbness, and hemiataxia. MRI
demonstrated ischemic lesions in the lateral aspect of the pons and middle cerebellar peduncle. Because the blood supply to the
inner ear and vestibulocochlear nerve arises from the AICA, the vertigo preceding the infarction may have resulted from transient
ischemia to the inner ear structures or the vestibular nerve [115].
Dorsal Cerebellar Infarct (Superior Cerebellar Artery)
SCA infarctions predominantly involve the cerebellum, sparing the brainstem. When the SCA is occluded at its origin, patients may
have ipsilateral

### [Page 473 begins | PDF 662]

cerebellar dysfunction, brainstem signs, and a contralateral dissociated sensory syndrome.
Conversely, when there is an occlusion of peripheral branches of the SCA, patients often present with isolated dysarthria and
unsteady gait but without vertigo, brainstem signs, or contralateral dissociated sensory disturbances [161]. The “classic” presentation
of SCA territory infarction, as described by Mills, is seldom observed [ 4 , 7 , 32 , 80 , 104 , 105 , 148 , 152 , 166]. The following may be found
on examination:
1. Vertigo and vomiting (due to involvement of the vestibular nuclei and connections)
2. Nystagmus (due to involvement of the medial longitudinal fasciculus and cerebellar pathways)
3. Ipsilateral Horner syndrome (due to interruption of the descending oculosympathetic fibers)
4. Ipsilateral ataxia and asynergia (due to involvement of the superior cerebellar peduncle and cerebellum)
5. Ipsilateral intention tremor (due to involvement of the dentate nucleus and superior cerebellar peduncle)
6. Contralateral trunk and extremity hypalgesia and thermoanesthesia (due to involvement of the lateral spinothalamic and
quintothalamic tracts)
7. Contralateral hearing impairment (due to involvement of the crossed fibers of the lateral lemniscus)
8. Contralateral fourth nerve palsy (due to involvement of the tectum)
SCA distribution infarctions may cause contrapulsion of saccadic eye movements associated with ipsilateral limb ataxia [ 123]. The
saccadic disorder has three elements, including horizontal saccades away from the lesion during attempted vertical saccades,
resulting in oblique trajectories; hypermetria of contralateral saccades; and hypometria of ipsilateral saccades. This contrapulsion of
saccades (lateropulsion contralateral to a cerebellar lesion) differs from the lateropulsion of saccades observed in the lateral
medullary syndrome of Wallenberg, which are directed to the side of the lesion and associated with hypermetria of saccades toward
the brainstem lesion and hypometria of saccades away from the lesion (see Chapter 15). Contrapulsion of saccades has also been
described in association with primary-position upbeat nystagmus in cases of hemispheric cerebellar lesions [20].
A study of 33 patients with SCA territory infarction [7] showed frequent association with infarctions in the distribution of the distal
basilar artery, including the rostral brainstem, thalamo-subthalamic region, and occipitotemporal lobes. Patients presented with
altered level of consciousness, ocular motor abnormalities, signs of thalamic dysfunction, visual field defects, cortical blindness,
and/or memory loss [4]. One-third also had infarctions in the distribution of the PICA, sometimes associated with infarctions in the
distribution of the AICA. Clinical presentations included a rostral basilar artery syndrome; coma at onset, often with tetraplegia; or
cerebellar-vestibular signs, often with delayed coma due to cerebellar swelling. However, a “classic” SCA syndrome was unusual [5
, 7].
Infarctions in the distribution of the lateral branch of the SCA (anterior rostral cerebellar infarct) may present with ipsilateral
dysmetria and axial lateropulsion, contrapulsion of saccades, dysarthria, unsteadiness, and vomiting [10 , 123]. A dysarthria-clumsy
hand syndrome may also be the presenting feature [10]. Patients with anterior rostral cerebellar infarction usually improve without
having major sequelae. Dysarthria is frequently observed with involvement of the territory of the medial branch of the SCA [5 , 90].
Selective involvement of the anterior cerebellar lobe in cases of medial SCA territory infarction may cause mild appendicular
dysmetria and spontaneous extensor posturing of the neck, trunk, and all four extremities [126].
Patients with infarctions in the territories of the PICA or the SCA were studied to compare their clinical presentations, course, and
prognosis [79]. In 36 patients with PICA territory infarctions, a triad of vertigo, headache, and gait imbalance predominated at stroke


<!-- PDF page 663 -->

onset. Head computed tomography showed signs of severe cerebellar mass effect (30%) with associated hydrocephalus and
brainstem compression, resulting in four deaths. In 30 patients with SCA infarctions, gait disturbances predominated at onset;
vertigo and headache were significantly less common. Isolated vertigo resembling vestibular neuritis is seldom seen with infarctions
in the SCA territory [91]. The clinical course was usually benign, and head computed tomography showed signs of cerebellar mass
effect, hydrocephalus, and brainstem compression in only 7% of the cases. Presumed cardiac embolism or artery-to-artery embolism
was the predominant stroke mechanism in these patients with SCA distribution infarctions, whereas in those with PICA territory
infarctions, the stroke mechanism was equally divided among those with cardiac sources of embolism and those with posterior
circulation steno-occlusive atherothrombotic disease. Thus, cerebellar infarctions in the PICA and SCA distribution have distinctive
characteristics in clinical presentation, course, and prognosis [79].
