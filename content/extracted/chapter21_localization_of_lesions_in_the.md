CHAPTER 21
Localization of Lesions in the Autonomic Nervous System
Organization of the Autonomic Nervous System
The autonomic nervous system (ANS) plays a crucial role in the maintenance of homeostasis. This chapter focuses on general
anatomic principles that should assist the readers in determining whether involvement of the ANS is present in a given patient. For
more detailed information, readers are referred to appropriate chapters of this book and standard neuroanatomy textbooks (Fig. 21-1
) [40 , 65 , 108 , 128 , 146].
The ANS consists of three anatomically distinct divisions: the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), the parasympathetic nervous
system (PNS), and the enteric nervous system (ENS). Activation of the SNS leads to the “fight or flight” response, whereas
activation of the PSN promotes the “rest and digest” processes. The ENS, characterized by an extensive weblike network of nervous
and glial cells, is primarily responsible for digestive (gastrointestinal) regulation [56 , 104].
The sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions have two-neuron series between the central nervous system (CNS) and the effector
organs. The ANS is bidirectionally connected with a central autonomic network that includes the medial prefrontal and insular
cortices, the central nucleus of the amygdala, the hypothalamus, periaqueductal gray, parabrachial/Kölliker-Fuse nuclear complex,
nucleus ambiguus, and nucleus tractus solitarius. This highly interconnected network controls sympathetic and parasympathetic,
neuroendocrine, and respiratory motor neurons [16 , 96 , 132].
The ANS carries afferent (sensory) and efferent (motor) fibers. Basic morphologic and physiologic differences between the SNS and
the PNS are illustrated in Table 21-1. Afferent signals that drive autonomic activity include visceral sensory, somatic sensory, and
special sensory fibers. The perikarya of visceral afferent axons are situated in the dorsal root ganglia.
A two-neuron pathway conveys motor impulses to the autonomic effectors. The perikaryon of first visceral efferent neurons reside
within the CNS (brainstem or spinal cord); preganglionic axons synapse on a ganglion or plexus of neurons. The perikaryon of the
second visceral efferent neuron is localized outside the CNS in a ganglion or plexus of neurons; postganglionic axons synapse on an
autonomic effector.
Sympathetic Nervous System
Most sympathetic preganglionic neurons (primary efferent neurons) have their cell bodies in the intermediolateral gray cell column
(lamina VII or intermediate zone) or lateral horns of the spinal cord (T1-L2 levels). Rostrally placed neurons innervate head and
neck structures, whereas neurons caudally located innervate the heart, lungs, and abdominal and pelvic viscera. Their axons reach a
postganglionic (secondary efferent) neuron with cell bodies situated in one of 22 to 23 pairs of paravertebral sympathetic ganglia (3
cervical, 10 to 12 thoracic, 4 lumbar, 4 to 5 sacral) on both sides of the vertebral column or in related prevertebral sympathetic
ganglia (celiac and superior and inferior mesenteric). Preganglionic fibers reach ganglionic neurons via exiting ventral spinal nerve
roots and myelinated white rami communicantes (14 pairs). Once preganglionic fibers reach the chain ganglia, they synapse with the
first encountered paravertebral ganglion or ascend or descend to reach a superior or inferior paravertebral ganglion or bypass the
paravertebral ganglia, joining other preganglionic fibers to reach one of the prevertebral ganglia.
The cervical paravertebral ganglia fuse to form the superior, middle, and inferior cervical ganglion. The inferior cervical ganglion
may fuse with the first thoracic ganglion to constitute the stellate ganglion. At the level of the coccygeal region, the caudal-most
ganglia fuse to form the ganglion impar (terminal ganglion of the sympathetic chain), also known as ganglion of Walther.
From a paravertebral ganglion, postganglionic fibers are distributed as branches to the spinal nerves through the gray rami
communicantes (31 pairs), or cranial nerves (IX, X, XII), or they course along blood vessel walls or reach directly their destination
in various organs, including the heart, blood vessels, bronchopulmonary system, sweat glands, smooth muscles of the gut and
bladder, and sexual organs.

Parasympathetic Nervous System
The PNS has a cranial and sacral subdivisions. Parasympathetic cranial preganglionic neurons are located within the following
cranial nerve nuclei: CN III (Edinger-Westphal nucleus in the rostral midbrain tegmentum); CN VII (superior salivatory and
lacrimal nuclei in the pontine tegmentum); CN IX (inferior salivatory nucleus in the rostral medulla); and CN X (nucleus ambiguus,
nucleus solitarius, and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus in the medulla). Most parasympathetic fibers are carried by the vagus nerve
(CN X). Myelinated cranial outflow

The sacral parasympathetic division is localized in the sacral intermediolateral cell columns of the second to fourth segments (S2-
S4) of the spinal cord. Myelinated axons exit via ventral nerve roots to form the sacral plexus and then reach the pelvic splanchnic
nerves, also known as nervi erigentes, which form the parasympathetic portion of the ANS in the pelvis. Myelinated efferent fibers

aorta. The sacral parasympathetic outflow innervates the descending colon, rectum, lower portion of the ureters, bladder, and
external genitalia [65 , 108 , 129]. The genital organs are innervated by the sacral parasympathetic (pelvic nerves or nervi erigentes),
thoracolumbar sympathetic (hypogastric and lumbar sympathetic chain), and sacral somatic nerves (pudendal nerves). The pelvic

Enteric Nervous System

Central Autonomic Network
Tightly interconnected neuroanatomic structures contribute to regulation of the autonomic circuits. Both the insula and the
infralimbic cortices and other widely distributed areas throughout the neuraxis are involved in cardiovascular regulation [28 , 107].
Medial Prefrontal Cortex
The medial prefrontal cortex is highly activated by stress and plays a key role in autonomic and affective responses. The medial
prefrontal cortex has been implicated in complex cognitive and emotional states and modulation of neuroendocrine and autonomic
function [8 , 67 , 130 , 131]. In addition, the anterior cingulate cortex has been implicated in modulating SNS tone [ 36 , 94].
Insular Cortex
The insular cortex plays an important role in the central autonomic network and is the most important cortical area involved in


<!-- PDF page 907 -->

cardiovascular regulation. Insular cortex neurons connect with the limbic system and lateral frontal cortical system and send
projections to the central nucleus of the amygdala and autonomic nuclei of the medulla oblongata [120]. The insula also has vast
connections with temporal and parietal cortices, basal ganglia, thalamus, and olfactory cortex. Moreover, the anterior insula has
connections to primary and supplementary motor cortices, ventral posterior medial nucleus of the thalamus, and nucleus tractus
solitarius, all of which are relevant to oropharyngeal swallowing [38]. A possible cortical lateralization in the regulation of
cardiovascular functions has been proposed. Right insular cortex lesions are more susceptible to being associated with cardio-
autonomic dysfunction, whereas left insular cortex lesions are predominantly associated with parasympathetic cardiovascular effects
[23 , 35 , 74 , 96 , 106 , 132 , 133]. Insular cortex strokes have been associated with arterial hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, increased risk
of myocardial injury, raised catecholamines levels, and increased susceptibility to sudden death [7 , 12 , 26 , 33 , 52 , 134].
Central Nucleus of the Amygdala
The amygdala plays a key role in emotional learning and in regulating arousal and vigilance and responds to the acquisition and
expression of conditioned fear. Fear is measured by changes in autonomic activity, including increased heart rate and increased
blood pressure. The amygdala is activated by diverse stimuli such as a fearful face or the expression of feelings of fear or aggression
[30 , 64 , 144 , 145]. Moreover, the central nucleus of the amygdala, which has direct connectivity to the hypothalamus, is involved in
cardiovascular regulation [119].
Hypothalamus
Anatomically, the hypothalamus represents the highest level of integration of autonomic function. The hypothalamus controls the
ANS by means of the pituitary gland and by direct descending pathways. Connections to the pituitary enable the hypothalamus to
influence activities of the endocrine glands. Descending hypothalamic sympathetic fibers are uncrossed and, by way of the lateral
brainstem tegmentum and

### [Page 645 begins | PDF 907]

lateral medullary formation, influence the craniosacral parasympathetic and the
thoracolumbar sympathetic outflows. In particular, the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus has emerged as one of the most
important centers involved in cardiovascular regulation. This nucleus receives afferents from the insula, prefrontal cortex, amygdala,
and other hypothalamic nuclei [128]. The lateral hypothalamus, in addition to the insula and the amygdala, also plays a key role in
cardiac autonomic control.
Periaqueductal Gray Region
The periaqueductal gray, also known as the central gray, consists of four regions, namely, the dorsomedial, dorsolateral, lateral, and
ventrolateral subdivisions. Chemical stimulation of the dorsolateral gray results in hypertension and tachycardia, whereas activation
of the ventrolateral gray results in the opposite effects. Different sets of neurons situated in different regions of the periaqueductal
gray have been implicated in coordinated patterns of somatic and autonomic changes characteristic of defensive responses [14 , 24 , 47 ,
48 , 138]. A major function of the periaqueductal gray is pain processing and modulation, primarily in descending pain inhibition [ 14 ,
24 , 47 , 48 , 138].
Parabrachial Nuclear Complex
The parabrachial/Kölliker-Fuse nuclear complex located in the dorsolateral pontine tegmentum consists of three main neuronal
groups: the medial parabrachial nucleus (corresponding to the classic “pneumotaxic center”), the lateral parabrachial nucleus, and
the Kölliker-Fuse nucleus. This nuclear complex plays a key role in the sensory processing of gustatory and visceral information,
pain modulation, and automatic control of respiration [15 , 58 , 60 , 84 , 148]. Moreover, vestibular input projecting to the nucleus tractus
solitarius, dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, and the parabrachial/Kölliker-Fuse nuclear complex may account for the autonomic
effects observed in cases of acute vestibular dysfunction [10 , 11].

Nucleus Ambiguus
The nucleus ambiguus, a group of large motor neurons situated in the ventrolateral medullary tegmentum, extends rostrally to the
dorsal aspect of the facial nucleus and caudally to the level of the pyramidal decussation. Together with the nucleus tractus
solitarius, and the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus, the nucleus ambiguus participates in the control of automatic respiration [58].
Involvement of the nucleus ambiguus in the rostral medulla oblongata is more likely to result in dysphagia compared with lesions
involving the middle-lower segment of the medulla oblongata [73 , 76].
Nucleus Tractus Solitarius
The nucleus tractus solitarius, a series of purely sensory nuclei, in the dorsal medulla, contains medullary respiratory and lower
brainstem hypnogenic neurons. It receives afferents from the cardiovascular and respiratory systems for autonomic control of
cardiac rhythm, circulation, and respiration and is believed to be of major importance in cardiovascular, respiratory, and GI system
regulation. The nucleus tractus solitarius also mediates the cough reflex, the gag reflex, the carotid sinus reflex, and the vomiting
reflex. Bilateral lesions of the nucleus tractus solitarius may result in acute neurogenic hypertension [41 , 101].
Localization Principles
Autonomic dysfunction may be focal or generalized; acute, subacute, or chronic; primary or secondary; and usually associated with
either underactivity or failure (Table 21-4). Severe paroxysmal sympathetic

### [Page 646 begins | PDF 908]

overactivity, including
neurogenic pulmonary edema, may result from acute brain or spinal cord injuries [9 , 17 , 124].
Examples of focal dysautonomias include Adie tonic pupil and Horner syndrome described in Chapter 8. Crocodile tears or Bogorad
syndrome (gustato-lacrimation) refers to an unusual complication of a recovering Bell palsy, characterized by profuse lacrimation
during eating or drinking. It is caused by a misdirection of regenerating autonomic fibers to the lacrimal gland instead of the
submandibular gland [102]. Gustatory sweating (Frey syndrome) is a rare complication of surgery in or near the parotid gland,
characterized by sweating and flashing of the temporal and retroauricular area after eating sour, spicy, or salty foods. It is thought to
result from simultaneous damage to sympathetic and parasympathetic nerves in or near the region of the parotid gland [43 , 105 , 111].
Primary hyperhidrosis, a common and troublesome condition, primarily involves the axillae as well as the palms and soles and is
characterized by excessive and uncontrolled sweat production [117]. Reduced tear secretion is observed in many patients with acute
pandysautonomias, multisystem atrophy (MSA)-related autonomic failure, and Riley-Day syndrome, where, in addition, there is
insensitivity to pain (Chapter 8). Finally, vasovagal syncope among the young and carotid sinus hypersensitivity among the elderly
are examples of localized dysautonomias.
Because various visceral functions are controlled by extrinsic pathways, involving neuronal circuits in the brain, hypothalamus,
brainstem, spinal cord, peripheral ganglia, or peripheral nerves, the following appear logical steps when attempting localization: (1)
Is there ANS dysfunction present? (2) Does the dysautonomia involve the SNS, PNS, or the ENS? (3) Is the autonomic disorder
expressed as autonomic failure or hyperactivity? (4) Is the ANS involvement central, preganglionic, ganglionic, postganglionic, or
localized to the neuroeffectors? (Table 21-5) [17 , 32].
Symptoms of dysautonomia are multiple and include cardiovascular symptoms, respiratory abnormalities, GI, urogenital,
sudomotor, and thermoregulatory disturbances, pupillary abnormalities, and sleep disorders.
ANS dysfunction may result in orthostatic intolerance, including orthostatic hypotension. Orthostatic hypotension is often
polyetiologic and defined as a sustained drop in the systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥20 mm Hg or the diastolic blood pressure (DBP)
of ≥10 mm Hg within 3 minutes of standing [17 , 55 , 93]. Orthostatic hypotension may be asymptomatic or manifest as
lightheadedness, blurred vision, neck pain (coat hanger distribution), or loss of consciousness. Symptoms are often worse in the


<!-- PDF page 909 -->

morning hours, following large carbohydrates or alcohol, or after heat exposure. Not uncommonly, orthostatic hypotension occurs
concomitantly with supine hypertension [62]. Several causes of orthostatic hypotension or worsening hypotension are
nonneurogenic and include drugs (vasodilators, diuretics, dopaminergic agents, sildenafil, chemotherapeutic agents), hypovolemia,
impaired venous return, and cardiac pump failure. Neurologic disorders likely to cause orthostatic hypotension include MSA, pure
autonomic failure (PAF), diabetic neuropathy, alcoholic polyneuropathy, amyloidosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), HIV/AIDS,
paraneoplastic neuropathies, B12 deficiency-associated neuropathies, spinal cord injury, transverse myelitis, syringomyelia,
baroreflex failure, and dopamine beta hydroxylase (DBH) deficiency caused by a mutation in the DBH gene and inherited in an
autosomal recessive manner [78 , 115]. Patients with autonomic failure due to DBH have normal parasympathetic and sympathetic
cholinergic function but lack sympathetic noradrenergic function [31 , 125]. Rarely, orthostatic hypertension, thought to involve
activation of the SNS, has been associated with diabetes mellitus and dysautonomias [51].
Autonomic dysfunction may complicate a number of CNS and PNS disorders. In many instances, the target organ is obvious, and
the clinical task is to first uncover the neuroanatomic pathways involved. Autonomic dysfunction is a prominent feature in acute
spinal cord injuries; acutely, autonomic disturbances on cardiovascular and respiratory regulation, and thermoregulation, may be life
threatening [2 , 3 , 75 , 103]. Low blood pressure, bradycardia, inability to lose excess heat by sweating, atonic bladder, and paralytic
ileus are prominent manifestations of acute spinal cord injury. Autonomic dysfunction is also a major feature of other neurologic
diseases such as Parkinson disease, PAF, MSA, dementia with Lewy bodies, and Wernicke encephalopathy [ 15 , 76 , 98]. Autoimmune
autonomic ganglionopathies, associated with high titers of ganglionic acetylcholine receptor antibody (g-AChR antibody), represent
examples of severe but potentially treatable antibody-mediated causes of autonomic failure [77 , 95 , 135]. Autonomic dysfunction such
as disorders of micturition, impotence, orthostatic intolerance, and sudomotor and GI disturbances often occurs in multiple sclerosis
[66 , 95]. Disturbances of autonomic function are also more common among patients with cluster headaches. Parasympathetic
overactivity in the greater superficial petrosal nerve, a branch of the nervus intermedius (nerve of Wrisberg), causes facial flushing
and lacrimation [ 42]. Hypothalamic activation occurs in the trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias [86]. In addition, loss of sympathetic
innervation to the ipsilateral parotid gland may account for the intriguing

Epileptic seizures may result in autonomic dysfunction, including convulsive apnea; hypersexuality and sexual arousal have been
linked to ictal seizure activity, and hyposexuality has been linked to interictal seizure activity [5 , 18 , 57 , 126 , 127]. Furthermore, many
patients with seizures have autonomic disorders of cardiac control, including sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP) [114 , 123
, 142]. Autonomic instability with wide-ranging fluctuations in body temperature, resulting in hyperthermia, sometimes alternating
with hypothermia, hypoventilation, blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations, constipation, and ileus, are often prominent features
of anti N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor encephalitis [37 , 53].
Most autonomic neuropathies occur in association with a somatic neuropathy, but they may present in isolation. Autonomic
neuropathies may result from immune-mediated, infectious, nutritional, toxic,

### [Page 648 begins | PDF 911]

drug-induced, paraneoplastic,
hereditary etiologies or may be classified as idiopathic. They may involve the SNS, PNS, or ENS [45]. Sensory abnormalities are
predominant features in hereditary sensory and autonomic neuropathies (HSAN) [137]. Widespread autonomic dysfunction is more
prominent in GBS, acute intermittent porphyria, amyloidosis, and diabetes mellitus [ 103 , 139 , 140]. In acute porphyric neuropathy, the
autonomic dysfunction is mainly the result of vagal insufficiency [112]. Typical autonomic manifestations of acute porphyric
neuropathy include abdominal pain (the commonest), severe vomiting, constipation and occasional diarrhea, inappropriate sinus
tachycardia, labile hypertension, postural hypertension, excessive sweating, and bladder dysfunction [83 , 97].
Autonomic dysfunction is also one of the most prominent manifestations of small-diameter lightly myelinated or unmyelinated fiber
neuropathies; sudomotor dysfunction is one of the earliest neurophysiologic abnormalities in these neuropathies [ 44 , 54 , 73 , 82].
Known causes of small fiber neuropathy include diabetes mellitus, amyloidosis, toxins, and a number of inherited sensory and
autonomic neuropathies. Acroparesthesias, impaired sweating, neuropathic pain, and GI dysmotility are frequent manifestations of
dysfunction of small autonomic fibers in Fabry disease, a rare X-linked inborn disorder of glycosphingolipid catabolism caused by a
deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme alpha-gal A (alpha-galactosidase). Peripheral nervous system involvement in Fabry disease
affects mainly the small A delta and C fibers [ 20 , 34 , 122].

Diabetes mellitus has a major impact in almost every segment of the GI tract, from the esophagus to the rectum, and is often
associated with nausea, vomiting, bloating, early satiety, abdominal pain, diarrhea, and/or constipation [88 , 147]. Numerous types of
neuropathies are associated with diabetes mellitus or diabetic treatments, including diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy,
autonomic and small fiber neuropathies, mononeuropathies, acute sensory painful sensory neuropathies, and lumbosacral
radiculoplexus neuropathy. Diabetic neuropathies involve both the somatic and the autonomic nerves. Symptoms of diabetic
neuropathy are often vague. GI dysmotility is frequent, and the role of the ENS has gained considerable importance in accounting
for many of these manifestations [29 , 147]. Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy is also among the most clinically relevant
complications of diabetes mellitus. Besides increasing morbidity and mortality, cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy may account
for exercise intolerance, cardiac arrhythmias, heart rate variability, and an increased rate of silent myocardial ischemia [39 , 72].
Ventricular arrhythmias due to relative sympathetic overactivity with early autonomic neuropathy may account for sudden nocturnal
death (“dead in bed”) in young people with type I diabetes mellitus [143]. Seldom, an acute painful autonomic neuropathy develops
shortly after initiation of intensive glycemic control [59].
Autonomic symptoms are also seen in uremic neuropathy, alcoholic neuropathy, familial dysautonomia, HIV infection, leprosy,
botulism, tetanus, and envenomation with jellyfish and other marine animals [1 , 21 , 69].
The important links between the brain and the digestive tract and the bidirectional influences between the gut microbiome and the
CNS [141] emphasize the influences of the gut microbiome in a variety of CNS/PNS and some neuropsychiatric disorders, including
multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder, GBS, autism spectrum disorder, depression, anxiety, and stress. Many
neurogenic and muscle conditions are also associated with gut motility disorders [49 , 70 , 110]. As discussed in other chapters, GI
manifestations are prominent in Parkinson disease (see Chapter 19), including swallowing problems, drooling, delays in gastric
emptying, and constipation [49]. Likewise, GI manifestations are common among patients with Down syndrome, neurofibromatosis
type 1, Riley-Day syndrome, enteric neuropathies (Hirschsprung disease, also known as Hirschsprung neurocristopathy or colorectal
aganglionosis, celiac disease, neuronal intestinal dysplasia, hypertrophic pyloric stenosis, mitochondriopathies), enteric myopathies
(muscular dystrophies, myotonic dystrophy, familial visceral myopathies), multiple endocrine neoplasias (MEN type 2B) [121],
collagenopathies (desmosis, connective tissue disorders), and the enteric neuropathies of Parkinson disease, prion disease, and
iatrogenic or drug-induced neuropathies (vinca alkaloids, cisplatin, etc.) [ 4 , 22 , 27 , 50 , 85 , 100 , 109 , 113 , 136]. Finally, chronic idiopathic
intestinal pseudo-obstruction has been reported in rare cases of a familial visceral neuropathy with discrete eosinophilic intranuclear
inclusions within neurons of the ENS. ANS abnormalities include intestinal dysmotility, peripheral neuropathy, and pupillary
abnormalities. Definitive pathologic diagnosis can be established by rectal biopsy [13 , 116].
