Basal Ganglia
Anatomy of the Basal Ganglia
The basal ganglia (Fig. 19-1) comprise a discrete set of deep nuclei located beneath the cerebral cortex. In the strictest anatomic
sense, the basal ganglia refer to the caudate, putamen, and globus pallidus (GP) in the cerebrum; the substantia nigra (SN) in the
cerebral peduncles of the midbrain; and the subthalamic nucleus (STN) in the diencephalon. The putamen and caudate nucleus is
called the striatum. The GP (also known as paleostriatum or dorsal pallidum) is divided by the medial medullary lamina of the white
matter in an external (GPE) and an internal (GPI) segment. The SN is divided into the pars compacta (SNpc) and the pars reticularis
or reticulata (SNpr). Other related nuclei include the nucleus accumbens, located between the caudate and putamen, and the
pedunculopontine nucleus, which has reciprocal connections with other parts of the basal ganglia.
The basal ganglia play a major role in the control of posture, motor planning, voluntary movements, eye movements, learning,
reward seeking, and cognitive and emotional behavior [ 185 , 300 , 301]. The basal ganglia are interconnected with the cerebral cortex,
thalamus, and brainstem. The main anatomic connections are complex and include several loops (Fig. 19-2). The striatum (putamen
and caudate nucleus) is the main input to the basal ganglia. Main outputs from the GPI and SNpr project to the thalamus and from
the thalamus to various cortical areas of the frontal lobe [11 , 62 , 347 , 364 , 380]. The main basal ganglia pathways include numerous
anatomic tracts: corticostriatal, striatopallidal, pallidothalamic, pallidosubthalamic, striatonigral, nigrostriatal, nigrotectal, and
nigrothalamic.
Inputs into the Striatum (Caudate and Putamen)
Cortical Projections to the Neostriatum
Various cortical areas project to the striatum. The frontal cortex projects to the head of the caudate nucleus and rostral putamen.
There are also cortical projections to the SN and STN. Corticostriatal projections are topographical. The ventral striatum, which
includes the nucleus accumbens, is associated with reward; the caudate nucleus with cognition; and the putamen with motor control
[185].
Thalamostriatal Projections
The intralaminar nuclei of the thalamus, in particular the centrum median nucleus, project to the striatum.
Nigrostriatal Projections
The dopamine-rich nigrostriatal pathway projects from cell bodies in the SNpc to terminals in the caudate nucleus, putamen, and
GP.
Raphe Nuclei-Striatal Projections
Efferents to the striatum arise from distinct populations of dorsal raphe nuclei [ 466].
Striatal Efferents
Most striatal efferents transit to both segments of the GP and the SN.
Pallidal Afferents and Efferents
Pallidal afferents arise from the SN and the STN. Both the GPI and the GPE receive striatal afferents. Most afferents end in the
striatum except for the STN, which ends in the GP.


<!-- PDF page 743 -->

The main pallidal outputs reach the thalamus and the SN. The main output arises from the GPI to the ventral anterior (VA) and
ventral lateral (VL) thalamic nuclei. These two thalamic nuclei also receive afferents from the SNpr. Due to specific thalamocortical
projections to the motor and premotor cortices, respectively, these pallidal efferents exert a major influence in motor activity. GPI
efferents also reach the centrum median nucleus of the thalamus, which in turn projects to the putamen. A loop is thus formed:
putamen-GPI-centrum median-putamen.
Efferents from the GPE transit to the GPI and to the STN. The STN, in turn, sends efferents to the GPI and GPE. Thus, another loop
is formed: GPE-STN-both GP.
Other pallidal efferents transit to the SN, red nucleus, inferior olive, hypothalamus, and mesencephalic reticular formation.
Nigral Afferents and Efferents

recognized.
They are the “direct” (excitatory) and the “indirect” (inhibitory) pathways. The “direct” and “indirect” pathways work in tandem.
The “direct pathway” (cortex-striatum-GPE-thalamus-motor cortex) promotes movement initiation. The SNpc excites the striatum
(nigrostriatal projections) to activate dopamine 1 (D1) striatal receptors. The “indirect pathway” (cortex-striatum-GPI-STN-GPE-
thalamus-cortex) limits motor function by terminating movements. The SNpc inhibits the striatum (nigrostriatal projections) to
activate dopamine 2 (D2) striatal receptors. A “hyperdirect pathway” bypassing the striatum and connecting the cortex directly to
the STN critical for the suppression of erroneous movements has also been proposed [ 221 , 303 , 398]. However, technically, there is no
separate “hyperactive pathway.” Instead, when the “direct pathway” is overactive, dystonia emerges; when the “indirect pathway” is
overactive (and the direct pathway is hypoactive), parkinsonism emerges.

Basal ganglia pathologic processes are often diffuse. When discrete, they often involve neighboring structures such as the internal
capsule, hypothalamus, and cerebral hemispheric white matter. Except for hemiballism, often due to damage of the contralateral
STN or neighboring structures, precise topographical correlation between basal ganglia structural damage and type of motor
dysfunction is tentative. However, in general, damage to the caudate, putamen, or GP results in inhibition of movements or
contralateral body turning [313].
Disorders associated with specific basal ganglia lesions result in the following:
1. STN lesions result in contralateral hemiballism [77].
2. Small unilateral lesions of the anterior segment of the caudate result in contralateral choreoathetosis [272].
3. Unilateral GP lesions cause contralateral hemidystonia, hemiparkinsonism, or tremor; bilateral lesions result in dystonia,
parkinsonism, abulia, or akinesia [336].
4. SN lesions result in parkinsonism.
5. Unilateral pallidal-putaminal strokes may cause a sudden and slow tendency to fall to the contralateral side, especially
when sitting, standing, or walking [256].
In an early study of the behavioral and motor manifestations of basal ganglia lesions, damage to the caudate was more likely to
result in abulia or disinhibition, rather than chorea or dystonia [49]. Lesions of the putamen and GP often resulted in dystonia,
particularly with putaminal

or dystonia-parkinsonism. Behavioral disorders were often associated with caudate lesions, whereas dystonia and, less commonly,

Movement disorder phenomenology results in an excess of movements (hyperkinesias, dyskinesias) or a paucity of voluntary and
automatic movements (akinesia, bradykinesia, hypokinesia). Thus, two main syndromatic categories of movement disorders are
distinguished: akinetic-rigid syndromes and hyperkinetic or dyskinetic syndromes [1].
The dyskinesias are discussed first.

Chorea is often associated with changes in muscle tone. Hypotonia is observed in Sydenham chorea; rigidity and hypokinesis in the
Westphal variant of Huntington disease; and dystonia in the juvenile variant of Huntington disease. There is hyperpronation of the
upper extremities when attempting to maintain an extended posture of the arms and inability to sustain a tight handgrip (milkmaid's
grip). The tongue cannot be kept protruded and darts in and out irregularly (trombone or flycatcher tongue). Facial grimacing and
abnormal respiratory sounds may be associated manifestations.
Huntington disease is an autosomal dominant progressive disorder caused by a single defective gene localized to the short arm on
chromosome 4. The mutant gene contains an expansion of CAG trinucleotide repeats that encodes the protein huntingtin. Age of
onset is in part determined by trinucleotide repeat length. The longer the trinucleotide repeat expansions (normal is 10 to 29 copies),
the earlier the disease onset. Huntington disease phenotype includes a spectrum of adventitious movements, disordered emotional
control and behavior, and a progressive and disabling subcortical dementia. Symptoms often begin insidiously around the third
through the fifth decades of life and are typically characterized by progressive chorea, dystonia, eye movement abnormalities,
mental inflexibility, irritability, personality changes, and progressive dementia. The choreic movements may precede or follow onset
of dementia. Saccadic eye movements become slow and uncoordinated. Smooth pursuit eye movements are frequently disrupted by
saccadic intrusions. The end stage of the disorder is characterized by extreme motor, cognitive, and behavioral disability. An
increased rate of suicide has been observed. Huntington disease beginning before age 21, also known as juvenile Huntington disease
, is most often paternally inherited and characterized by marked bradykinesia, rigidity, and dystonia. Neuroimaging and pathologic
studies demonstrate severe caudate atrophy.
Huntington disease-like 2 (HDL2) refers to a progressive autosomal dominant disease with marked clinical and pathologic
resemblance to Huntington disease [295 , 462]. The gene defect is a CAG/CTG expansion in a variably spliced exon of the
junctophilin-3 gene. HDL2 usually presents in the fourth decade of life with acanthocytosis, weight loss, and incoordination.
Patients display dystonia, chorea, rigidity, dysarthria, hyperreflexia, bradykinesia, tremors, psychiatric disturbances, and dementia.
Other autosomal dominant disorders associated with chorea (Huntington-like disorders) include benign hereditary chorea (BHC),
spinocerebellar ataxia 3 (SCA 3 or Machado-Joseph disease), spinocerebellar ataxia 17 (SCA17), dentatorubropallidoluysian
atrophy (DRPLA), frontotemporal dementia-motor neuron disease (FTD-MND), and idiopathic basal ganglia calcification [ 320].
BHC is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by childhood-onset chorea, lack of dementia, occasional levodopa response,
and little or no clinical progression [26 , 150 , 238].

Chorea-acanthocytosis (ChAc) refers to a familial or nonfamilial multisystem and progressive disorder resulting in chorea and star-
shaped red blood cells [33 , 53 , 191]. Mean age at onset is around the third decade of life. There is associated acanthocytosis with
normal serum lipoprotein levels. Early in the disease, patients present with lip and tongue biting followed by orolingual “eating
dystonia,” motor and phonic tics, generalized chorea, akinetic-rigid features, vertical ophthalmoparesis, and seizures [30]. The
dystonic orofacial-lingual movements and pseudobulbar features result in dysphagia and dysarthria. Sudden head drop and axial
extension have been posited as pathognomonic for ChAc, but some patients with Huntington disease may show similar findings as
well [335 , 404]. Cognitive impairment, psychiatric disturbances, and personality changes are common. Additional manifestations
include amyotrophy, axonal neuropathy, decreased or absent muscle stretch reflexes, and increased serum creatine kinase levels

without associated myopathy [191]. Chorea associated with neurogenic atrophy has also been reported in
cases of Fotopoulos syndrome [359].
Walker and colleagues reviewed clinical and laboratory features of NA [461]. NA was divided into three groups: the “core” NA
syndromes with basal ganglia neurodegeneration, comprising autosomal recessive ChAc due to mutations in the VPS13 A gene, and
X-linked McLeod syndrome (MLS) due to mutation of the XK gene on the X chromosome; conditions associated with decreased
serum lipoprotein levels, namely, abetalipoproteinemia (Bassen-Kornzweig syndrome) and hypo-betalipoproteinemia, characterized
by peripheral neuropathy and ataxia due to dorsal column degeneration, but without accompanying movement disorders; and
disorders where acanthocytosis is occasionally observed, such as PKAN and HDL2. In addition, acanthocytosis has been associated
with some mitochondrial diseases.
Chorea is the prototype involuntary movement disorder in ChAc, MLS, HDL2, and PKAN, but an array of other involuntary
movement disorders may be observed, including dystonia, parkinsonism, and “tourettism.” In “atypical” cases of PKAN, rigidity is
prominent. Severe orofacial dystonias with tongue and lip biting are characteristic of ChAc and less suggestive of MLS. Repetitive
tongue thrusting may result in serious feeding problems. Speech difficulties, specifically palilalia or dysarthria, are prominent
features of PKAN. Patients with ChAc, MLS, or HDL2 often have early neuropsychiatric symptoms, including bipolar disorder,
schizoaffective disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, depression, anxiety, and agitation. Cognitive problems may range from
minor abnormalities to dementia. Children with atypical PKAN may develop cognitive deterioration and various psychiatric
abnormalities. Seizures are common in patients with ChAc and MLS. In the French Canadian population with ChAc, partial
complex seizures of temporal lobe origin were the presenting clinical features several years before the onset of the involuntary
movements. Seizures are unusual in HDL2 or PKAN. Oculographic studies in ChAc show slow and hypometric saccades and
frequent square-wave jerks and oscillations, similar to those observed in Huntington disease. Most patients with typical PKAN
develop retinopathy. Various eye movement abnormalities have been documented, including saccadic pursuit, impaired saccades,
abnormal vestibulo-ocular reflex, and abnormal pupillary reactivity. Autonomic nervous system dysfunction may result in abnormal
respiratory rhythms, orthostatic hypotension, and impaired gastrointestinal motility. Autonomic dysfunction has also been reported
in MLS with hypersalivation and excessive sweating. Cardiomyopathy and cardiac arrhythmias are observed in approximately two-


<!-- PDF page 749 -->

thirds of MLS patients. Childhood onset (younger than 10 years) is observed in typical cases with the PANK2 mutation. Atypical
features and slower disease progression may be more common among young adults. ChAc is clinically evident between ages 20 to
40 and seldom under age 20 or after age 50. MLS typically develops in men aged 40 to 60 years. Exceptionally, female mutation
carriers manifest the disease. Age of onset of HDL2 is variable; as in HD, longer trinucleotide repeat expansions correlate with
younger age at onset.
Neuroferritinopathy refers to a dominantly inherited, progressive, potentially treatable movement disorder due to a mutation of the
ferritin light chain gene (FTL1) [90 , 106 , 328]. Serum ferritin levels are low. Brain histochemistry shows abnormal aggregates of
ferritin and iron. Symptom onset is around the third to the sixth decades of life. Patients present with chorea, dystonia, or an
akinetic-rigid syndrome. Clinical features overlap with other common extrapyramidal disorders such as idiopathic torsion dystonia,
PD, and Huntington disease. In a study of a genetically homogeneous group of 41 subjects with the 460 InsA mutation in FTL1,
mean age of onset was 39.4 years; beginning with chorea in 50%, focal lower limb dystonia in 42.5%, and parkinsonism in 7.5% of
patients [ 90]. Most patients had a family history of movement disorder often misdiagnosed as Huntington disease. The disease
progresses relentlessly, becoming generalized over a 5- to 10-year span, eventually leading to aphonia, dysphagia, and severe motor
disability with subcortical/frontal cognitive dysfunction. A characteristic action-specific facial dystonia was common, and in two-
thirds of patients there was asymmetry throughout the disease course. Serum ferritin levels were low in most men and
postmenopausal women, but values were normal among premenopausal women. Isolated parkinsonism is unusual in cases of
neuroferritinopathy. Unlike Huntington disease, cognitive changes are absent or rather subtle in the early stages of the disease [ 90].
DRPLA refers to an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by choreoathetosis, ataxia, dementia, seizures, myoclonus, and
dystonia [73 , 243 , 465 , 479]. DRPLA is particularly prevalent in Japan. A similar condition has been described in the southern United
States as “Haw River” syndrome [73]. Average age of onset is 54.3 ± 14.3 years, and disease duration is 7.9 ± 5.8 years [479]. Most
common initial symptoms include balance disturbances. Patients have a variety of movement disorders, including chorea. About
one-third have coexistent motor neuron disease. Some patients present with a combination of myoclonus, epilepsy, and intellectual
disability, whereas a late-onset subtype is characterized by choreoathetosis, cerebellar ataxia, dystonia, rest and postural tremors,
parkinsonism, and dementia. Pathologic studies

have shown degeneration of the dentate-rubral system; GP;
STN; and, to a lesser extent, the striatum; SN; inferior olive; and thalamus.
Familial dyskinesia and facial myokymia (FDFM) begins in early childhood or adolescence and is characterized by choreiform
movements associated with perioral and periorbital myokymia [151]. The involuntary movements may become constant by the third
decade of life. Thereafter, there is no further deterioration, and there may even be improvement with advanced age. Although FDFM
may be confused with Huntington disease, there is no intellectual impairment, and life span is normal.
Sydenham chorea (rheumatic chorea or St. Vitus dance), related to group A beta-hemolytic streptococcal infection, occurs in
childhood and adolescence. Sydenham chorea is predominantly or exclusively unilateral (hemichorea) and may result in apparent
flaccid paralysis (chorea mollis). Some patients may display “hung-up” patellar reflexes, which have been attributed to choreic
movements induced by the tendon tap [ 189].
HIV encephalitis, tuberculous meningitis, herpes simplex encephalitis, Whipple disease, and variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease
(vCJD) are rare causes of chorea [166 , 298 , 341 , 361]. Chorea may result from medication side effects (levodopa, neuroleptics,
metoclopramide, antiseizure medications, propofol, pemoline, corticosteroids, lithium, digoxin) and abuse of drugs such as cocaine-
induced choreoathetosis known as “crack dancing,” or amphetamines [115]. Chorea has been associated with a variety of systemic
disorders (systemic lupus erythematosus, antiphospholipid antibody syndrome, Henoch-Schönlein purpura, sarcoidosis, vasculitis,
multiple sclerosis, Behçet disease, hyperthyroidism, Hashimoto encephalopathy, hypoparathyroidism, acquired hepatocerebral
degeneration, renal failure, polycythemia, hypo or hypercalcemia, hypo or hypernatremia, hypo or hyperglycemia, mercury
poisoning, and carbon monoxide poisoning). Chorea with associated dementia may also result from paraneoplastic striatal
encephalitis [438]. Paraneoplastic chorea has been associated with CRMP-5 neuronal antibody and lung carcinoma [361 , 454].
A physiologic form of chorea may develop in some infants. Chorea has been associated with cardiac surgery (postpump chorea) in
children, particularly in cases with prolonged pump time, deep hypothermia, or circulatory arrest [317]. Choreoathetosis, orofacial
dyskinesia, hypotonia, and pseudobulbar palsy (CHAP syndrome) may occur following surgery for congenital heart disease [ 174]. A
steroid-responsive chorea has also been described following heart transplant [57]. Chorea has been reported in pregnancy (chorea
gravidarum) and with the use of oral contraceptives [305]. Congophilic angiopathy may cause chorea associated with progressive
dementia [48]. Chorea is the most common type of involuntary movement disorder following strokes [8].

The pathogenesis of choreiform movements remains essentially unknown. Evidence linking choreiform dyskinesias to caudate and
putaminal damage is by no means convincing, because most relevant disease processes (Huntington disease) involve multiple neural
structures. Rarely, dystonia and choreoathetosis result from cervical spinal cord lesions, such as ependymoma, glioma, myxoma,
demyelination, trauma, or disc prolapse [436].
Hemichorea is often observed with contralateral STN, thalamus, or caudate nucleus lesions. The choreic movements may spare the
face. Hemichorea has been associated with ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes, putaminal cavernous malformations [135 , 431],
thalamotomy, moyamoya disease, trauma, migraine, or neoplasms.

The most common tardive syndrome is tardive dyskinesia, which is characterized by repetitive, almost rhythmic movements
frequently involving the oral-lingual-buccal region. These dyskinesias result in complex chewing movements associated with
occasional popping out of the tongue and writhing movements of the tongue at rest [211]. At time, the hands, feet, and trunk develop
rhythmic movements; the abdominal and pelvic muscles may be affected, resulting in truncal or pelvic rocking or thrusting
movements. Respiratory dyskinesias result in involuntary chest and diaphragmatic movements [144]. Tardive dyskinesia is often
accompanied by a feeling of inner restlessness and inability to stay still, a condition known as akathisia[ 54 , 72]. Another common
manifestation is tardive dystonia, which may be associated with tardive akathisia or tardive dyskinesia [70 , 71 , 72 , 235 , 446]. In tardive
dystonia, the involuntary movements are more sustained and at times torsional in nature. Facial dystonia results in blepharospasm or
facial grimacing. Involvement of the mandible causes tonic jaw deviation, jaw protrusion, sustained opening and closing of the jaw,


<!-- PDF page 754 -->

or bruxism. Dystonic posturing of the neck in the form of retrocollis is common [228]. Tardive dystonia can occur at all ages,
whereas classic tardive dyskinesia is more common among the elderly. Less frequent tardive syndromes include myoclonus, tremor,
oculogyric crisis, and tics.

Orofacial dyskinesias[ 12] refer to abnormal involuntary facial, lips, and tongue movements that are either spontaneous, particularly
among elderly edentulous patients, or observed in patients with Huntington disease, Sydenham chorea, or Wilson disease. Their

as the likely pathophysiology. Unilateral striatonigral lesions may result in bilateral orofacial-lingual dyskinesias plus contralateral

Abdominal Dyskinesias
Abdominal dyskinesias are characterized by continuous movements of the abdominal wall or the diaphragm [207 , 248]. The
involuntary movements are rhythmic and often referred to as belly dancer's dyskinesia. Abdominal dyskinesias may follow
laparotomy. Some cases have associated abdominal myoclonus. Abdominal dyskinesias may also be part of the tardive syndrome
spectrum.
Ballism
Ballism[ 237 , 287 , 458] refers to an involuntary hyperkinesia often confined to one-half of the body (hemiballism), at times a single
extremity (monoballism) or, exceptionally, both halves of the body (paraballism or biballism). Hemiballism is characterized by
sudden, paroxysmal, large-amplitude, flinging, throwing movements of the arm and leg, traditionally attributed to a contralateral
lesion in the STN or its connections [287]. However, hemiballism often results from lesions outside the STN [373]. Hemiballism
persists during wakefulness, ceases during sleep, and is often associated with hypotonia of the involved limbs.
Hemiballism has been described with an ipsilateral STN infarction [108], as well as with caudate, putamen, GP, precentral gyrus, or
thalamic nuclei lesions [254 , 324]. Simultaneous onset of hemiballism and acute ipsilateral central-type pain has been observed
following anterior parietal artery stroke [391]. Hemiballism/hemichorea may result from nonketotic hyperosmolar hyperglycemia [
264]. Hemiballism may be caused by discrete ischemic or hemorrhagic vascular lesions of the STN. The involved vessels arise from
the posterior thalamoperforators, posterior communicating artery, or anterior choroidal artery [154 , 458]. Hemiballism has also been
associated with tumors, arteriovenous malformations, encephalitis, abscess, systemic lupus erythematosus, complications of
acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, cysticercosis, head trauma, subdural hematoma, tuberculous meningitis, demyelination,
tuberous sclerosis complex, Sydenham chorea, basal ganglia calcifications, multiple system atrophies, as a side effect of levodopa
therapy or oral contraceptives, and following surgery for advanced PD [87 , 132 , 177 , 324 , 381 , 458]. Bilateral ballism has been described
with bilateral hemorrhagic infarctions of the caudate and putamen, multiple sclerosis, disseminated intravascular coagulation,
systemic lupus erythematosus, nonketotic hyperosmolar hyperglycemia, phenytoin intoxication, dopaminergic drug-induced
dyskinesia in PD and following ventriculoperitoneal shunting [279 , 355].
Akathisia
Akathisia refers to a feeling of inner restlessness and dysphoria, associated with an inability to stay still, causing back and forth
trunk rocking and/or marching in place movements [54 , 171]. Other movements include squirming, repetitive weight shifting,
crossing and uncrossing the legs, pacing, moaning, humming, or groaning. Differential diagnosis include other disorders associated
with moaning sounds or humming, including tic disorders, oromandibular dystonia, Huntington disease, and parkinsonism [155 , 171 ,
326].
Drug-induced akathisia most often results from the use of dopamine receptor antagonists, neuroleptics, antiemetics, or tetrabenazine.
Akathisia may follow initiation of drug therapy (acute akathisia), subsequent to the emergence of drug-induced parkinsonism, or
following chronic therapy (tardive akathisia).
Hypotensive akathisia observed in patients with autonomic failure results in frequent voluntary, transiently suppressible, yet
irresistible leg movements occurring only in the sitting position. Repetitive leg crossing, muscle tensing, foot twirling or wiggling,
and heel or toe tapping while sitting may compensate for the orthostatic hypotension [ 89].
Athetosis
Athetosis refers to slow, uncoordinated, twisting, writhing, wide amplitude involuntary movements involving distal appendicular
muscles, particularly the upper extremities.
Athetoid movements may be unilateral (hemiathetosis) or bilateral (double athetosis) and may often interfere with activities of daily
living. Athetoid movements are often associated with episodic hypertonia

of axial and appendicular muscles.
Differentiating athetosis from chorea or dystonia may at times be challenging because, not uncommonly, patients have “mixed”
dyskinesias (i.e., choreoathetosis).
Athetosis is usually observed with neurodegenerative or neurometabolic disorders such as Wilson disease, kernicterus, status
marmoratus, or perinatal anoxia involving widespread cerebral structures, including the GP, STN, red nucleus, or midbrain
tegmentum. Seldom, focal vascular or neoplastic striatal lesions sparing the motor cortex or its efferents result in athetosis.

Athetosis must be distinguished from “pseudoathetoid” movements that are noted on attempts to maintain posture such as extending
both arms. Pseudoathetosis results from faulty proprioception due to damage to large peripheral nerve fibers, dorsal root ganglia,
dorsal columns/medial lemniscal pathway, or the parietal lobes [414].
Dystonia
Dystonia is a disorder characterized by cocontraction of agonist and antagonist muscles resulting in slow, sustained, contorting,
involuntary movements and postures involving proximal appendicular and axial muscles [9 , 170 , 300]. The dystonic movements are
typically slow and “wrapping” (athetotic dystonia). Some patients may demonstrate superimposed, rapid, involuntary jerks, termed
dystonic spasms or myoclonic dystonia[ 372]. The dystonic posturing results in disabling and abnormal pose (dystonic postures) of
the involved body regions such as torticollis, tortipelvis, lordotic or scoliotic postures, inversion of the hands and forearms, or
equinovarus posturing [249] (
Videos 19-9, 19-10 and 19-11). A characteristic feature of a dystonic contraction is the consistent
directionality. Dystonia may be associated with fast and rhythmic tremulous movements (dystonic tremor) [222 , 389]. Dystonic hand
tremor may result from a contralateral anterior thalamic infarction [92]. Oculogyric crisis has been observed as an adverse effect of
neuroleptics, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, Wilson disease, encephalitis lethargica, organophosphate poisoning, and lesions of
the lentiform nuclei [231].

Dystonia may be generalized and idiopathic (dystonia musculorum deformans) or symptomatic (drug-induced, Wilson disease,
Menkes disease, metachromatic leukodystrophy, Lesch-Nyhan disease, Whipple disease, homocystinuria, hexosaminidase A and B
deficiencies, progressive supranuclear palsy [PSP], cortical-basal ganglionic degeneration [CBGD], and GM1 and GM2
gangliosidosis). Conversely, dystonia may be segmental and idiopathic (spasmodic torticollis, writer's cramp, musician's cramp or
dystonia, spasmodic dysphonia, blepharospasm, and orofacial dyskinesia), or symptomatic (posthemiplegic dystonia). Occasionally,
dystonia may be focal and task specific and at times associated with tremor [294 , 389]. An unusual form of occupational-specific
oromandibular hemidystonia is auctioner's jaw[ 409].
Lesch-Nyhan disease results from a deficiency of the purine salvage enzyme hypoxanthine-guanine phosphoribosyltransferase
(HPRT) [224]. Biotinresponsive basal ganglia disease (BBGD), an autosomal recessive disorder associated with mutations of the
SLC19A3 gene, typically presents in childhood with subacute episodes of encephalopathy often precipitated by a febrile illness and
characterized by confusion, dysarthria, dysphagia, and external ophthalmoplegia [435]. Patients progress to severe dystonia,
quadriparesis, and coma. Brain MRI shows bilateral caudate and putaminal lesions.
Hemidystonia may follow lesions of the contralateral caudate, putamen, or thalamus [96 , 162 , 304 , 339 , 484]. Most common etiologies
include stroke, trauma, and perinatal injury [96]. Lesions resulting in dystonic spasms or myoclonic dystonia predominate in the
contralateral striatopallidal complex or thalamus [ 268]. Lesions of the striatopallidal complex involved the putamen posterior to the
anterior commissure, extending into the dorsolateral caudate nucleus, posterior limb of the internal capsule, or GPE. Thalamic
lesions were centered in the ventral intermediate and ventral caudal nuclei, whereas the ventral oral anterior and posterior thalamic
nuclei were largely spared [268]. Acute dystonic camptocormia may result from vascular lesions involving the lenticular nucleus,
particularly the putamen [345].
Brainstem lesions, including tegmental pontomesencephalic hemorrhages and posttraumatic diffuse axonal injury have also been
associated with dystonia [280]. Paroxysmal hemidystonia has been observed with contralateral midbrain lesions [412].
Hemidystonia-hemiatrophy syndrome refers to a disabling neurologic condition similar to the hemiparkinsonism-hemiatrophy
syndrome [475]. In a study of 26 patients with a mean age at onset of hemidystonia of 14.9 years, mean latency from the onset of
hemiparesis to the onset of hemidystonia was 14.7 years [475]. All patients with hemiparesis had marked motor improvement prior
to the onset of hemidystonia. Common etiologies included birth or perinatal injuries and stroke. Hemidystonia-hemiatrophy is
usually the result of a static encephalopathy, the sequelae of stroke or brain injury [475].
Primary dystonias may be genetic. Hereditary childhood-onset dystonia (idiopathic torsion dystonia, DYT1) most commonly
manifest between 6 and 12 years of age, with the onset of foot dystonia while walking. The disorder is slowly progressive, with the
dystonia becoming generalized. DYT1 is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait with a penetrance of about 30% [9].

The abnormal gene, located on chromosome 9q, produces a protein called Torsin A[ 357]. Other autosomal dominant
dystonias include DYT4, DYT6, DYT7, and DYT13 [119]. DYT6 dystonia is typically an adolescent onset mixed dystonia caused
by a primarily dominantly inherited mutation in the Thanatos-associated protein domain containing apoptosis-associated protein 1
gene (THAPI). DOPA-responsive dystonia (Segawa variant or hereditary progressive dystonia) is an autosomal dominant disorder
linked to chromosome 14q22.1 to q22.2. DOPA-responsive dystonia typically presents in childhood (age 5 to 6 years) with dystonic
movements and postures that are remarkably responsive to low doses of levodopa [193 , 410]. These children may also have
parkinsonism, lower extremity hyperreflexia, and bilateral extensor plantar responses. As a result, not uncommonly, they are


<!-- PDF page 759 -->

misdiagnosed as having “cerebral palsy.” The dystonic movements and postures show marked diurnal variations, being more
pronounced in the late afternoon and evening hours. Dopa-responsive dystonia often results from a mutation in the GTP
cyclohydrolase I gene, which accounts for a deficiency in dopamine [206]. The clinical phenotype has been expanded to include
adult-onset parkinsonism, oromandibular dystonia, spontaneously remitting dystonia, spasticity with developmental delay
mimicking cerebral palsy, and generalized hypotonia with proximal weakness [119]. Patients with autosomal dominant Segawa
syndrome due to GTP cyclohydrolase deficiency may also have late-onset mild dopa-responsive symptoms of rigidity and frequent
falls [449]. Major depressive disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and sleep disorders, may also be observed in these patients [
449]. Likewise, some patients have cerebellar dysfunction and scoliosis [83].
DYT14 is another genetic defect resulting in dopa-responsive dystonia [184].
Myoclonic dystonia (DYT11) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by dystonic myoclonus and prolonged spasms [168 ,
234]. Clinical manifestations generally occur in the first or second decades of life. Myoclonus is the most disabling feature and
predominantly involves the arms and axial muscles. The dystonia is usually mild and often results in cervical dystonia or writer's
cramp. Tremor may also occur. Patients often improve with alcohol consumption. In some families, the genetic abnormality has
been localized to the protein epsilon-sarcoglycan [27 , 385]. Levodopa-responsive myoclonus-dystonia has also been described in a
family with tyrosine hydroxylase deficiency [425] and myoclonic dystonia in patients with cerebrotendinous xanthomatosis [257].
An X-linked recessive dystonia-parkinsonism, known as Lubag disease, is primarily found in the Philippines [468]. Rapid-onset
dystonia parkinsonism (DYT12) is an autosomal dominant disorder characterized by abrupt onset of dystonia, associated with
parkinsonism [120]. Autosomal dominant dystonia-plus with cerebral calcifications presents with focal, segmental, multifocal, or
generalized dystonia, at times associated with chorea, intellectual decline, postural tremor, and dysarthria [ 480].
Woodhouse Sakati syndrome refers to a rare autosomal recessive neuroendocrine disorder, with onset in adolescence, characterized
by alopecia, hypogonadism, diabetes mellitus, intellectual disability, sensorineural deafness, and extrapyramidal features [402].
Movement disorders consist of dystonia and choreiform limb movements [402]. The facial muscles are usually spared. Dysarthria is
common. Upper motor neuron findings and peripheral abnormalities are inconsistent features. Most reported families are from
Middle Eastern countries.
An unusual familial dystonia-plus phenotype resulting in dystonia and cerebellar atrophy has been associated with focal or
generalized dystonia, with involvement of the vocal cords and upper limbs. Cerebellar ataxia was restricted to gait unsteadiness and
evolved rather slowly. The paucity of cerebellar signs contrasted with the extent of cerebellar atrophy on brain MRI. An autosomal
recessive pattern of inheritance has been proposed; however, X-linked inheritance is possible [ 262].
Focal dystonias such as torticollis are usually sporadic and occur later in life. Patients may display more than one form of dystonia.
There may be a genetic basis for focal dystonias; for example, one family with spasmodic torticollis (DYT7) had a genetic linkage
to chromosome 18p [270]. Secondary dystonias result from a variety of neurologic disorders, including PD, Wilson disease,
gangliosidoses, leukodystrophies, Leigh syndrome, pantotheanate kinase-associated neurodegeneration (PKAN), juvenile
Huntington disease, CBGD, and lesions involving the putamen, caudate, and thalamus.
Dystonia may also have a psychogenic basis [261]. Schrag and colleagues described the clinical features of 103 patients presenting
with “fixed dystonia” [407]. Most patients were female and had a young age of onset. A peripheral injury preceded onset in two-
thirds of the cases. The dystonia spread to other body regions in about half of the patients. After an average follow-up of 3.3 years
(overall disease duration 8.6 years), partial or complete remission had occurred in a minority of patients. The fixed postures affected
predominantly the limbs, and rarely the neck, shoulders, or jaw. The authors posited that fixed dystonia may occur following a
peripheral injury.

Torticollis
Torticollis (idiopathic cervical dystonia, wryneck, nuchal dystonia), the most common type of adult onset focal dystonia, is
characterized by asymmetric posturing of the head and neck. Primary muscles involved are the sternocleidomastoid and trapezius.
Several presenting positions include flexion, extension, right or left tilt. Torticollis abates during sleep and is more pronounced with
stress and anxiety. Sensory tricks may correct the abnormal postures. Applying light-touch on the face, leaning against a high-
backed chair, pulling the hair out, or placing something in the mouth may be effective relieving maneuvers (geste antagoniste).
Localized pain is often reported. Head tremor is present in two-thirds of patients [17 , 59 , 116 , 217 , 258 , 286].
Torticollis may be congenital and in some cases is associated with unilateral fibrosis of the sternocleidomastoid muscle. Acquired
torticollis may be associated with cervical spine abnormalities such as Klippel-Feil syndrome, hemivertebrae, or other congenital
abnormalities of the cervical spine, cervical spondylosis, cervical spine subluxation, inflammatory disorders, high cervical spinal
cord tumors, and syringomyelia [236]. Posttraumatic cervical dystonia may follow a relatively mild neck trauma [179]. Cervical
dystonia may also be associated with posterior fossa tumors. Retrocollis has been reported with bilateral putaminal hemorrhages [
417] and focal dystonia with vascular or neoplastic lesions of the lenticular and caudate nuclei [ 252]. Transient cervical dystonia may
develop during pregnancy (dystonia gravidarum) [97 , 273]. A trial of levodopa should be considered in any patient with young-onset
cervical dystonia [ 405]. However, most often, cervical dystonia results from an extrapyramidal disorder of unknown etiology and
pathologic substrate (spasmodic torticollis or idiopathic cervical dystonia) [116].

Writer's Cramp, Musician's Dystonia, the Yips, and Other Focal Dystonias
Classic focal task-specific dystonias include writer's cramp and musician's dystonia.
Writer's cramp (graphospasm) is a focal, task-specific dystonia characterized by spasms, cramps, aches, and occasional hand
tremors induced by writing or holding a writing utensil. Examination shows no evidence of oromandibular, axial or appendicular
dystonia, blepharospasm, or torticollis. Patients with writer's cramp have been shown to have structural abnormalities within a
sensorimotor network including the cerebellum and cortical representation of the affected hand [223 , 226 , 416].
Musician's dystonia, also known as musician's cramps, is another focal task-specific dystonia induced by highly trained movements
such as playing certain musical instruments. Focal hand dystonia has been attributed to a maladaptive response of the brain to the
repetitive performance of stereotyped and attention-demanding hand movements. However, not all patients with focal hand dystonia


<!-- PDF page 765 -->

have a history of excessive hand use. For example, patients with musician's dystonia spend many hours per day with their attention
focused on instrumental practice, whereas many patients with writer's cramp have a history of average daily hand use [124 , 130 , 212 ,
223 , 390 , 394 , 408].
A unique clinical phenomenon, known as mirror dystonia, is a motor overflow phenomenon characterized by dystonia occurring in
the dominant hand when writing with the homologous hand [223]. The pathophysiology is unknown, although decreased
interhemispheric inhibition has been proposed [ 38 , 102]. Up to one-third of golfers develop the yips, a movement disorder
characterized by involuntary jerking of the wrists leading to an inability to complete a stroke, most often affecting short putts [3 , 4 ,
133 , 312]. The involuntary spasms occur most commonly during putting and, to a lesser extent, during chipping. The yips have been
attributed to a task-specific focal dystonia. The yips can also affect people who practice other sports such as pistol shooting, dart
throwing, billiard players, and tennis and baseball players [133]. Table tennis dystonia is another example of focal task-specific
dystonia [267].


### [Page 543 begins | PDF 765]


Focal lip dystonia presents with acute onset of involuntary, tonic, sustained, lateral, and outward protrusion movements of one-half
of the lower lip [240]. Focal lip dystonia is present at rest, whereas in some patients it is also present when talking. The abnormal lip
posture can be voluntarily suppressed. Spontaneous remissions are frequent.
Intermittent or sustained severe involuntary tongue protrusion dystonia may result in speech, swallowing, and breathing difficulties
that at times may be life threatening [ 401
]. Tongue protrusion dystonia may be associated with neuroacanthocytosis, PKAN, Lesch-Nyhan syndrome, anoxia, and tardive
dystonia.
Oromandibular dystonia, also known as cranial dystonia, is a focal dystonia characterized by forceful contractions of the face, jaw,
and/or tongue resulting in difficulties with opening and closing of the mouth [ 332]. Jaw dystonia and laryngospasm are common
accompaniments of ANNA-2 (anti-Ri) autoimmunity associated with lung cancer [370]; oromandibular and lingual dystonia may
also occur in patients with spinocerebellar ataxia type 8 [447].
An unusual painless and debilitating focal task-specific dystonia known as embouchure dystonia (ED), affects the muscles of the
lower face, jaw, tongue, and pharynx that control the flow of air into the mouthpiece of a woodwind or brass instrument [ 163 , 164 , 194
]. Specific musical techniques trigger ED, particularly in one instrumental register. Task-specific embouchure tremor and lip-pulling
phenotypes are common among high-register brass players (trumpet and French horn), whereas lip-locking occurs exclusively in
low-register brass players (trombone and tuba). Jaw and tongue phenotypes occur predominantly among woodwind players.
Functional brain MRI shows enhanced activation of somatotopic face areas within the primary sensory motor cortices and in both
premotor cortices during buzzing at the instrumental mouthpiece among individuals with ED [194]. About 10% of patients with ED
have coincident writer's cramp, suggesting a possible genetic predisposition [164].
Unilateral, bilateral, focal or segmental, hyperkinetic, or hypokinetic movement disorders are a recognized complication of stroke.
The involuntary movements can occur at stroke presentation or be delayed. Vascular dystonias can involve the ipsilateral side of the
face, arm, and leg (hemidystonia) or can be focal or segmental. Hemidystonia is most frequently observed with infarctions of the
caudate, putamen, or thalamus. Focal dystonia post stroke most commonly affects the hand [49 , 349 , 366 , 421]. A caudate head
infarction may cause contralateral hand weakness, followed by a gradually severe focal hand dystonia with characteristic spreading
of the digits, resembling a “starfish” (starfish hand) [203].
In contrast to childhood-onset dystonia, the lower extremities are infrequently affected in adult-onset primary dystonia. Foot
dystonia in an adult should suggest a secondary cause [403].
A rare form of adult-onset focal dystonia occurring in long-distance runners (runner's dystonia) is often erroneously attributed to an
orthopedic disorder. These patients develop proximal dystonia of one leg during long-distance running. Foot flexion or inversion is a
common presenting symptom [481].

Benign essential blepharospasm (BEB) or blepharospasm is an uncommon focal dystonia characterized by involuntary eyelid

Spasmodic Dysphonia
Spasmodic dysphonia or laryngeal dystonia is a task-specific focal dystonia affecting the laryngeal muscles while speaking.
Adductor spasmodic dysphonia (ADSD) is the most common (90% of cases); abductor spasmodic dysphonia (ABSD) is relatively
rare [ 15 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 75 , 153 , 214 , 265 , 289 , 290 , 311 , 363 , 365 , 371].
ADSD is associated with involuntary hyperadduction of the vocal folds, resulting in a strained or strangled voice quality with
voiceless breaks during vowels. ABSD is characterized by involuntary abductions of the vocal folds, resulting in intermittent bursts
of breathy phonation. Associated manifestations of spasmodic dysphonia include stridor, irregular breathing pattern, paroxysmal
cough, hiccups, or sneezing. Patients with spasmodic dysphonia often have other neurologic findings, including abnormal rapid
alternating movements, and tremors, including focal vocal voice tremors. In contrast to spasmodic dysphonia, in spasmodic
laryngeal dyspnea, the laryngopharyngeal spasms occur primarily during respiration rather than phonation. In cases of spasmodic
laryngeal dyspnea, the dyspnea results from intermittent glottic and supraglottic airway obstruction due to laryngeal and
supralaryngeal/pharyngeal muscle spasms. Acute laryngeal dystonia is a life-threatening side effect of classic antipsychotics [95 , 486

Paroxysmal Dyskinesias
The paroxysmal dyskinesias are a heterogeneous group of episodic involuntary movement disorders (dystonia, choreoathetosis, and
ballism), associated with normal motor function between attacks [ 46 , 66 , 125 , 145 , 225 , 259 , 450]. The paroxysmal dyskinesias are
classified as follows:
1. Paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD), also known as paroxysmal kinesigenic choreoathetosis, episodic kinesigenic
dyskinesia, familial paroxysmal dystonia, PKD, is the most common type of paroxysmal dyskinesias. PKD is characterized by
short lasting attacks of involuntary movements involving the extremities, trunk, neck, and/or face, induced by sudden
movements. The attacks may result in falls and may affect speech. Most PKD cases are familial and inherited as an autosomal
dominant trait. Familial PKD can be caused by mutations in the proline-rich transmembrane protein 2 (PRRT2), which
encodes a cell surface protein expressed in neurons [464]. PKD may be idiopathic or associated with multiple sclerosis, head
trauma, PSP, putaminal/thalamic infarctions, hypoparathyroidism with basal ganglia calcifications, HIV infection, and
hyperglycemia with vascular malformations of the lenticular nucleus [ 55 , 330 , 387].
Bruno and colleagues reviewed clinical features of 121 individuals with a presumptive diagnosis of idiopathic PKD [66]. Most
individuals had a distinctive phenotype. The following diagnostic criteria were proposed: identified trigger for the attacks
(sudden movements), short lasting attacks (<1 minute), no loss of consciousness or pain during attacks, responsiveness to


<!-- PDF page 767 -->

anticonvulsants, exclusion of other organic diseases, and age at onset between 1 and 20 years if there was no contributing
family history (age at onset was considered a less stringent criterion for those individuals with family history). Comparing
familial and sporadic cases of PKD, the latter were more common among men; a history of infantile convulsions was more
common among the familial kindred. Women had a higher remission rate than men. An infantile-onset subgroup without
consistent kinesigenic triggers, lack of universal response to anticonvulsants, and occurrence of attacks during sleep was
identified.
2. In paroxysmal nonkinesigenic dyskinesia (PNKD; paroxysmal dystonic choreoathetosis), attacks of involuntary movements
(usually lasting minutes to hours) occur spontaneously or are brought on by alcohol, caffeine, menses, stress, fatigue, or
excitement. Familial forms of PNKD are caused by mutations in the PNKD gene. PNKD has also been associated with
multiple sclerosis, perinatal encephalopathy, hypoparathyroidism with basal ganglia calcification, encephalitis, thyrotoxicosis,
strokes, infantile hemiplegia, head trauma, recurrent hypoglycemia, AIDS, diabetes mellitus, anoxia, and brain tumors. Not
sensitive to anticonvulsants, only one-third of PNKD patients improve with medications [55 , 118 , 219 , 330 , 434].
3. In paroxysmal exertion-induced dyskinesia, the attacks are induced by prolonged physical exertion. The involuntary
movements primarily affect the legs and last between 5 and 30 minutes.
4. In paroxysmal hypnogenic dyskinesia. In this type of dyskinesia, the episodes of involuntary movements occur only during
sleep.
5. Infantile convulsions and choreoathetosis syndrome (ICCA syndrome). Families with ICCA often have relatives with
infantile convulsions who subsequently develop episodes of paroxysmal choreoathetosis resembling PKD [ 46 , 307 , 451].
Other paroxysmal movement disorders include benign paroxysmal dystonia/torticollis in infancy (Sandifer syndrome) and
paroxysmal ataxia and tremor. Sandifer syndrome refers to a spasmodic tilting downward of the head and neck after eating a meal,
occurring mainly in boys as a result of gastroesophageal reflux [ 293]. Paroxysmal ataxia and tremor are associated with limb
myokymia or neuromyotonia, nystagmus, or ocular motility dysfunction [46 , 47].
Myoclonus
Myoclonus is a movement disorder characterized by unexpected, sudden, brief, brisk, shock-like, involuntary, repetitive,
synchronous, or asynchronous jerks of all or part of the body. Myoclonus has been categorized into physiologic, essential, epileptic,
and symptomatic. Nonepileptic myoclonus may originate in the basal ganglia, brainstem, spinal cord, or peripheral nervous system.
Myoclonus may also be classified in many ways: positive or negative, focal, multifocal, or generalized.
For example, diaphragmatic myoclonus (diaphragmatic flutter) is an unusual focal myoclonus resulting in repetitive, involuntary
contractions of the diaphragm and other inspiratory muscles [88]. Myoclonus may develop spontaneously or on attempted
movement (action myoclonus) [260]. It may be triggered by cutaneous, auditory, or visual stimuli. Action or intention myoclonus is
most often a sequela of cerebral hypoxia (Lance-Adams syndrome). It can also occur in association with certain neurodegenerative
disorders such as Ramsay Hunt syndrome. Myoclonus may also occur with structural lesions or metabolic spinal cord disorders,
brainstem, cerebellum, or cerebral cortex disorders (“sleep starts”).

Essential myoclonus (myoclonic dystonia; DYT11) may be sporadic or hereditary. Typical onset is beyond the age of 20 years.
Clinical course is benign and compatible with an active and normal life span, absence of cerebellar ataxia, spasticity, dementia, or

Epileptic myoclonus refers to the presence of myoclonus in the setting of epilepsy. The myoclonus may be focal or generalized,

Opsoclonus-myoclonus syndrome (Kinsbourne syndrome) is a rare autoimmune inflammatory disorder associated with a variety of
viral or bacterial infections, toxins, or a variant of a paraneoplastic syndrome associated with small cell lung cancer and breast
cancer in adults and with neuroblastoma in children. The myoclonus is associated with conjugate, involuntary, rapid, large-

Spinal myoclonus involves repetitive myoclonic jerking of an arm or leg and may be due to spinal cord trauma, tumor, or
inflammatory lesions [158]. When spinal myoclonus is suspected, simple segmental myoclonus (SSM), propriospinal myoclonus

SSM is characterized by focal, repetitive, rhythmic jerks confined to one to three adjacent spinal levels and may be bilateral. SSM
has been associated with an array of structural spinal cord disorders.
PSM consists of predominantly axial and often arrhythmic, slow flexor or extensor jerks involving many spinal segments linked by
long propriospinal pathways. The movements may be stimulus sensitive. PSM has been described with cervical cord

Respiratory myoclonus, also termed diaphragmatic flutter, diaphragmatic myoclonus, or Leeuwenhoek disease, refers to a rare
disturbance characterized by brief, repetitive, involuntary contractions of the diaphragm. Although the pathophysiology is unknown,
some have implicated involvement of the respiratory center in the dorsal medulla. Patients with diaphragmatic disorders present
with shortness of breath, inspiratory stridor, epigastric pulsations, or abdominal wall pain [143 , 367]. Familial nocturnal

Weight-bearing hyperkinetic movement disorders include orthostatic tremor (OT) and orthostatic myoclonus (OM). OM is
characterized by gait and steadiness and lower limb myoclonus [176 , 368]. Some patients with OM have PD or atypical parkinsonism.
Other posited associations have been more controversial [196 , 251 , 455].
Palatal tremor (palatal myoclonus) encompasses two separate disorders: essential palatal tremor (EPT) and symptomatic palatal
tremor (SPT). EPT is an uncommon disorder and is distinct from SPT. In EPT, brief and rhythmic movements of the soft palate and
ear click are the only manifestations. The cause of EPT remains unknown. In SPT, the rhythmic movements of the soft palate may
also be associated with synchronous movements of ocular and facial muscles. The rhythmic movements persist during sleep. SPT
results from vascular, demyelinating, neoplastic, or traumatic lesions involving the central tegmental tract in Guillain-Mollaret

triangle (aka: dentatorubroolivary pathway). The Guillain-Mollaret triangle has three corners, namely, the ipsilateral red nucleus in

nucleus. Lesions involving the Guillain-Mollaret triangle disinhibit (activate) the inferior olivary nucleus, which subsequently
develops pseudohypertrophy, and its rhythmic discharges result in palatal tremor [ 52 , 69 , 81 , 128 , 129 , 131 , 137 , 308 , 440 , 459 , 476 , 483].

Painful Legs and Moving Toes
Although there is no proof that the syndromes discussed further on or in the next section are related to basal ganglia or related nuclei
disorders, they are discussed in this chapter to distinguish them from other dyskinesias.
The painful legs and moving toes syndrome (PLMT) is an adult-onset disorder characterized by unilateral or bilateral debilitating leg
or foot pain associated with continual, slow, writhing, “athetoid-like” movements of one or more toes [136 , 195 , 423]. The toe
movements have a sinusoidal quality and may occur during sleep [334]. Most reports suggest an association with a peripheral
nervous system lesion, usually at the level of the nerve root or peripheral nerve with secondary impairment in spinal or supraspinal
controls and central sensitization. The neurologic examination is normal, except in cases associated with peripheral neuropathy,
including HIV-related neuropathy or radiculopathy [ 136 , 334 , 342 , 441].
Rarely, the disorder affects the upper extremities and may even be painless. This is known as painful arms or hands and moving
fingers and painless legs and moving toes[ 140 , 208 , 430 , 453]. Another spinal segmental disorder possibly analogous to PLMT is the
painful shoulder-moving deltoid syndrome[ 14 , 296].
Restless Legs Syndrome and Periodic Limb Movements of Sleep
Restless legs syndrome (RLS; Willis-Ekbom syndrome, also known as anxietas tibiarum) refers to a prevalent sensorimotor disorder
with a circadian rhythmicity characterized by an overpowering urge to move the legs during sleep. People with RLS experience
feelings of “crawling,” “pulling,” “aching,” “throbbing,” “itching,” or “pins and needles.” Symptoms are generally worse during the
evening or night and often interfere with sleep. RLS can also involve the arms and trunk [ 84 , 93 , 141 , 204 , 354 , 445 , 463 , 477]. Criteria for
diagnosis of RLS include (a) an intense, irresistible urge to move the legs, usually associated with sensory complaints, including
paresthesias and dysesthesias, (b) motor restlessness, (c) worsening of symptoms with rest and relief with motor activity, and (d)
increased symptom severity in the evening or at night. Arm restlessness is reported by half of the patients and may be the initial
symptom [157 , 325]. The neurologic examination is normal.
Some patients with RLS also have myoclonic jerks or dystonic movements. Most commonly, in older people RLS is often
associated with a hypnogenic dyskinesia known as PLMS [93 , 100 , 200 , 291]. PLMS is characterized by repetitive movements of the
lower limbs that occur every 20 to 40 seconds, usually during light stages I or II of non-REM sleep. The movements appear as
dorsiflexion of the foot and flexion of the knees and hips. The upper limbs are seldom involved.

The pathophysiology of RLS is unknown. Dopaminergic dysfunction and reduced levels of iron in the basal ganglia and thalamus
are likely contributing factors [331]. RLS has been associated with various medical conditions, including diabetes mellitus, vitamin
deficiencies, iron deficiency anemia, pregnancy, uremia, malabsorption, carcinoma, amyloidosis, chronic obstructive pulmonary
disease, spinal cord disorders, PD, and certain medications such as antidepressants, antihistaminics, and beta blockers. However,
most cases of RLS are idiopathic [ 192 , 354]. PLMS has been reported with various disorders involving the spinal cord, including
multiple sclerosis, Isaac syndrome (neuromyotonia), motor neuron disease, cervical spondylosis, spinal cord injuries, spinal cord
tumors, spinal anesthesia, and syringomyelia [ 346]. PLMS may also develop following pontine infarction [232]. Growing
epidemiologic evidence argues in favor of an increased risk of hypertension and cardiovascular disease among people with RLS and
PLMS [ 149].
Tics
Tics are sudden, recurrent, nonrhythmic, stereotyped, clonic hyperkinesias that may be willfully suppressed for brief periods and
that abate during sleep. Tics usually start around the eyes or mouth, at times spreading to the neck or shoulders or becoming
generalized. Tics may result in simple motor movements (eye blinking, nose twitching, shoulder shrugging, head jerking), complex
motor movements (head shaking, skipping), simple phonic sounds (throat clearing, grunting, barking), or complex vocalizations
(coprolalia, hiccuping, echolalia). Tics are common in childhood and most commonly do not persist beyond a year (transient tics of
childhood). However, tics may persist into adult life, although the tics generally decrease in intensity and frequency (chronic motor
tics).

Tremor
Tremor[ 35 , 152 , 187 , 201 , 213 , 413 , 482], the most common movement disorder, is an involuntary, rhythmic, oscillatory movement of a
body part resulting from alternating or synchronous contractions of reciprocally innervated antagonist muscles. Tremor usually
involves the distal extremities and, less often, the head and neck.
Tremors are classified as physiologic (7 to 11 Hz) or pathologic. Physiologic tremor is a low-amplitude, high-frequency tremor
often barely seen with the unaided eye. It may be enhanced by fatigue, anxiety, withdrawal of opioids or alcohol, thyrotoxicosis,
hypoglycemia, pheochromocytoma, or certain medications (catecholamines, steroids, amphetamines, caffeine, or theophylline) [413
]. Nonphysiologic tremors are classified as resting, postural, action, or task specific.
1. Resting tremor (3.5 to 7.0 Hz) is seen in a relaxed body part and completely supported against gravity. Rest tremor is a
cardinal sign of PD. Typical features include pronation-supination of the forearm and rhythmic movements of the thumb
across the fingers (pill rolling). PD tremor is often insidious in onset and markedly asymmetric or purely unilateral at onset.
The tremor may be enhanced by mental stress or movements of another body part. PD tremor may affect the chin, mandible,
tongue, or lower limbs. Tongue tremor is less often reported. A postural tremor may be present as well.
2. Postural tremor (6 to 11 Hz) occurs while maintaining an antigravity position. Postural tremor is more commonly observed
with essential tremor (ET), drug-induced tremor, dystonic tremor, and physiologic tremor.
3. Kinetic or action tremor (3 to 7 Hz) is most prominent with goal-directed movements and amplified when the target is
reached. Action tremor is usually associated with lesions of the cerebellum or cerebellar pathways [51 , 413].
ET, the most common cause of an isolated tremor disorder, is a postural and kinetic tremor of variable amplitude usually
affecting both hands. The tremor is usually symmetric and can also affect the head (“yes-yes,” “no-no”), lower limbs, voice,
tongue, face, and trunk. Handwriting tends to be large and shaky. Alcohol consumption lessens the tremor. The condition is
not associated with other neurologic signs such as dystonia, ataxia, or parkinsonism. More than half of the patients with ET
have a family history suggesting an autosomal dominant inheritance, although specific genes linked to ET have not been
identified [35 , 104 , 165 , 246 , 282 , 418 , 428 , 429]. The true cause of ET is still not understood, but is thought to originate by abnormal
oscillatory activity of the cortico-ponto-cerebello-thalamo-cortical loop [369]. Pathologic studies are remarkable for loss of
cerebellar Purkinje cells and no Lewy bodies and ET with Lewy bodies in the brainstem, particularly in the locus ceruleus [283
].

Jaw tremor may occur in either ET or PD. Jaw tremor in patients with ET is associated with older age, more severe action tremor of
the arms, and presence of head and voice tremor. Whether the presence of early jaw tremor in ET is a marker for subsequent
conversion to a parkinsonian disorder is uncertain [ 85 , 202 , 266 , 285].
Isolated lingual dyskinesias are very unusual. Orolingual tremor is a rhythmic, involuntary oscillatory movement of the jaw, tongue,
pharynx, and face. Tongue tremors can be observed in patients with PD and ET. Tongue tremor can also be seen with oral
mandibular dystonia and can also be task specific [220 , 420].
Wilson disease, also known as progressive hepatolenticular degeneration, is a rare autosomal recessive disorder of copper
metabolism resulting from the mutation of the copper transport ATP7 gene. Common neurologic symptoms (see Chapter 8) include
tremors, dystonia, ataxia, parkinsonism, dysarthria, dysphagia, and drooling. A large amplitude “wing-beating tremor” may be
demonstrated with the shoulders abducted to 90 degrees. Some patients have undulating tongue movements; others have autonomic
nervous system abnormalities [6 , 314 , 318 , 337]. Ophthalmologic abnormalities consist of Kayser-Fleischer rings, best appreciated on
slit-lamp examination, caused by the deposition of copper in the Descemet membrane, “sunflower” cataracts, slow saccadic eye
movements, and rarely ophthalmoplegia. Typical facial manifestations include excessive grinning, sustained open-mouth smile
sometimes referred to as “vacuous smile”; and fixed forced smile due to a sustained spasm of the risorius and zygomaticus muscles (
risus sardonicus) [82]. Striatal lesions are the most characteristic lesions found in the brain in patients with Wilson disease with
associated neurologic symptoms. Brain MRI studies demonstrate bilateral symmetric hyperintensities in the putamen, thalami,
brainstem (“double panda sign”), and posterior limb of internal capsule. The lesions can be more diffuse, including the pons,
midbrain, dente nucleus, and, less frequently, the corpus callosum [ 374].
OT or “shaky legs syndrome” is an unusual tremor of middle-aged or elderly people characterized by rapid, irregular, and
asynchronous movements of the legs and trunk triggered by standing. The tremor lessens partially or completely when the person is
walking or sitting. Touching a wall or the examiner's hand abates the tremor. There may be loss of extensor muscle tone in the legs
(negative myoclonus). Auscultation with the diaphragm of a stethoscope over the quadriceps and hamstrings demonstrates a
repetitive thumping sound, similar to the noise of a distant helicopter. A surface EMG may establish the diagnosis by recording the
presence of a fast tremor of 13 to 18 Hz in the legs, trunk, and rarely the arms [ 40 , 41 , 60 , 61 , 63 , 165 , 190 , 199 , 275 , 316 , 323 , 327 , 442 , 456].
OT can be idiopathic or secondary to a number of structural lesions. It can occur in isolation (pure OT) or in association with other
movement disorders (OT plus) [ 323]. Associated movement disorders included PD, PSP, RLS, dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB),
multifocal action tremor, and focal dystonia of the arm. Most cases of OT are sporadic, although a few familial cases have been
reported. OT has been associated with pontine and midbrain lesions, cerebellar degeneration, spinocerebellar ataxia type 2,
nontumoral aqueductal stenosis, spinal cord lesions, head trauma, multiple sclerosis SPS, Graves' disease, thiamine and B12
deficiencies, dopamine blocking agents, chronic relapsing polyradiculoneuropathy, and biclonal IgG and IgA lambda gammopathy [
40]. OT with eyelid ptosis and progressive external ophthalmoplegia has been described due to a novel missense mutation at an
evolutionary conserved residue of the C10orf2 TWINKLE gene [327].
Focal task-specific tremors are rare. The most common task-specific tremor is primary writing tremor[ 34]. Primary writing tremor
has been considered a task-induced tremor or a positionally sensitive tremor. Other task-specific tremors include those induced by
playing certain musical instruments, golfing, dart throwing, rifle shooting, typing, brick laying, etc., that are present in the hand
exclusively during writing [ 353].
Vocal tremor often occurs in isolation or may be associated with spasmodic dysphonia. Another unusual type of rhythmic
movement is the painful “jumping” of a stump following amputation of a limb [ 427]. The rabbit syndrome, characterized by rest
tremor involving the perioral and perinasal muscles, has been associated with the use of neuroleptics and PD [ 126].
Tremor may also be psychogenic (psychogenic tremor) [50 , 233]. Psychogenic tremor may assume different forms, but is most
commonly characterized as action tremor with alternating activity of antagonist muscles. Psychogenic tremor varies in amplitude
more than expected and may change frequency. Patients can be assessed while asking them to tap with one limb at specific
frequencies. Psychogenic tremors typically have a sudden onset involving more than one limb. The tremor is present in more than
one limb position and is more associated with a relative lack of progression [127].

Parkinsonism
Bradykinesia; rigidity; resting tremor; freezing; flexed posture of the neck, trunk, and limbs; and impaired postural reflexes are
cardinal features of parkinsonism [ 74 , 169].
Bradykinesia, the most disabling manifestation, is characterized by delays in the initiation and execution of willed movements
accompanied by a reduction of associated automatic movements. Bradykinesia explains, at least in part, the facial hypomimia,
reduced rate of eyelid blinking, impaired ocular convergence, monotonous and low-volume speech (bradylalia eventually leading to
anarthria), drooling of saliva, micrographia, and slow shuffling gait [105].
Rigidity results in resistance to passive movements involving equally both agonist and antagonist muscles and is constant throughout
the full range of movements, predominantly involving the axial and proximal limb muscles.
Cogwheel rigidity is characterized by increased muscle tone with a ratchet quality that can be brought out by distraction maneuvers.
PD is the prototypic akinetic-rigid syndrome resulting from loss of dopamine producing cells in the SN [227]. An akinetic-rigid
syndrome may also be observed in the Westphal variant of Huntington disease, Wilson disease, striatonigral degeneration, PSP,
PKAN, Pick disease, CBGD, hydrocephalus, and diffuse cerebrovascular disease. Therefore, any disorder leading to extensive
bilateral disruption of the striatopallidal complex or its outputs may result in an akinetic-rigid syndrome [300].
Parkinsonian tremor is characteristically a slow, medium to coarse amplitude (3.5 to 7.0 Hz) resting tremor. The tremor is
exacerbated by emotion, fatigue, stress, and anxiety. It is absent during sleep and lessens with volitional activity. It typically affects
distal appendicular muscles, leading to flexion-extension movements of the metacarpophalangeal and interphalangeal joints of
fingers and thumbs; adduction-abduction movements of the thumbs (“pill rolling”); or pronation-supination wrist movements. The
tremor often begins unilaterally in the hands and may initially involve only the thumb or a single digit. The tremor subsequently
spreads to the ipsilateral lower extremity (“hemiparkinsonism”) before involving the opposite half of the body. In addition to rest
tremor, an action tremor (7 to 12 Hz) may be observed. Tremor of the protruded tongue is not uncommon, whereas tremors of the
head, lips, and jaw are less frequent.
Postural disorders may involve the head, trunk, limbs, or the entire body, resulting in forward displacement of the head, forward or
backward trunk instability, difficulty in maintaining an erect posture when being gently pushed, and easy falling.
Freezing phenomena are also common and characterized by very brief, transient periods, usually lasting few seconds, whereby an
ongoing motor act is halted [173]. With freezing, an attempted voluntary motor act is halted because agonists and antagonist muscles
spontaneously and isometrically contract. Freezing phenomena include start-hesitation (freezing when gait is initiated), turn-
hesitation (freezing when turning), destination-hesitation (freezing when approaching a target), freezing when a perceived
“obstacle” is present, sudden palilalia, or freezing of speech, apraxia of eye opening (levator inhibition), or freezing of a limb
(during writing or teeth-brushing) [146]. In cases of start-hesitation, the feet take short sticking, shuffling steps before patients start
walking. With further progression, the feet become literally “glued to the ground.” Freezing is observed in PD, PSP, multisystem
atrophy (MSA), or may be idiopathic without other features except for loss of postural reflexes and mild bradykinesia [2 , 28 , 329 , 382].
Patients with parkinsonism may also demonstrate a “simian posture” characterized by forward flexion of the trunk, elbow flexion,
and partial knee flexion. Patients may also display a “parkinsonian hand” characterized by dorsiflexion of the wrist, flexion of the
metacarpophalangeal joints, extension and adduction of the fingers, and slight ulnar deviation. There may also be a dystonic foot
posture with extension of the great toe, flexion of the other toes, arching of the sole, and foot inversion [343]. Other relevant clinical
features include REM sleep disorder abnormalities, pain, a variety of sensory complaints, hyposmia/anosmia (see Chapter 6),
dysautonomia (orthostatic hypotension), constipation, hesitance and frequency of micturition, seborrhea, hyperhidrosis, exaggerated
nasopalpebral reflex (glabellar tap or Myerson sign), limb-kinetic apraxia, blepharospasm, blepharoclonus, and oculogyric crisis [ 86 ,
322 , 375]. Speech abnormalities include hypokinetic dysarthria, hypophonia, bradyphrenia (slowness in thinking how to respond to
questions), tachyphemia (repetition of a word or phrase with increasing speed and decreasing volume), palilalia, inappropriate silent
periods, involuntary humming, and “tip-of-the-tongue” phenomenon [159 , 309]. Patients with PD may often be dependent, fearful,
indecisive, and passive. Depression occurs in one-third of patients, whereas the risk of dementia occurs in 40% and increases with
age [230 , 310].
Sudden onset of sleep (SOS) can occur in PD [250]. Strongest predictors of SOS include increasing

Parkinsonism is classified as idiopathic or PD, or secondary or parkinsonism. PD, the most common cause of parkinsonism, is a

Several autosomal recessive genes associated with juvenile and young-onset levodopa-responsive parkinsonism have been identified
[360]. Mutations in PRKN, DJ-1, and PINK1 are associated with a rather pure parkinsonian phenotype and have a more benign
course with sustained treatment response and absence of dementia. Conversely, Kufor-Rakeb syndrome has additional features,
including supranuclear vertical gaze palsy, myoclonic jerks, upper motor neuron signs, and cognitive impairment.
Neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation type I due to mutations in PANK2 gene may share similar features with Kufor-
Rakeb syndrome. Mutations in three other genes, PLA2G6 (PARK14), FBXO7 (PARK15), and Spatacsin (SPG11), also account for
similar phenotypes with rapidly progressive parkinsonism initially responsive to levodopa treatment, evolving to cognitive decline
and loss of levodopa responsiveness. Many of these patients have swallowing impairment, severe dystonia, and upper motor neuron
findings. The data suggest that these genes account for many of the cases of levodopa-responsive parkinsonism with “pyramidal”
signs, previously categorized as pallido-pyramidal syndrome [360].
Stiff-Man (Stiff-Person) Syndrome
Stiff-man (stiff-person) syndrome (SPS) is an unusual progressive immune-mediated disorder characterized by waxing and waning
muscle rigidity [32 , 64 , 271 , 422]. In classic SPS, the rigidity affects axial muscles of the back, abdomen, hips, and shoulders, resulting
in hyperlordosis, prominent paraspinal muscle contractions, a “board-like” abdomen, and leg stiffness. Intermittent spasms are
triggered by excitement, anxiety, volitional movements, unexpected noise, or other peripheral stimuli. The spasms are often
debilitating and result in bone fractures or joint dislocations. At times, the spasms result in falls, “like a wooden person.” SPS
develops between the ages of 30 and 60. Men and women are affected equally. Onset is usually gradual. Examination demonstrates
excessive contraction of paraspinal and abdominal muscles without other neurologic manifestations except for brisk muscle stretch
reflexes. The disorder is slowly progressive, with muscle stiffness spreading from the trunk to the hips and shoulders. Gait becomes
slow and stiff-legged. Some patients may

only have limb stiffness (stiff limb syndrome) [37 , 64]. Others
progress to a clinical picture resembling PERM [180].
SPS may be idiopathic, associated with other disorders or represent a paraneoplastic variant. Incidents of paraneoplastic SPS are
unknown. Many patients have antibodies against glutamic acid decarboxylase (GAD), the enzyme responsible for the synthesis of
gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), in blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [460]. Others also have antibodies to amphiphysin or the
GABA (A) receptor-associated protein (GABARAP) [379 , 395]. SPS may occur in association with other paraneoplastic disorders,
including sensory neuropathy, cerebellar ataxia, and opsoclonus. OT tremor may herald onset of SPS [456], and SPS has been
triggered by West Nile Fever [197].
Cortical-Basal Ganglionic (Corticobasal) Degeneration
CBGD or cortico-dentato-nigral degeneration, with neuronal achromasia, or corticobasal syndrome (CBS), is a distinct
neurodegenerative disorder with clinical features referable to both cortical and basal ganglionic dysfunction [ 19 , 39 , 172 , 182 , 247 , 277 ,
383 , 384 , 392 , 406 , 424 , 472]. The illness starts in the sixth or seventh decade with focal dystonia, arm myoclonus, sensory or visual
neglect, alien limb phenomenon (see Chapter 20), or an akinetic-rigid syndrome. The most common initial complaint is unilateral
clumsiness, stiffness, or jerking of an arm. The clinical hallmark of CBGD is asymmetric parkinsonism, unresponsive to levodopa


<!-- PDF page 778 -->

therapy associated with limb ideomotor limb apraxia, alien limb phenomenon, action-induced or stimulus-sensitive myoclonus
(focal reflex myoclonus), dystonia, cortical sensory loss, and parkinsonism. Patients may develop a horizontal and vertical
supranuclear gaze palsy. Other oculomotor findings include saccadic pursuit, hypokinetic vertical saccades, difficulty initiating
voluntary saccades and smooth-pursuit eye movements, and oculogyric crisis. CBGD may present as a primary progressive aphasic
syndrome. Language impairment is common even in patients without aphasia with prevalent phonologic and spelling disturbances [
45 , 156 , 358 , 396].
Four CBGD phenotypes have been described: corticobasal syndrome (CBS), frontal behavioral-spatial syndrome (FBS),
nonfluent/agrammatic variant of primary progressive aphasia (naPPA), and progressive supranuclear palsy syndrome (PSPS) [19].
Pathologic heterogeneity of CBGD is characteristic. In a pathologic study of 13 cases with clinical diagnosis of CBGD, 7 patients
had CBGD, 2 had Alzheimer disease, 1 had PSP, 1 had Pick disease, 1 had CJD, and 1 had nonspecific findings [58].
Some patients with CBGD have a pattern of frontal dementia similar to frontotemporal dementia (FTD). In fact, CBGD shares a
common genetic basis with PSP and FTD [134]. These neurodegenerative conditions are best conceptualized as part of a spectrum of
disorders of the tau gene (tauopathies) [134 , 205].
Progressive Supranuclear Palsy (Steele-Richardson-Olszewski Syndrome)
PSP is one of the 4R tauopathies, so named for the pathologic accumulation of specific isoforms of the microtubule-associated
protein tau, characterized by four repeats in the microtubule-binding domain. PSP is pathologically defined by accumulation of tau
and neuropil threads in the SN, STN, red nucleus, striatum, pontine tegmentum, oculomotor nucleus, medulla, and dentate nucleus.
PSP is often sporadic, but reports of familial cases and genetic association with markers in chromosome 17q21 suggest familial
aggregation. PSP is the second most common cause of degenerative parkinsonism after PD and is characterized by a symmetric
akinetic-rigid syndrome, vertical supranuclear ophthalmoplegia, and early falls [101 , 160 , 161 , 198 , 209 , 242 , 276 , 277 , 278 , 288 , 340 , 348 , 426 ,
439]. Other clinical features include pseudobulbar palsy and retrocollis [101 , 160 , 242]. Mild changes in truncal muscle tone with
prominent neck dystonia and rigidity are characteristic [ 439]. Tremor is usually absent. In contrast to the short and shuffling steps,
stooped posture, narrow base, and flexed knees typically observed in PD, patients with PSP have a stiff and broad-based gait, with a
tendency to have their knees and trunk extended and their arms slightly adducted. Instead of en bloc turning, they tend to pivot,
which further compromises their precarious balance. Patients with PSP may also present with “pure akinesia,” also referred to as
motor blocks or gait ignition failure, and freezing may be an early manifestation of impending PSP [28 , 382].
Often, patients with PSP have deep facial folds and a classic “worried” or “astonished” facial appearance. This characteristic facial
expression results from a focal dystonia of the procerus muscle (procerus sign) [388]. PSP patients usually have a combination of
spastic, hypernasal, hypokinetic, and ataxic dysarthria. Stuttering, dysphasia, apraxia of phonation, and palilalia may also occur [215 ,
241 , 277]. While most patients demonstrate a low-pitched, monotonous dysarthria, some have continuous involuntary vocalizations,
including loud groaning, moaning, humming, and grunting sounds. As a result of chewing difficulties, inability to look down, and
poor hand coordination, patients with PSP have been described as “sloppy eaters” [278]. Less common findings include limb greater
than axial

rigidity, a narrow-based gait, mild rest tremor, upper limb apraxia or ataxia, myoclonus, and
chorea [101]. Spontaneous arm levitation, although usually characteristic of CBGD, may sometimes occur [36].
The initial oculomotor deficits consist of impairment of vertical saccades. Downward saccades are affected first. There is a
disproportionate hypometria of vertical saccades compared with horizontal saccades, abnormal smooth pursuit, blepharospasm,
apraxia of eyelid opening and eyelid closure, eyelid ptosis, bilateral lid retraction, decreased rate of eyelid blinking, loss of the Bell
phenomenon, internuclear ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus, square-wave jerks, ocular flutter, impaired convergence, lateral gaze
palsies, and impaired performance of antisaccade tasks (see Chapter 8) [122 , 160 , 161 , 178 , 209 , 292 , 392]. Patients with PSP show slow
voluntary vertical saccades and nystagmus quick phases compared with PD or controls [167]. Small, paired, horizontal saccadic
intrusions (square wave jerks) are more frequent and larger in PSP during fixation. Patients often lose the ability to read and make
eye contact and often complain of diplopia and photophobia [340]. At a stage when full vertical excursions are still present, some
patients display an inability to produce pure vertical saccades along a straight line in the midline. Instead, they can only accomplish
vertical saccades by moving their eyes in a lateral arc (the round the houses sign) [377]. Late in the development of the disease, the
ocular motor deficit may progress to a complete ophthalmoplegia. Age of onset of PSP is usually in the sixth and seventh decades [
426]. Early disease onset, frequent falls, slowness, and inability to move the eyes downward early in the development of the disease
predict poor survival span [399]. Rarely, PSP may occur without the classic supranuclear gaze palsy [101 , 113 , 117 , 239]. Other
disorders presenting with supranuclear ophthalmoplegia and motor findings similar to PSP include Lewy body disease and
idiopathic striopallidodentate calcifications [148 , 400]. As previously noted, PSP shares a common genetic basis (tauopathies) with
CBGD and FTD [ 134].
In a study of the clinical features of patients with pathologically proven PSP, CBGD, PD, LBD, Pick disease, and MSA, the

The applause sign (a tendency to initiate an automatic program of applause when one is asked to initiate a voluntary program of

5. PSP-cerebellar;
6. PSP-CBGD, cortical predominant, with progressive asymmetric dyspraxia, cortical sensory loss (alien limb phenomenon),

8. PSP and progressive apraxia of speech (PSP-AOS), with prominent pathology in the peri-Sylvian region, especially the
inferior frontal gyrus (Broca area).
Dementia with Lewy Bodies
DLB (Lewy body dementia), a common cause of dementia, is a pathologically defined disease process characterized by cognitive
fluctuations, early spontaneous visual hallucinations, and sensitivity to neuroleptic drugs. Other features include repeated

falls, syncope, and REM-sleep behavioral disorder. Anatomic regions of greater burden of disease include the
superior temporal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, parahippocampal cortex, and amygdala [7 , 142 , 284 , 315 , 443].
Multiple System Atrophy
MSA encompasses a group of sporadic and progressive akinetic-rigid neurodegenerative disorders characterized by autonomic
failure, poor levodopa response parkinsonism, cerebellar ataxia, and upper motor neuron signs, in any combination. Other features
suggestive of MS include orofacial dystonia, inspiratory stridor, a reversible lateral flexion of the trunk while standing relieved by
the supine position (Pisa syndrome), and REM-sleep behavior disorder [274 , 376 , 469 , 470 , 471 , 473]. Neuropathologic findings consist
of widespread and abundant central nervous system α-synuclein-positive glial cytoplasmic inclusions.
The three subtypes of MSA include the cerebellar type (MSA-C), the parkinsonian type (MSA-P), and MSA-related autonomic
failure.
One-third of patients with isolated late-onset cerebellar ataxia and less than one-tenth of patients presenting with parkinsonism

eventually develop MSA. Parkinsonian features predominate in most patients with MSA-P subtype. Cerebellar features are the
major motor disturbance in patients with MSA-C subtype [42 , 474]. MSA-P associated parkinsonism is characterized by progressive
akinesia and rigidity; jerky postural tremor and, less commonly, tremor at rest may be observed. Postural stability is compromised
early, but recurrent falls are unusual. The cerebellar damage of MSA-C results in gait ataxia, limb kinetic ataxia, scanning
dysarthria, and cerebellar ocular motor disturbances [467 , 469]. Impotence is the most frequent autonomic symptom in males,
whereas urinary incontinence predominated in women. Orthostatic hypotension is often the most disabling autonomic manifestation.
Other autonomic manifestations include anhidrosis, decreased tearing, and iris atrophy. Parkinsonism associated with neck extensor
myopathy (head drop) may also occur with MSA [25].
Paraneoplastic Movement Disorders
Paraneoplastic hyperkinetic or hypokinetic movement disorders are an unusual autoimmune nonmetastatic complications of cancer [
183 , 263]. Common paraneoplastic movement disorders include cerebellar syndrome, opsoclonus myoclonus, basal ganglia disorders,
stiff-person syndrome, and neuromyotonia. Paraneoplastic syndromes usually present before cancer diagnosis and are commonly
associated with one or more serum antibodies. Increasing numbers of antibodies associated with a wide array of movement disorders
have been identified (anti-CRMP5-associated chorea; anti-Ma2-hypokinesis and rigidity; anti-Yo cerebellar ataxia and tremor; anti-
Hu ataxia and pseudoathetosis; anti-N-methyl-D-aspartic acid receptors [NMDAR] encephalitis-chorea, dyskinesia, ballism, or
dystonia; SPS-[GAD, amphiphysin, GABA A-receptor associated protein or glycine receptor antibodies]; Caspr2 antibodies-
neuromyotonia; and opsoclonus-myoclonus ataxia [unknown antigens]) [ 91 , 362].
Clinical clues to a paraneoplastic etiology include acute/subacute onset, severity, rapid progression, multifocal localization, and
resistance to treatment. Most common associated malignancies include small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, gynecologic
malignancies, testicular tumors, lymphomas, and thymomas. Early identification and treatment sometimes lead to neurologic
improvement and may even improve cancer prognosis [183].
Autoimmune chorea may be associated with neoplasms; for example, chorea may occur in association with antibodies to collapsing
response mediator protein 5 (CRMP5) [263 , 356]. Other features include limbic encephalitis, ataxia, neuropathy, uveitis, and optic
neuritis [107]. Movement disorders have also been associated with antibodies to the voltage-gated potassium channel (VGKC)
complex[ 263]. Neurologic manifestations may include cognitive impairment, seizures, dysautonomia, myoclonus, dyssomnia,
extrapyramidal manifestations, brainstem/cranial nerve dysfunction, and peripheral nerve manifestations [437]. Four relatively
distinct but interrelated clinical syndromes have been recognized: (1) faciobrachial dystonic seizures (FBDS); (2) limbic
encephalitis; (3) Morvan syndrome; and (4) neuromyotonia. Characteristics of FBDS are brief, frequent, stereotyped paroxysmal
dystonic spasms typically involving one arm and ipsilateral side of the face.
Patients with antibodies to the NR1 subunit of N-methyl-D-aspartic acid (NMDA) receptors (NMDAR) often develop a
characteristic movement disorder 1 to 3 weeks following a psychiatric phase characterized by delusions, hallucinations, mood
changes, and disorientation [111 , 112 , 263]. Patients present with seizures and autonomic instability and, subsequently, with a
characteristic rhythmic or semirhythmic orofacial dyskinesia, and choreoathetoid limb movements. NMDAR encephalitis is more
common in young women; over half of adults harbor an ovarian tumor. About 5% of men with NMDAR encephalitis are found to
have a testicular germ cell tumor or other neoplasms [91].
