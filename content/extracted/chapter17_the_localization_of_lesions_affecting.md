Anatomy of the Region
The hypothalamus constitutes the lateral wall of the third ventricle [ 38 , 64]. It is separated from the thalamus by the hypothalamic
sulcus. The two walls of the third ventricle merge anteriorly to form the lamina terminalis, related superiorly to the anterior
commissure and inferiorly to the optic chiasm [34]. Lateroposteriorly, the hypothalamus borders on the globus pallidus, basal
forebrain nuclei, internal capsule, subthalamic region, and crus cerebri. An inferior prolongation of the floor of the third ventricle,
the pituitary stalk or infundibulum, joins the hypothalamus with the pituitary gland or hypophysis. Each pillar of the fornix,
descending rostrocaudally to end in the mammillary body, divides the hypothalamus into a medial and a lateral region. Although the
hypothalamus is small, different regions can be visualized with high-resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) [16 , 115 , 157].
Main Hypothalamic Nuclear Groups
The hypothalamic nuclei can be conceptualized by considering the hypothalamus as divided by (1) a coronal plane through the
infundibular stalk and (2) an angled parasagittal plane containing the fornix. These planes separate four regions: anterior, posterior,
medial, and lateral. The topography of the hypothalamic nuclei is illustrated in Figures 17-1 and 17-2.
This simplified description is useful for localization, but the complex structure of the hypothalamus continues to be unveiled and
explains ever more clearly its manifold functions as a central hub for the integration of cognitive and homeostatic activity [24 , 111 ,
128 , 131 , 138 , 149 , 176 , 177 , 190 , 196 , 202]. Furthermore, the localization of function to any given region or nuclear group has to be
qualified by the effect of life events on this as in any other brain region. For instance, progesterone receptor-expressing neurons in
the ventromedial hypothalamus are critical for male territorial aggression, but they become inactive with socialization [232].
Connections of the Hypothalamus
The origin, pathways, and termination of the main afferent and efferent hypothalamic connections are listed in Table 17-1. In
summary, the hypothalamus has strong to-and-fro connections with (1) the midbrain and posterior tegmentum, which play an
important role in alertness; (2) the limbic system, through the anterior and mesial temporal cortex, anteromedial thalamic region, and
amygdala, which play an important role in emotion and memory [199]; and (3) the “autonomic” nuclei of the brainstem and spinal
cord, such as the dorsal nucleus of the vagus and the nucleus tractus solitarius. Although direct connections have been traced to the
ipsilateral intermediolateral cell column of the spinal cord, much of the influence of the hypothalamus on the autonomic centers of
the cord is probably exerted through the brainstem reticular formation [38]. Pathways from the retina and olfactory system convey to
the hypothalamus information needed for the circadian control of vegetative functions and for feeding and reproductive behavior [
187].
Much work remains to be done to define the localization and function of the many putative neurotransmitters identified in the
hypothalamus [ 33 , 176]. Multiple neuropeptides have been identified in the hypothalamus of experimental animals and humans [68 , 83
, 128 , 136 , 155]. In addition to the hypophysiotropic hormones regulating anterior pituitary secretion, other neuropeptides play a role in
the regulation of body temperature (bombesin, neurotensin), alertness (orexin, somatostatin), cardiopulmonary function
(thyrotropin-releasing hormone, calcitonin gene-related peptide), water balance (enkephalins), circadian rhythms (neuropeptide Y),
feeding behavior (cholecystokinin, bombesin, galanin, leptin, neuropeptide Y), body growth (substance P), and reproductive
function (oxytocin, vasoactive intestinal peptide). However, the complex hypothalamic actions of these peptides and others present
in high concentration in the hypothalamus (e.g., motilin, secretin) need to be clarified further [136 , 176]. Hypothalamic steroids play
an important role in the sexual differentiation of hypothalamic

Hypothalamic control of vegetative functions is exerted, to a great extent, through the pituitary gland. The hormonal secretions of
the anterior pituitary are regulated by the hypothalamic-releasing factors or hypophysiotropic hormones, which are released into the
infundibular portal system (Fig. 17-3). Through this system, the anterior pituitary receives the richest arteriolar blood flow of any
organ in the body, 0.8 mL per g per min [158]. The infundibulum also contains the important supraopticohypophysial tract,
constituted by axons from neurons in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. Those axons end in a rich capillary network in the
posterior lobe of the pituitary (neurohypophysis), where they secrete oxytocin and antidiuretic hormone (ADH; also called
vasopressin).

Clinical Manifestations of Hypothalamic or Pituitary Dysfunction
Before discussing the most likely location of a lesion causing such symptoms or signs as are attributable to the hypothalamic-
pituitary region (Table 17-2), several points should be noted:
1. Because these structures are small, several portions may be involved simultaneously. For this reason, the fine localization
of functions to specific hypothalamic structures is often not known from human lesions and has to be extrapolated from
experimental animal data [197].
2. Lesions that progress rapidly cause a more florid clinical symptomatology than those that proceed slowly. For instance, a
surgical or vascular lesion in the posterior hypothalamus renders the patient

### [Page 483 begins | PDF 677]

comatose, whereas a
slowly growing tumor affecting the same structures causes only apathy.
3. Unilateral lesions are seldom symptomatic.
4. The changes of hypothalamic function with age are reflected in the disparity of syndromes caused in different age groups
by similarly located lesions [167]. For instance, a similar lesion may cause dwarfism during childhood and gigantism during
adulthood [54].

Disturbances of Temperature Regulation
The hypothalamic “thermostat” for normal temperature regulation is located in the anterior-preoptic hypothalamic area, whose
neurons alter their firing rate in response to a warm or cold environment [ 22 , 210]. However, the physiologic responses for heating
(e.g., vasoconstriction, shivering, increased food intake) and cooling (e.g., increased sweating, peripheral vasodilation, panting,
decreased motor behavior) are controlled by mechanisms located in or traversing the posterior hypothalamus. Because heat
dissipation is normally needed in a warm ambient temperature, hyperthermia results from anterior hypothalamic lesions, whereas
posterior lesions cause hypothermia or poikilothermia by interfering with heat-conservation responses [136 , 167]. Several


<!-- PDF page 679 -->

neurotransmitters and neuropeptides delivered into the hypothalamus of experimental animals have been shown to induce
temperature changes. For instance, serotonin and low doses of opioids induce hyperthermia, whereas high doses of opioids,
angiotensin 11, dopamine, acetylcholine, somatostatin, and neurotensin cause hypothermia [136 , 151]. Their role in temperature
regulation in humans needs to be clarified further.
Physiologic Rhythms
DIURNAL VARIATION
Body temperature peaks in early evening and reaches the lowest point in early morning [136]. Lesions in the median eminence may
flatten the diurnal temperature variation [ 194].
MENSTRUAL CYCLE
Body temperature increases at the time of ovulation; progesterone produces this effect by acting on hypothalamic neurons.
Hypothermia
CHRONIC
Most often, the lesion involves the posterior or the entire hypothalamus. When discomfort is also present, the lesion may be in the
anterior hypothalamus. The most common causes include Wernicke encephalopathy [224], head trauma, craniopharyngioma,
glioblastoma multiforme, surgery, hydrocephalus, infarction, and sarcoidosis [136].
PAROXYSMAL
Spontaneous periodic hypothermia; paroxysmal hypothermia with hyperhidrosis; “diencephalic epilepsy.” Rare syndrome
characterized by an episodic decrease in body temperature [109 , 123 , 147]. The onset is abrupt, with sweating and vasodilatation
leading to hypothermia (as low as 30°C rectally)

### [Page 484 begins | PDF 679]

accompanied by nausea, vomiting, hypotension,
bradycardia, cardiac arrhythmias, salivation, lacrimation, ataxia, asterixis, and mental dullness. The episodes last from minutes to
hours; they may recur only after decades, or they may recur more often, even daily. Thermoregulation may be normal between
attacks [123]. Responsible lesions have involved the arcuate nucleus and premammillary area. A similar syndrome appeared after
surgery for a midbrain glioma [ 69]. This disturbance may be associated with agenesis of the corpus callosum (Shapiro syndrome) [
156], but many patients do not have detectable underlying lesions [22]. One patient had altered norepinephrine metabolism and
responded to clonidine therapy [185]. Others have responded to oxybutynin, glycopyrrolate, cyproheptadine, chlorpromazine, or
phenytoin, suggesting a multiplicity of mechanisms [22].
Hyperthermia
PYROGEN INDUCED
This is the most common cause of hyperthermia. Cases clinically labeled as “hypothalamic hyperthermia” after an unrevealing
search for an infectious source often belong to this category. Bacterial or viral pyrogens can stimulate directly the hypothalamus
and, in addition, induce the release of interleukin 1 (endogenous pyrogen) from leukocytes and macrophages [55]. Circulating
interleukin 1 acts in the preoptic and paraventricular area of the anterior hypothalamus, inducing prostaglandin E2 synthesis, which
can be blocked by aspirin [13 , 136 , 198]. Sepsis may disrupt hypothalamic function [139].
ACUTE HYPERTHERMIA
Acute hyperthermia may occur as a consequence of an acute process (craniotomy, trauma, bleeding) and lasts for <2 weeks. The
lesion affects the anterior hypothalamus. Cardiovascular changes, normally present with fever, are disproportionately small with
hyperthermia due to hypothalamic lesions [167].
PAROXYSMAL HYPERTHERMIA
Involvement of the ventromedial hypothalamus has been suspected in these rare cases, although the precise localization is not
known [136 , 220]. One patient improved after phenytoin administration. Another patient responded to chlorpromazine. This boy had
recurrent episodes of fever, hypertension, weight loss, and vomiting, lasting for about 3 days [ 167]. A patient with agenesis of the


<!-- PDF page 680 -->

corpus callosum and periodic hyperthermia became hypothermic after dopamine administration [87].
MALIGNANT HYPERTHERMIA
Although not due to hypothalamic dysfunction, this syndrome is discussed here with other abnormalities of temperature control.
When exposed to various anesthetic agents and other drugs, susceptible individuals may develop a potentially fatal state of severe
generalized muscular rigidity, metabolic acidosis, myoglobinuria, and hyperthermia [76 , 154]. Hyperthermia is thought to be due to
an intrinsic abnormality of the excitation-contraction coupling mechanism in skeletal muscle, induced by drug exposure, resulting in
sustained myofibrillar contraction. Susceptibility to this syndrome is transmitted as an autosomal dominant trait, and in some cases,
calcium channels are abnormal [75].
NEUROLEPTIC MALIGNANT SYNDROME
This syndrome is a potentially life-threatening idiosyncratic reaction to neuroleptic drugs [48 , 84]. The clinical triad consists of (1)
hyperthermia, usually with other autonomic dysfunctions such as tachycardia, falls in blood pressure, and diaphoresis; (2)
extrapyramidal signs, usually increased muscle tone (rigidity) with dystonia, often accompanied by elevated creatinine kinase; and
(3) altered mental status, such as inattention, agitation, and confusion. Clinical manifestations usually occur abruptly at therapeutic
levels of neuroleptics, with all of the symptoms fully manifest within 24 hours and reaching a maximum within 72 hours. A
differential diagnosis is provided in Table 17-3. This syndrome, which has also been described following the abrupt withdrawal of
levodopa [63 , 107] and is more frequent when neuroleptics are used to treat the psychiatric manifestations of NMDA receptor
antibody encephalitis [121], can be fatal in about 12% of cases [215].
SEROTONIN SYNDROME
This syndrome, described with the use of drugs acting on the serotonin system, is similar to the previous one but usually with milder
fever and a greater tendency to develop myoclonus, ocular clonus, and hyperreflexia [39 , 227] (Table 17-3). It is thought to arise
from 5HT1A and 5HT2 receptor stimulation [227].
Poikilothermia
Poikilothermia is the fluctuation of >2°C in body temperature following ambient temperature [6]. It is the most common central
neurogenic abnormality of heat regulation in humans. Such patients are unaware of their condition and show no sign of discomfort
or behavioral regulation with thermal stress. Poikilothermia results from posterior hypothalamic lesions [167].

Disturbances of Alertness and Sleep
Larger lesions involve the rostroventral components of the ascending reticular activating system [ 201]. Smaller lesions may cause
more localized dysfunction of structures regulating sleep [226], particularly the suprachiasmatic nucleus, which plays a major role in
the regulation of circadian cycles [23], or the orexin (also known as hypocretin) nucleus in the posterolateral hypothalamus,
important for the control of wakefulness as motivational activation [18 , 133]. The suprachiasmatic nucleus, located in the anterior
hypothalamus, receives afferents from the retina and possibly from the lateral geniculate body and projects mainly to other
hypothalamic nuclei but also to the basal forebrain, thalamus, and periaqueductal gray [ 187]. Circadian rhythms are important for
most hypothalamic functions. For instance, oxytocin, which plays a major role in sexual behavior, is secreted mainly in the early
hours of the night [62].
Coma, Hypersomnia, or Akinetic Mutism
These are occasionally related to posterior hypothalamic or larger lesions [186]. The most common reported causes have been
tumors and Wernicke encephalopathy [179 , 224]. A patient with extreme akinesia after removal of a hypothalamic epidermoid cyst
improved with dopamine receptor agonists [181]. Hypersomnia and coma resulting from midbrain lesions more often than from
hypothalamic lesions, but the hypersomnia often present after traumatic brain injury has been related to damage of histaminergic
tuberomammillary neurons and other hypothalamic neurons [219]. A non-rapid eye movement and rapid eye movement parasomnia
with sleep breathing disorder is associated with antibodies to IgLON5; neuropathology showed neuronal loss and
hyperphosphorylated tau mainly involving the tegmentum of the brainstem and hypothalamus [183]. Although anti-aquaporin 4
antibody disease does not involve the hypothalamus nearly as often as multiple sclerosis does [172], a 21-year-old woman with
subacute onset of hypothermia, hypotension, and hypersomnia was found to have a hypothalamic lesion on MRI and anti-aquaporin
4 antibodies [ 206]; subsequent to the acute event she developed narcolepsy.
Narcolepsy
In the perifornical area of the posterolateral hypothalamus, there is a cluster of cells that secrete orexin A and B, peptides that
mediate wakefulness, facilitate feeding behavior, and contribute to motivational activation [18 , 133]. A lack of orexins

### [Page 486 begins | PDF 681]

causes narcolepsy type 1, characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness and cataplexy, accompanied by sleep-wake
symptoms, such as hallucinations, sleep paralysis, and disturbed sleep [17]. The similar HLA type (DQB1*06:02) of the patients [61
], the onset of symptoms in relation to likely autoimmune events, such as following the 2009 H1N1 outbreak in China [80] and the
coexistence with movement disorders in children [ 166] indicate that the damage of these cells is of an autoimmune nature.
Insomnia
Fewer than 10 cases that implicate the anterior hypothalamus have been reported [167]. However, some insomniacs may have
increased production of stress-related hormones [223]. Orexin receptor antagonists reduce insomnia [86].
Circadian Abnormalities
Loss of neurons in the suprachiasmatic nucleus occurs in Alzheimer disease, attended by phase advance and reduced period and
amplitude of the sleep cycles, as well as increased variability and decreased stability of the rhythm [125 , 146]. Similar changes can be
observed in older individuals [125 , 225]. Loss of circadian rhythmicity has also been described with lesions in the region of the
suprachiasmatic nucleus, including optic glioma [45 , 148].
Autonomic Disturbances
Sympathetic areas tend to be ventromedial and posterior. Stimulation of these areas causes hypertension, pupillary dilation,
tachycardia, vasoconstriction of vascular beds, vasodilation of muscular beds, and increased cardiac contractility in association with
the expression of rage or fear [70 , 110 , 130 , 186]. Hypotension in multiple system atrophy may be, in part, related to neuronal loss in
several hypothalamic nuclei, mainly in the paraventricular nucleus [25 , 49].
Parasympathetic areas tend to be paraventricular or lateral, and anterior. Stimulation of these areas causes pupillary constriction.
Stimulation of the anterior parasympathetic areas causes hypotension and bradycardia, whereas stimulation of the posterior
parasympathetic areas causes only increased blood flow through the bowel and decreased blood flow in skeletal muscle [167].
Although the hypothalamus seems to contribute to the control of micturition in humans, urinary incontinence is not described as a
symptom of isolated hypothalamic damage [32].

Cardiac Manifestations
Hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, electrocardiogram (ECG) abnormalities simulating myocardial infarction, or even myocardial
infarction in a nonvascular pattern may follow subarachnoid or intraventricular hemorrhages, particularly those due to ruptured
anterior communicating artery aneurysm [209], but can be observed with other causes of hypothalamic dysfunction, including
hydrocephalus [97]. The cardiac damage is mediated by an outpouring of catecholamines. Chronic heart failure with chronic stress
may be mediated by the paraventricular nucleus [21].
Respiratory Abnormalities
Pulmonary edema and hemorrhage can result from acute hypothalamic damage (hemorrhage, head trauma). Sudden dysfunction of
the parasympathetic region in the anterior hypothalamus, with consequent hypertension, left heart strain, and loss of pulmonary
surfactant, may explain the clinical picture [136 , 167]. A lack of hyperpnea despite marked hypoxia has been reported in patients with
hypothalamic lesions from COVID-19 [162], but normal respiratory drive despite hypoxia has been ubiquitously reported in this
infection.
Gastrointestinal Abnormalities
Acute hypothalamic lesions (trauma, encephalitis, acute multiple sclerosis, hemorrhage, infarction, abscess, meningitis) can cause
gastrointestinal ulceration. Neurogenic ulcers are most often located in the lower esophagus, otherwise an uncommon site for
ulceration. Neurogenic ulcers may be caused by acute lesions anywhere in the neuraxis, from the anterior hypothalamic region to the
dorsal nucleus of the vagus or even in the spinal cord. Although the hypothalamus is activated during emesis, there is no evidence
that hypothalamic damage alters the emetic reflex [93]. Emesis is a prominent feature of the epileptic syndrome in children called
“autonomic seizures and autonomic status epilepticus” or “Panayiotopoulos syndrome” [159]. In a typical presentation, the child,
fully conscious, able to speak and understand, complains “I feel sick,” looks pale, and vomits. Other autonomic symptoms may
follow, as well as a generalized seizure. Although the electroencephalogram (EEG) often shows occipital spikes, Panayiotopoulos is
of the opinion that the hypothalamus is involved in the genesis of this syndrome [159].
Diencephalic Epilepsy
This term refers to episodes of hypertension, tachycardia, flushing, salivation, sweating, and oscillations in temperature with
preserved alertness, but with the behavioral and affective responses appropriate to the altered autonomic response [167]. The EEG
may be abnormal in half the cases, showing slowing but seldom the paroxysmal dysrhythmias characteristic of most forms of
epilepsy. About half the patients have responded to anticonvulsants. Although autonomic disturbances are common in many types of
seizures,

the clinical picture described earlier has been found with third ventricular tumors or third ventricular
dilation caused by hydrocephalus [136 , 167].
Unilateral Anhidrosis or Hyperhidrosis
Unilateral hypothalamic lesions may cause ipsilateral anhidrosis of the body, which is generally incomplete. An ipsilateral Horner
syndrome is often present in these cases. Dysfunction of the sympathetic centers in the posterior hypothalamus may be responsible
for these findings. Transient hyperhidrosis contralateral to large cerebral infarcts has also been described [117]. No associated
autonomic dysfunction was present. In at least one of these cases, the ipsilateral pupil was smaller and the patient was febrile,
raising the possibility that the finding may actually represent relative anhidrosis on the side ipsilateral to the infarct. Generalized or
segmental hypohidrosis or anhidrosis may be seen with central nervous system conditions, such as Shy-Drager syndrome
(multisystem atrophy with autonomic failure), Parkinson disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord disease, stroke, or thalamotomy or
be idiopathic [41 , 72]. Segmental anhidrosis or hyperhidrosis, involving only one portion of the body, such as an arm [114] or one
side of the chest [215], is likely a manifestation of segmental sympathetic rather than hypothalamic pathology.
Disturbances of Water Balance
Hypothalamic osmoreceptors are in the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei or their proximity. It has been postulated that
intracellular dehydration, manifested by increased intracellular sodium concentration, or extracellular dehydration, manifested by
increased angiotensin II concentration in the hypothalamic blood, stimulate these osmoreceptors, which, in turn, elicit the release of
ADH by the large cells of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei. By contrast, when the intravascular volume increases,
peripheral volume receptors in the large veins and left atrium mediate inhibition of ADH secretion [167].
The lateral hypothalamus, classically considered the drinking center, contains osmoreceptors but may also influence drinking

behavior by causing general excitability of the region. In experimental animals, destructive lesions of the lateral hypothalamus cause
adipsia (reduced water intake), but not enough to result in dehydration. By contrast, destructive lesions of the ventromedial nuclei
may cause hyperdipsia.
Diabetes Insipidus (Decreased ADH Release but Normal Thirst)
Diabetes insipidus refers to the excretion of a large volume of dilute urine. It can be caused by hypothalamic dysfunction, the only
type we review in this chapter, but also by excessive water intake (hyperdipsia) and kidney disease [175]. Gestational diabetes
insipidus may be related to hypothalamic dysfunction or be due to excessive vasopressinase, a placental enzyme that degrades
arginine vasopressin, but not 1-deamino-8-D-arginine vasopressin, which can be used as therapy [28 , 101 , 169].
In central or hypothalamic diabetes insipidus, although lack of ADH prevents water reabsorption in the distal tubule, with
consequent excretion of a large volume of dilute urine, an intact thirst mechanism induces water intake, thereby preventing
hypernatremia. Diabetes insipidus results from involvement of at least 90% of the large neurons in the supraoptic and
paraventricular nuclei [ 136 , 167]. Except for the familial variety, the lesion often affects the supraopticohypophysial tract rather than
the neuronal bodies themselves. In such cases, the disorder is often transient.
Diabetes insipidus may be caused by granulomas (sarcoidosis, meningovascular syphilis, histiocytosis), germinomas, vascular
lesions, trauma, meningoencephalitis, or by autoimmune damage of the pituitary or hypothalamus (autoimmune or lymphocytic
hypophysitis) [53 , 132 , 188 , 200 , 208]. Anxiety, alcohol, phenytoin, and anticholinergic agents reduce the secretion of ADH. In rare
cases, diabetes insipidus may be familial, such as in Wolfram syndrome 1 (WS1), an autosomal recessive disorder characterized by
DIDMOAD (diabetes insipidus, diabetes mellitus, optic atrophy, and deafness) syndrome, caused by a mutation in the WFS1 gene
on chromosome 4p16.1 [135]. In other families, diabetes insipidus has been associated with mutations in the vasopressin region of
chromosome 20 [106 , 174].
Essential Hypernatremia (Decreased ADH Release with Absence of Thirst)
Diagnosis of this rare syndrome requires (1) hypernatremia unaccompanied by a corresponding fluid deficiency, (2) preserved renal
responsiveness to ADH, (3) impaired secretion of ADH with hypernatremia, and (4) the absence of thirst despite preserved
conscious behavior [9 , 79 , 136 , 167]. Some patients with the syndrome have a remarkable tolerance to hypernatremia, to the point of
developing water intoxication when the condition is treated. Sodium levels reaching 170 mEq per L, however, are attended by
muscle cramping, tenderness and weakness, fever, anorexia, paranoia, and lethargy. Lesions causing this syndrome have affected the
tuberal region or the entire hypothalamus [59]. The regulation of atrial natriuretic peptide may be abnormal in some patients with a
similar metabolic derangement [99]. Other patients have excessive renal responsiveness to ADH [ 57].

Inappropriate Secretion of ADH (SIADH) (Elevated ADH Release with Normal Thirst)
This syndrome [47 , 98 , 136 , 167] is characterized by (1) serum hyposmolarity (<280 mOsm/kg) and hyponatremia (<135 mEq/L), (2)
normal renal excretion of sodium, and (3) inappropriately high urine osmolality without body fluid depletion. Renal and adrenal
function are normal. Serum levels of ADH are elevated. The patient has anorexia, nausea, vomiting, and irritability that may
progress to paranoid delusions and generalized seizures when the serum sodium falls below 110 mEq per L. Although SIADH is
more often due to extrahypothalamic causes, partial damage of the supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei or neighboring areas may
cause the syndrome. Such damage may be due to trauma; subarachnoid hemorrhage; hydrocephalus; tumors; meningitis;
encephalitis; autoimmune disorders, such as anti-LGI1 antibodies [95]; or drugs, particularly vincristine, chlorpropamide,
cyclophosphamide, carbamazepine, and chlorpromazine. Production of ADH by a tumor (e.g., carcinoma of the lung) or
inflammatory tissue outside the hypothalamus may also cause the syndrome, which has also been linked to myxedema, cardiac
failure, nonneoplastic pulmonary disease, and porphyria and other peripheral neuropathies. Impairment of peripheral afferent
inhibitory pathways carrying information from the volume receptors to the hypothalamus has been invoked to explain SIADH with
polyneuropathies [167]. Often, hyponatremia with hypothalamic-pituitary disease is due to polydipsia with normal ADH levels [204].
Reset Osmostat Hyponatremia
Criteria for this diagnosis [12] include normovolemic hypotonic hyponatremia; normal renal, adrenal, and thyroid function; ability to
concentrate the urine when serum tonicity is raised above the reset level of serum osmolality; ability to excrete a water load; and
maintenance of normal sodium balance without correction of hyponatremia during salt loading.
Primary Polydipsia or Hyperdipsia (Excessive Water Drinking in the Absence of Hypovolemia or Hypernatremia)


<!-- PDF page 684 -->

Patients with this disturbance [ 167] may drink in response to (1) conditioned behavior (“beer drinker's hyponatremia,” “tea party
epilepsy”) or other psychogenic factors; (2) hyperangiotensinemia, found in renal patients with thirst despite a normal electrolyte
balance maintained with hemodialysis; or (3) rarely, hypothalamic disease. In the last case, drinking often compensates for mild
diabetes insipidus.
Disturbances of Caloric Balance and Feeding Behavior
The hypothalamus participates in feeding behavior through carefully tuned mechanisms [4 , 7 , 8 , 66 , 230 , 233]. For instance, neurons in
the arcuate and paraventricular nuclei are rich in AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a sensor of the cellular AMP/ATP ratio
and, therefore, a gauge of cellular metabolism [ 100]. Feeding, hyperglycemia, and the anorexigenic hormones (insulin and leptin)
decrease AMPK activity in hypothalamic nuclei. Conversely, fasting, hypoglycemia, and the stomach-derived orexigenic hormone
(ghrelin) increase hypothalamic AMPK, thus stimulating feeding behavior.
Although disturbances of feeding behavior are not uncommon with hypothalamic lesions, they have been reported also with frontal
or temporal lesions, particularly in the right hemisphere [217].
Obesity
Lesions in the ventromedial portion of the hypothalamus may cause obesity [19]. Characteristically, such patients are hyperphagic
(bulimic) until a higher body weight is reached, at which point they maintain a stable body weight unless the lesion progresses. The
feeding behavior of patients with hypothalamic lesions resembles that of obese individuals with no demonstrable lesions: compared
to normal individuals, patients with hypothalamic lesions (1) eat only a slight excess of food each day, (2) are less active, (3) eat
fewer meals each day, (4) eat more at each meal, (5) eat more quickly, (6) eat more of a good-tasting food, (7) eat more when food
is easily accessible, and (8) react more emotionally and are appeased by food intake [136]. Obesity after ventromedial lesions may
result from affection of the catecholaminergic pathways coursing in this region, rather than from destruction of the nuclei
themselves. Most frequent lesions include craniopharyngioma, pituitary adenoma, surgery for the removal of these tumors, other
types of trauma, encephalitis (infectious and autoimmune), and vascular lesions of the base of the brain [35 , 144 , 206].
Chronic caloric excess can lead to hypothalamic microinflammation, which, in turn, participates in the development and progression
of metabolic syndrome disorders, such as obesity, glucose intolerance, and hypertension [77 , 212 , 213]. Compulsive eating may be
observed in frontotemporal dementia, likely due to hypothalamic involvement [2 , 164], but also in the absence of detectable brain
lesions. Occasionally, compulsive eating may respond to anticonvulsant therapy [74]. Impaired secretion of cholecystokinin,
corrected by tricyclic antidepressants, has been

### [Page 489 begins | PDF 684]

described in bulimia nervosa [67]. Antidepressants may work
through the stimulation of hypothalamic serotoninergic receptors, which reduces food intake, particularly of carbohydrates, and
increases energy expenditure, thus reducing weight [120]. A large number of identified genetic disorders coursing with obesity are
thought to be associated with hypothalamic dysfunction; 84 are listed in the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database
at the time of this writing [153]. In others, the genetic basis is still unknown. The following obesity syndromes are thought to be due
to hypothalamic dysfunction.
KLEINE-LEVIN SYNDROME
This is a rare variety of compulsive eating behavior in adolescent males, characterized by episodes of hyperphagia, with or without
excess appetite; periodic hypersomnolence; hyperactivity when awake; and behavioral disturbances, particularly hypersexuality and
exhibitionism. Although traditionally considered a hypothalamic derangement, medial thalamic pathology was reported in one case [
40] and hypopigmentation of the substantia nigra and locus ceruleus in another [ 112]. Because a viral illness preceded the onset of
the syndrome in some cases and an association has been described with the HLA-DQB1*0201 allele, it has been postulated to be
autoimmune in origin [52]. The disorder usually disappears during the third decade. Endocrinologic evaluation has failed to show
abnormalities during the periods of abnormal behavior [140]. Metabolism has been reported to be increased in several brain
structures, including the medial thalamus, but not the hypothalamus [51]; by contrast, other study reported decreased thalamic and
hypothalamic perfusion [105].
PRADER-WILLI SYNDROME
This syndrome comprises obesity, hypogenitalism, mental retardation, short stature, micromelia, and a tendency to develop diabetes
mellitus [92]. Affected infants tend to be hypotonic (neonatal hypotonia), somnolent, and eat little, but between 6 months and 2
years of age, they begin to eat in excess and become obese. They lack normal hypothalamic responses to disgusting food [31].
Abnormal luteinizing hormone-releasing neurons are thought to be responsible for the decreased levels of sex hormones, resulting in
nondescended testes, but primary testicular dysfunction may account for undersized sex organs and insufficient growth during


<!-- PDF page 685 -->

puberty [ 88]. A lack of growth hormone-releasing hormone may also contribute to the short stature of patients with Prader-Willi
syndrome [50]. In addition, they may have aberrant control of body temperature and daytime hypersomnolence. The number of
oxytocin neurons—the putative satiety neurons—in the hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus is markedly decreased in Prader-Willi
syndrome. This is presumed to be the basis of the insatiable hunger and obesity of patients with the syndrome [126]. It is caused by a
lack of paternal genetic information at 15q11-q13, due to impaired paternal imprinting of several genes [71 , 102].
RAPID-ONSET OBESITY WITH HYPOTHALAMIC DYSFUNCTION, HYPOVENTILATION, AND AUTONOMIC DYSREGULATION
(ROHHAD)
Fewer than 100 cases are described for this disorder, which is similar to the Prader-Willi syndrome, but differs in a later onset,
typically when a 2- to 7-year-old previously healthy child with normal neurocognitive development develops rapid-onset obesity.
Later, a full hypothalamic syndrome ensues, with disordered water balance, hyperprolactinemia, growth hormone and adrenal
insufficiency, hypothyroidism, alveolar hypoventilation (which unless treated will result in cognitive impairment), and autonomic
dysregulation [15]. It is thought to be genetically determined, but candidate genes have not been identified to date [15 , 173].
LAURENCE-MOON-BARDET-BIEDL SYNDROME
This syndrome comprises obesity, hypogonadism, mental deficiency, retinitis pigmentosa, and polydactyly [ 73 , 136]. Diabetes
insipidus and renal failure are often present. The condition is transmitted as an autosomal recessive trait, genetically heterogeneous,
with at least four loci located to date. In most families, the defect is linked to 11q13 [20]. Hypothalamic lesions have not been found
[228].
Emaciation
DIENCEPHALIC SYNDROME OF INFANCY
This syndrome is a distinct clinical condition characterized by emaciation, with loss of subcutaneous fat, pallor, motor overactivity,
and an inappropriately jovial behavior [136 , 167]. Progressive emaciation occurs despite normal food intake. Nystagmus, optic
atrophy, and tremor are less frequently encountered manifestations. Growth hormone levels may be high. The syndrome usually
appears in boys aged 3 to 12 months and is caused by a slow-growing astrocytoma of the anterior hypothalamus or optic nerve.
Children who survive beyond their second year become obese and irritable.
LATERAL HYPOTHALAMIC SYNDROME
Few case reports deal with lateral hypothalamic lesions causing aphagia, cachexia, and death [ 167]. Multiple sclerosis [103], tumors [
11], and trauma

### [Page 490 begins | PDF 685]

have been implicated. Weight loss in Huntington chorea has been ascribed to neuronal loss in
the lateral tuberal nucleus [113]. An increased cortisol secretion has also been documented in this disorder [14].
ANOREXIA NERVOSA
Anorexia nervosa is a psychiatric disorder characterized by altered body image, persistent food restriction, and low body weight [42 ,
189]. Dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis includes hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism, growth hormone resistance,
hypercortisolemia, nonthyroidal illness syndrome, hyponatremia, and hypooxytocinaemia. Serum levels of leptin, an anorexigenic
adipokine, are suppressed, and levels of ghrelin, an orexigenic gut peptide, are elevated; however, levels of peptide YY, an
anorexigenic gut peptide, are paradoxically elevated. Although the syndrome suggests hypothalamic dysfunction, in most cases, no
lesions have been found in the hypothalamus, but, as expected, ghrelin is increased in the pituitary [142 , 182].
Disturbances of Reproductive Functions
Hypothalamic lesions frequently alter reproductive functions both by decreasing gonadotropin substances that exert a trophic effect
on sexual organs and by altering the neural mechanisms of intercourse. For instance, the paraventricular nucleus plays an important
role in penile erection [ 10]. Several hypothalamic nuclei, particularly the GABA-containing sexually dimorphic or intermediate
nucleus, have been found in young adults to be twice as large in men as in women, but the function of these nuclei is still unclear [65
].
Hypogonadotropin Hypogonadism
This type of hypogonadism is often genetically determined, occasionally associated with hyposmia as part of the Kallmann
syndrome [218], but may follow any hypothalamic or pituitary lesion [ 136 , 229] or be linked to genetic variants [ 211]. It is manifested


<!-- PDF page 686 -->

by amenorrhea or male gonadal dysfunction. When no lesion is found, the condition in women is termed functional hypothalamic
amenorrhea, which has an endocrinologic pattern closely resembling the findings in depression (decreased reproductive hormones,
increased growth hormone, and cortisol) [ 26 , 143]. Hypothalamic hypogonadism has been documented in individuals subjected to
extreme exercise programs, such as marathon runners [129]. More frequent reproductive dysfunction in women with epilepsy may
reflect hypothalamic-pituitary changes due to the disease and the effect of anticonvulsant medications [96].
Nonpuerperal Galactorrhea
PROLACTIN-SECRETING PITUITARY TUMORS
About one-third of chromophobe adenomas secrete prolactin. When present in several family members, adenomas may be caused by
aryl hydrocarbon receptor interacting protein (AIP) gene mutations, an autosomal dominant disease with incomplete penetrance [85].
STRUCTURAL DAMAGE TO THE INFUNDIBULUM OR HYPOTHALAMUS
Such damage may interrupt the dopaminergic pathway that inhibits prolactin secretion by the pituitary. This pathway originates in
the arcuate nucleus [136].
OTHER CAUSES
Other causes of nonpuerperal galactorrhea include irritative lesions of the anterior chest wall, thoracic spinal cord lesions,
neuroleptic and contraceptive drugs, and hypothyroidism [136].
Precocious Puberty
Although generally idiopathic [ 58], precocious puberty may be due to hypothalamic disease, most often hypothalamic hamartomas
of the pedunculated type [43 , 124]. These are congenital malformations formed by heterotopic mature neurons and glial cells in the
tuber cinereum, between the mammillary bodies and median eminence of the hypothalamus [ 36 , 178]. Precocious puberty has also
been associated with hypothalamic germinomas [152] and with mutations in the imprinted gene MKRN3[ 1].
Excessive or Uncontrollable Sexual Behavior
Occasionally, this behavior may be a consequence of lesions of the caudal hypothalamus [ 168].

Disturbances of Memory
The mammillary bodies are frequently involved in Korsakoff amnestic syndrome, and in experimental animals, they seem to play an
important role in memory mechanisms [ 165 , 221]. However, when

### [Page 491 begins | PDF 687]

alcoholics with and without amnesia were
studied, amnesia correlated with neuronal loss in the anterior nucleus of the thalamus, not in the mammillary bodies [81]. Other
dorsal hypothalamic lesions have been accompanied by amnesia [19 , 104]. Bilateral interruption of the mamillothalamic tract is
considered by some to be critical for the production of memory loss by lesions in the ventromedial hypothalamus [108 , 134 , 170], but
congenital absence of the mammillary bodies was found in a case of developmental amnesia [ 180].
Disturbances of Emotional Behavior and Affect
Rage and Fear: Inappropriately Disinhibited Behavior
When caused by hypothalamic lesions, rage and fear occur in episodic outbursts, usually triggered by a threatening or frustrating
event (such as restraint or a delay in feeding) and are part of a fully coordinated behavioral response with an intense autonomic
component [116 , 160 , 167]. Between the outbursts, the behavior is normal, and the patient may realize the inappropriateness of such
behavior and apologize for it. Attacks of rage may also result from lesions of the orbitofrontal cortex, septal region, or temporal lobe
[5]. When the hypothalamus is responsible, the ventromedial region is usually involved. By contrast, stimulation of the posterior
“sympathetic” area elicits responses of fear and horror [186]. Some patients with hypothalamic lesions have the uninhibited behavior
thought to be characteristic of orbitofrontal lesions [137]. Hypothalamic hormones play a role in modulating social interactions.
Oxytocin secretion, for instance, seems to enhance trusting on other people [203].
Apathy, Chronic Fatigue
Apathy may follow lesions of the posterior or lateral hypothalamus. Bilateral stereotactic lesions in this area have been used to treat
unmanageable aggressive behavior due to brain pathology [186]. Although some cases of the chronic fatigue syndrome have been
chalked to dysfunction of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, including mild hypocortisolism, this axis is normal in
patients studied early in the process [ 44].
Depression
Although there is no evidence that depression is caused by hypothalamic dysfunction, many depressed persons have an excessively
active HPA axis [234]. Postmortem studies after suicides have shown increased numbers of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)
neurons and increased vasopressin expression in the paraventricular nucleus, increased CRH in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), and
decreased CRH receptors in the frontal cortex. Challenge with dexamethasone and CRH is the most sensitive test available to detect
altered HPA system regulation in depression [163].
Gelastic Seizures
Seizures accompanied by involuntary laughter (gelastic seizures) have been described in association with precocious puberty [36].
Hypothalamic hamartoma, particularly of the sessile or intrahypothalamic type, at the level of the mammillary bodies [161], is most
often the cause and origin of the seizures [43 , 116 , 124 , 213]. The patient may stare and giggle briefly, without any other motor
manifestation. Crying or sobbing seizures may alternate with the gelastic spells in the same patient. Other patients only have an
interior feeling of pressure to laugh, without overt manifestations [ 205]. Hypothalamic hamartomas often cause executive function
disorders (“frontal lobe syndrome”) [116 , 160] and are rarely part of the Pallister-Hall syndrome (hypothalamic hamartoma,
polysyndactyly, imperforate anus, bifid epiglottis, and renal abnormalities), which is an autosomal dominant disorder due to a
mutation of the GLI3 gene on chromosome 7 [ 78]. Gelastic seizures have also been described with cortical dysplasia in the cingulate
gyrus [141].
Headache
In one of the seven patients with a pituitary tumor, the presenting complaint is headache (Video 17-1). This is usually bitemporal or
bifrontal, behind the eyes, and is thought to be due to compression of the diaphragma sellae [136]. Sudden worsening of headache,
particularly of a retroorbital nature, in a patient with a pituitary tumor should raise the diagnostic suspicion of pituitary apoplexy,
discussed earlier. Headache is more frequent with pituitary abscesses, a rare condition, than with tumors [222].

Episodic Headaches
CLUSTER HEADACHE
This is thought to involve a synchronized abnormal activity in the hypothalamus, trigeminovascular system, and autonomic nervous
system [89 , 127]. The hypothalamus appears to play a fundamental role in the generation of a permissive state that allows the
initiation of an episode. Increased activation to noxious stimuli or perfusion in the posteromedial hypothalamus, dorsal to the
mammillary bodies, has been documented with functional MRI or positron emission tomography (PET) at baseline

### [Page 492 begins | PDF 688]

or during some episodes of cluster headache, paroxysmal hemicrania or the type of headache known as “short-lasting
unilateral neuralgiform headache attacks with conjunctival injection and tearing” (SUNCT) [184 , 192]. Connectivity of this area with
the salience network may be decreased [171]. Deep brain stimulation of this or closely related areas has resulted in the improvement
of patients with these disorders [3 , 60 , 122].
HYPNIC HEADACHE
In hypnic headache, characterized by recurring headache attacks developing only during sleep, generally in people older than 50
years, there is a decreased volume of the posterior hypothalamus [ 90 , 118].
MIGRAINE
In migraine, the hypothalamus shows altered functional coupling with the spinal trigeminal nuclei and the region of the migraine
generator, that is, the dorsal rostral pons, as well as increased activation to painful trigeminal stimulation during the premonitory
phase of migraine, about 48 hours before the acute attack [ 145 , 191 , 192 , 193].
Chronic Pain
The HPA axis seems to be dysfunctional in many patients with chronic pain syndromes, such as fibromyalgia [30].
Impaired Visual Acuity, Visual Field Defects
Some patients with pituitary adenoma present with visual complaints, and, before MRI was widely available, about 15% or more
have decreased visual acuity or field defects on formal testing [91]. Bitemporal defects, related to compression of the inferior aspect
of the chiasm, are most common. However, asymmetric tumor growth may cause preferential involvement of one eye, with
unilateral blindness, or of the optic tract, with consequent homonymous hemianopia. The position of the chiasm in relation to the
sella also determines the pattern of the visual field defect. The chiasm lies over the sella in 80% of brains, over the tuberculum sellae
in 9% of brains (prefixed chiasm), and over the dorsum sellae in 11% of brains (postfixed chiasm) [27]. About 6% of patients have
central or temporal scotomas, which may pass unnoticed if only the periphery of the visual field is tested.
Other tumors characteristic of the region, such as craniopharyngioma, may cause visual impairment [144 , 150].
Optic glioma of childhood is accompanied by signs of hypothalamic dysfunction in about one-third of cases [94]. These patients
present with impaired visual acuity, which may remain stable, and optic disc pallor. For more details on the localization of visual
pathway disturbances, see Chapter 7.
Diplopia, Pupillary Changes
Diplopia with hypothalamic-pituitary tumors is rare unless (1) the tumor is large, (2) it becomes suddenly enlarged by ischemia or
hemorrhage into it (pituitary apoplexy) [29], or (3) it involves primarily the cavernous sinus (carotid aneurysm, metastatic tumors).
Tumors extending laterally from the sella tend to cause dysfunction of the oculomotor nerve, expressed by ptosis and adduction
weakness. Unilateral hypothalamic lesions may betray their presence by causing an ipsilateral Horner syndrome with miosis.
Pituitary apoplexy causes headache or retroorbital pain related to compression of the ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve,
vomiting, visual field defects or reduced acuity, and ocular motor paresis, most often of the oculomotor nerve, located more
medially in the cavernous sinus [29]. Some patients are confused, most often from the systemic metabolic derangement that often
attends pituitary infarction of hemorrhage or from compression of the hypothalamic region. Seldom, other causes are at work, such
as nonconvulsive status epilepticus [46]. Rarely, some of the symptoms may be related to carotid compression [231]. Often due to
hemorrhage within a pituitary adenoma, infarction of the tumor with sudden expansion is also frequent [37 , 56 , 82 , 119 , 231]. It has
been reported after hormonal stimulation, either physiologic or in the course of endocrine testing [ 56 , 119]. When the cause is an
ischemic prolactinoma, rapid improvement may result from bromocriptine administration. Most often, this condition requires swift

surgery [29]. For more details on the localization of ocular motor disturbances, see Chapter 8.
