Spinal Cord
Anatomy of the Spinal Cord
Gross Anatomy and Relationship to Vertebral Levels
Anchored to the dura by the thick bands of pia-arachnoid of the dentate (denticulate) ligaments, the spinal cord extends from the
level of the cranial border of the atlas, where it is continuous with the medulla oblongata, to the lower border of the first lumbar
vertebra [18 , 22 , 26 , 37 , 44]. During early fetal development, the spinal cord extends to the lower end of the sacrum, but at birth, it
extends only as far as the upper border of the third lumbar vertebra. At 20 weeks' gestational age, the conus medullaris
is at the L4-L5 level. By 40 weeks' gestational age or at term, the conus medullaris is at the L3 level. By the age of 2 months, the
conus has reached the adult L1-L2 level [6].
The average length of the spinal cord is approximately 45 cm in an adult male and 42 to 43 cm in an adult female. The
corresponding average length of the spinal column is 70 cm. Cylindrical and flattened in a dorsoventral direction, the spinal cord
occupies only one-third to one-half of the vertebral canal and has a cervical and lumbar enlargement. The cervical enlargement
corresponds to the segmental innervation of the upper extremities and extends from C5 to T1 spinal levels, whereas the lumbar
enlargement corresponding to the innervation of the lower extremities extends from L3 to S2. Below the lumbar enlargement, the
spinal cord narrows, ending as the conus medullaris. From the conus medullaris, a fine pial thread known as the filum terminale
traverses to the first coccygeal segment.
Although the spinal cord is a continuous and non-segmental structure, the 31 pairs of nerves originating from it portray a segmental
appearance. On this basis, the spinal cord is considered to have 31 segments analogous to the spinal nerves (8 cervical, 12 thoracic,
5 lumbar, 5 sacral, and 1 coccygeal). Due to the different growth rates of the spinal cord and the spinal column, the more caudal
(lumbar and sacral) spinal nerve roots must travel a considerable distance in the subarachnoid space before reaching their
corresponding intervertebral foramina. This lower group of nerve roots congregates around the filum terminale in the spinal theca
and is known as the cauda equina.
At the cervical level, the relation between the spine and the spinal cord roughly corresponds. However, the thoracic spinal cord is
located at the spinal canal formed by the first to the eighth (T1-T8) thoracic vertebrae, the lumbar spinal cord at the canal formed
between the ninth and the eleventh (T9-T11) thoracic vertebrae, and the sacral spinal cord at the canal formed between the twelfth
thoracic (T12) and the second lumbar (L2) vertebrae. In the cervical region, the spinous process of a given vertebra matches the
level of the corresponding spinal cord segment; in the upper thoracic region, there is a discrepancy of two segments (e.g., the fourth
thoracic spinal segment overlies the sixth), and in the lower thoracic region, there is a discrepancy of three segments. The eleventh
thoracic spinous process overlies the third lumbar cord segment, and the twelfth overlies the first sacral cord segment.
The external surface of the spinal cord displays a ventral median fissure and a dorsal median sulcus (continued by a dorsal median
septum), which divide the cord into two symmetric halves. In the posterolateral surface, there is a dorsolateral sulcus marking the
entry of the dorsal nerve roots. In the anterolateral surface, there is a ventrolateral sulcus that is not as well delineated as the other
sulci because the ventral nerve roots emerge as a number of separate nerve twigs.
Cross-Sectional Anatomy of the Spinal Cord
In its cross section (Fig. 5-1), the spinal cord consists of the centrally placed gray matter surrounded by white matter. The gray
matter is butterfly shaped like a modified H, with two lateral columns joined by a transverse commissure. Each lateral column has a
dorsal (posterior) horn lying dorsolaterally and a ventral (anterior) horn lying ventrolaterally. The central canal of the spinal cord,
which is connected to the fourth ventricle, is located in the center of the gray commissure. The size of the gray matter is more
pronounced at the cervical and lumbosacral regions, which innervate the upper and lower limbs, respectively.
In addition, there is an intermediolateral gray column extending from segments T1 through L2, which gives rise to preganglionic
sympathetic autonomic fibers. There is also an intermediolateral zone of gray matter at the second, third, and fourth sacral segments,
which is the source of the sacral portion of the parasympathetic outflow.

The ventral horns have two types of motor neurons: (a) large or alpha motoneurons and (b) small or gamma motor neurons. The
alpha motor neurons innervate the extrafusal muscle fibers of skeletal muscles, and the gamma motor neurons innervate the
intrafusal fibers of the neuromuscular spindles, which are the receptors for the muscle stretch reflexes. There is a somatotopic
organization whereby ventral horn neurons innervating flexor muscles lie dorsal to those neurons innervating extensor muscles.
Moreover, neurons innervating hand muscles lie laterally to those neurons innervating the trunk muscles.
Each half of the spinal cord white matter is separated into three funiculi by the gray matter and the intramedullary portions of the
spinal nerve roots, as follows:
1. The dorsal funiculus: white matter between the dorsomedian and the dorsolateral sulci

Bands of white matter known as the dorsal and ventral white commissures correlate with the gray commissure. The spinal cord
white matter houses the ascending and descending tracts.
Major Ascending and Descending Tracts of the Spinal Cord
Ascending Tracts
Almost all the sensory afferent input to the spinal cord enters by way of the dorsal nerve roots. The central end of a dorsal nerve root
splits into lateral and medial bundles. The finely myelinated or unmyelinated nerve

### [Page 113 begins | PDF 182]

fibers of the lateral
bundle bifurcate into short ascending and descending branches within the zone of Lissauer and end on dorsal horn neurons. The
axons of second-order neurons, with cell bodies presumably in laminae VI and VII, decussate over several segments by way of the
ventral white commissure; they then proceed to the ventrolateral quadrant of the spinal cord and ascend as the lateral spinothalamic
(neospinothalamic) tract to reach the thalamus (ventral posterolateral [VPL] nucleus). These fibers convey pain and temperature
sensation and display a laminar configuration. As a consequence of this laminar arrangement, fibers carrying information from
cervical regions are situated dorsomedially, and those from sacral regions are situated ventrolaterally. There also seems to be a
segregation between pain and temperature fibers, with fibers carrying temperature information situated dorsolaterally to the pain-
carrying fibers.
Pain may also be transmitted by way of the spinoreticulothalamic (paleospinothalamic) system. Fibers from this system have short
axons synapsing in the brainstem reticular formation ending in the intralaminar nuclei of the thalamus. The lateral spinothalamic
tract conveys information that is perceived as sharp and localized pain, whereas the spinoreticulothalamic system is concerned with
poorly localized pain sensation.
Fibers carrying tactile sensibility (light touch) also divide upon entering the zone of Lissauer and end after synapsing with dorsal
horn interneurons. The axons of second-order neurons, whose cell bodies presumably lie in laminae VI and VII, crossover to the
opposite side through the ventral white commissure and ascend as the ventral spinothalamic tract to reach the VPL nucleus of the
thalamus. Light touch is also transmitted by way of the dorsal column-medial lemniscus pathway.
The heavily myelinated fibers of the medial bundle of the dorsal nerve roots pass over the dorsal horn into the dorsal funiculus.
After giving rise to collaterals, which end largely in laminae III and IV, they ascend in the dorsal funiculus. Fibers from the
lowermost part of the body (sacral, lumbar, and the lowest six thoracic levels) are located more medially and constitute the
fasciculus gracilis (Goll column), whereas those coming from the upper part of the body (upper six thoracic and all cervical levels)
occupy a more lateral position and constitute the fasciculus cuneatus (Burdach column). The fasciculus gracilis ends in the nucleus
gracilis of the medulla oblongata. The fasciculus cuneatus also reaches the dorsal surface of the medulla oblongata and terminates in
the nucleus cuneatus. Axons of these two nuclei decussate in the lower medulla and ascend as the medial lemniscus to reach the
VPL nucleus of the thalamus. These fibers carry information concerning discriminative sensation (position sense, vibration sense,
weight perception, discriminative touch, pressure touch, two-point discrimination, stereognosis, and shape and movement
awareness). Other ascending tracts include the dorsal spinocerebellar and ventral spinocerebellar tracts, which transmit
unconscious proprioceptive information from the lower limbs and the inferior half of the body to the cerebellum, and the
cuneocerebellar and rostrocerebellar tracts, which convey similar information from the upper limbs and rostral half of the body.
Descending Tracts
Five descending tracts exert tonic effects on the alpha and gamma motor neurons; these systems are, therefore, important in the
postural control of the limbs. Two of these systems (the vestibulospinal tract and the medial reticulospinal tract) tend to facilitate
the alpha and gamma motor neurons of antigravity muscles, and the other three tracts (corticospinal tract, corticorubrospinal tract,
and lateral reticulospinal tract) inhibit the antigravity muscles and facilitate the antagonists.
Corticospinal Tract
Fibers of the corticospinal pathway arise primarily from somatotopically organized areas from the primary motor cortex, lateral
premotor cortex, and supplementary motor cortex of the contralateral hemisphere. Corticospinal neurons are found primarily in
Brodmann area 4, which occupies the posterior aspect of the precentral gyrus (primary motor cortex or MI). The lateral premotor
and supplementary motor cortices are located in Brodmann area 6. Corticospinal axons also arise from neurons in the primary
sensory cortex in the postcentral gyrus (Brodmann areas 3, 1, and 2), anterior paracentral gyri, superior parietal lobule (Brodmann

areas 5 and 7), and portions of the cingulate gyrus on the medial hemispheric surface. These fibers descend through the corona
radiata, posterior limb of the internal capsule, and ventral aspect of the mesencephalon and pons down to the ventral aspect of the
medulla oblongata, where they form the two large pyramids. Upon reaching the caudal medulla oblongata, ˜90% of the estimated 1
million fibers of each pyramid cross over in an interdigitated manner to descend in a massive projection tract in the lateral funiculus
of the spinal cord known as the lateral corticospinal tract. Fibers in the lateral corticospinal tract extend all the way through the
spinal cord ending in laminae IV through VII and IX. The remainder 10% of fibers that do not decussate descend in the ipsilateral
ventral funiculus as the ventral corticospinal tract, which ends (after crossing in the ventral white commissure) in lamina III of the
cervical and upper thoracic regions.

Motor neurons innervating the axial musculature are situated in an extreme ventromedial sector of lamina IX, whereas those
innervating the intrinsic extremity musculature are clustered within a dorsolateral sector; motor neurons for the limb-girdle
musculature are located in an intermediate position [2]. This ventromedial-dorsolateral gradient of the proximal-distal representation
is also maintained within the intermediate zone (laminae V-VIII) that contains propriospinal neurons. Propriospinal neurons
projecting to the limbs and axial motor neurons project for considerable distances above and below their segmental origin, thereby
influencing large groups of proximal muscles. By contrast, propriospinal neurons projecting to motor neurons innervating the
intrinsic limb muscles project only for short distances above and below the segment of origin, thereby influencing smaller, more
restricted groups of distal muscles [2].
Corticorubrospinal Tract
Cells in Brodmann cortical areas 4, 6, 3, 1, and 2 project to the ipsilateral red nucleus. Axons of some of these rubral cells decussate
and descend through the brainstem tegmentum and lateral funiculus of the spinal cord as the rubrospinal tract.
Lateral Reticulospinal Tract
This tract originates in the medullary reticular formation, chiefly on the ipsilateral side, and descends in the ventrolateral funiculus.
Vestibulospinal Tract
Fibers originating in the lateral vestibular nucleus extend through the entire length of the spinal cord in the anterior aspect of the
lateral funiculus. Fibers from the medial vestibular nucleus extend through the cervical and upper thoracic levels in the ventral
funiculus.

Arterial Supply to the Spinal Cord
The main arterial supply to the spinal cord arises from the anterior spinal artery (running along the anterior median fissure), the
paired posterior (posterolateral) spinal arteries (along the posterolateral sulcus), and the perimedullary plexus interconnecting them.
The vascular supply of the spinal cord (Fig. 5-2) is conceptually divided into extraspinal and intraspinal systems [ 42 , 122 , 153].
Extraspinal System (Extramedullary Arteries)
The lateral spinal arteries, present in early development, give rise to radicular arteries, radiculopial arteries, and radiculomedullary
arteries. The latter, by means of the anterior and posterior spinal arteries, are responsible for most of the blood supply to the spinal
cord. The radiculomedullary arteries are present only at certain segmental levels. Approximately 6 to 10 of them join the anterior
spinal artery, running along the anterior median fissure, and 10 to 23 join the posterior spinal arteries. The anterior

### [Page 115 begins | PDF 184]

spinal artery originating from the anastomosis of two branches from the intracranial vertebral artery extends from the level of
the olivary nucleus to the tip of the conus medullaris, supplying the ventral surface of the medulla oblongata and the anterior two-
thirds of the spinal cord. Sulcal branches of the anterior spinal artery supply the anterior horn, the lateral horn, the central gray
matter, and the basal aspect of the posterior horn. The posterior spinal arteries are paired branches of the intracranial vertebral
artery or the posterior inferior cerebellar arteries. The posterior spinal arteries also extend throughout the length of the spinal cord
and supply the dorsal horns and the dorsal funiculi. This arterial system receives 10 to 20 posterior radicular vessels [42 , 153]. At the
conus medullaris, the anterior and posterior spinal arteries are joined by the anastomosing ansa of the conus medullaris. Anastomotic
radicular arteries, most of them branches of the aorta, also feed both arterial systems at various anatomic levels.
Three main functional spinal cord regions are distinguished according to their unequal blood supply as follows:
1. The upper or cervicothoracic region, which is richly vascularized, embraces the cervical and first two thoracic cord
segments. The first four cervical segments are supplied by the anterior spinal artery and have limited or no radiculomedullary
blood supply. The lower four cervical and the first two thoracic segments receive their blood supply from two to four large
radicular arteries arising from the vertebral and the ascending and deep cervical arteries. The most important of these radicular
arteries, named the artery of the cervical enlargement typically arises between C4 and C8, usually enters the spinal cord with
the seventh and eighth cervical roots. A variable number of radiculomedullary vessels feed the posterior spinal arteries, and
they predominate in the cervical enlargement.
2. The intermediate or midthoracic region, which is poorly vascularized, is supplied by branches of the intercostal arteries and
includes the third through the eighth thoracic segments. It receives a single radiculomedullary artery, which enters with the
sixth, seventh, or eighth thoracic root. There are two to three segmental feeders to the posterior spinal arteries.
3. The lower or thoracolumbosacral region has a rich vascularization and is nourished by radiculomedullary branches of the
intercostal and lumbar arteries. The most important source to the anterior circulation is the great anterior radicular artery of
Adamkiewicz (artery of the lumbar enlargement), which most frequently enters from the left side with the ninth, tenth,
eleventh, or twelfth thoracic (75% of cases between T9 and T12) or first two lumbar nerve roots, seldom from the lumbar
region or higher thoracic segments between T6 and T8. Numerous posterior radicular arteries are also present in this region.
Intraspinal System (Intramedullary Arteries)
Branches of the anterior and posterior spinal arteries form a perimedullary circuitry around the spinal cord. Branches arising from
this plexus supply a substantial amount of white matter and the dorsal horns of the gray matter. The arterial blood supply of the gray
matter is richer than the white matter. Largest branches of the anterior spinal artery (sulcocommissural arteries) enter the ventral
median fissure and supply the gray matter, except for the dorsal horns and the innermost portion of the white matter. The dorsal
horns and funiculi are supplied by the paired posterior spinal arteries, the posterior medullary feeders, and perforating pial branches.
Venous Drainage of the Spinal Cord
Veins draining the spinal cord have a similar distribution to the arteries. The anterior longitudinal trunks consist of anteromedial and
anterolateral veins. Sulcal veins drain the anteromedial aspect of the spinal cord, anterolateral regions drain into the anterolateral
veins, and posterior longitudinal venous trunks drain the dorsal funiculus. Throughout the coronal venous plexus, they are
interconnected. At each spinal cord segment, small radicular veins drain the nerve roots, but at some levels, larger medullary veins
arise from the anterior median spinal vein. There are approximately 10 to 20 anterior medullary veins and an equal number of
posterior medullary veins. The posterior half of the spinal cord drain into the posterior veins, whereas the anterior half drain into the
anterior medullary veins. These medullary veins join with radicular veins and internal and external vertebral plexus to form the
intervertebral vein that drains blood from the spine and spinal cord.

Physiology of the Spinal Cord Circulation
Under normal physiologic conditions, the spinal cord perfusion is maintained through autoregulatory mechanisms similar to those
regulating cerebral blood flow. Spinal cord blood vessels autoregulate in response to changes in systemic arterial blood pressure,
dilating when the PaCO 2 is increased and constricting when it is reduced. Spinal cord perfusion is more directly affected by changes
in systemic blood pressure than brain perfusion. Low peripheral vascular resistance with aortic hypotension diverts aortic outflow
away from spinal cord blood vessels. When peripheral vascular resistance in the lower extremities is high, aortic outflow is diverted
toward the spinal circulation, increasing pressure and, possibly, spinal cord perfusion. If patients have hypertension and increased
peripheral vascular resistance, a column of contrast medium in the aorta during aortography is diverted into the spinal cord
circulation. If the peripheral resistance is decreased, rapid forward flow occurs, and the aorta is virtually emptied of contrast at the
end of one cardiac cycle and none is visible in the spinal arterial circulation.
Covered by the dura mater, arachnoid, and the innermost pia mater, the spinal cord is encased within a bony structure similar to the
skull. Thus, any changes in volume can take place only at the expense of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), blood, or spinal cord tissue
shifts. Damage to the spinal cord typically occurs within 20 to 30 minutes of ischemia and during systemic arterial hypotension with
failure of the autoregulatory response. The resulting ischemic injury affects areas with high metabolic demand, such as the anterior
horn cells and gray matter before, leading to irreversible spinal cord necrosis [122].
Spinal Cord Lesions
Spinal cord syndromes may develop acutely (minutes or hours), subacutely (days or weeks), or chronically (months or even years).
Spinal cord syndromes may be complete or incomplete [48].
Complete Spinal Cord Transection (Transverse Myelopathy)
With complete spinal cord transection (Fig. 5-3), all ascending tracts below the level of the lesion and all descending tracts above
the level of the lesion are interrupted [6 , 38 , 52]. As a result, there is impaired motor and sensory function below the level of spinal
cord damage. More often, the section is incomplete.
Acute onset of transverse myelopathy is often the result of traumatic spine injuries, tumors, multiple sclerosis, or spinal cord
vascular disorders [ 141]. Less commonly, acute transverse myelopathy results from spinal epidural hematomas or abscesses,
autoimmune disorders, herniated intervertebral discs, parainfectious, postvaccinal, or paraneoplastic myelopathies [69]. Etiologies
implicated are summarized in Table 5-1.
Sensory Disturbances
All sensory modalities are impaired below the level of the lesion. Assessment for pin-prick loss below a segmental level is the most
valuable in localizing the lesion. A sensory level may be easily missed unless diligently sought after. With extramedullary cord
lesions, a sensory level may be found many segments below the level of the lesion. The somatotopic distribution in the lateral
spinothalamic tract, with the lowest segments represented more superficially, accounts for this apparent discrepancy. Band-like
radicular pain or segmental paresthesias may also occur at the level of the lesion. With cervical pain, there may be radiation to the
arms; with thoracic pain, there may be radiation to the chest or the abdomen; and if lumbar or sacral, the pain often radiates to the
lower limbs. Localized vertebral pain, accentuated by palpation of the spine or vertebral percussion, may occur with destructive
lesions. Pain worsened by recumbence, which improves when sitting or standing, suggests underlying malignancy.
Impaired pain and temperature sensation below a certain level in the trunk is almost always indicative of spinal cord disease.
However, due to the somatotopic organization of the spinothalamic tracts at higher levels, a lateral medullary or lateral pontine
lesion may, on rare occasions, also account for sensory deficits in the contralateral leg or trunk or both [ 97]. For example, a laterally
placed medullary lesion may damage sacral and lumbar afferent fibers of the lateral spinothalamic tract, sparing the more medial
thoracic and cervical afferent fibers, resulting in sensory loss below a specific lumbar level. Sensory levels with parietal lobe lesions

Motor Disturbances
Paraplegia or quadriplegia below the level of a lesion results from interruption of the descending corticospinal tracts. With acute
lesions, the paralysis is flaccid as a result of spinal cord hypoexcitability, also known as spinal shock. Eventually, hypertonic,
hyperreflexic paraplegia or tetraplegia develops with bilateral Babinski signs, loss of superficial abdominal and

or high spinal cord lesions, whereas flexion at the hips and knees occurs with complete lower spinal cord lesions.

Hemisection of the Spinal Cord (Brown-Séquard Syndrome)
Functional hemisection of the spinal cord results in the Brown-Séquard syndrome (Fig. 5-3) [77], consisting of the following
findings:
Impaired pain and temperature sensation contralateral to the hemisection due to interruption of the crossed spinothalamic
tract. The sensory level is usually one or two segments below (caudal) the level of the lesion.
Ipsilateral loss of proprioceptive function below the level of the lesion due to interruption of the ascending fibers in the
posterior columns (dorsal funiculi). Tactile sensation may be normal or minimally decreased [125].
Ipsilateral spastic weakness with hyperreflexia and Babinski sign caudal to the level of the lesion due to interruption of the
descending corticospinal tract.
Segmental LMN (segmental muscle weakness and atrophy) and sensory signs (segmental anesthesia) at the level of the lesion
due to damage of the anterior horn cells and dorsal rootlets at this level.
Ipsilateral loss of sweating caudal to the level of the lesion due to interruption of descending autonomic fibers in the ventral
funiculus; ipsilateral Horner syndrome, if the lesion is cervical; and ipsilateral hemidiaphragmatic paralysis due to damage
of the upper motor neuron (UMN) pathways for breathing, if the lesion is high cervical.
The causes of the Brown-Séquard syndrome are diverse, and etiologies implicated are listed in Table 5-2.

The spinal cord damage begins centrally spreading centrifugally to involve other spinal cord structures. Decussating spinothalamic
fibers conveying pain and temperature sensation are compromised initially, resulting in thermoanesthesia and analgesia in a “vest-
like” or “suspended” bilateral distribution. There is preservation of soft touch and proprioception (dissociation of sensory loss).
With forward expansion, the ventral horn cells become involved at the level of the lesion, resulting in segmental neurogenic atrophy,
paresis, and areflexia. Lateral expansion results in ipsilateral Horner syndrome (involvement of the ciliospinal center of Budge with
C8-T2 lesions), kyphoscoliosis (involvement of dorsomedian and ventromedian motor nuclei supplying paraspinal muscles), and,
eventually, spastic paralysis below the lesion (corticospinal tract involvement). Dorsal extension disrupts dorsal column function
(ipsilateral position sense and vibratory loss), and with advanced ventrolateral extension, the spinothalamic tract is affected,
resulting in thermoanesthesia and analgesia below the lesional level. As a result of the spinothalamic tract lamination (dorsomedial
cervical sensation and ventrolateral sacral sensation), sacral sensation is spared (sacral sparing). Syringomyelia may also result in
neuropathic arthropathies of the shoulders, elbows, or other joints, as well as a variety of trophic and vasomotor changes [7 , 142].
An acute cervical central spinal cord syndrome may follow severe hyperextension cervical spine injuries [134]. Patients may
suddenly become quadriplegic, but often regain strength in the legs within hours or even minutes. There is urinary retention, patchy
and variable sensory loss below the lesion, and UMN pattern of weakness more pronounced in the arms (“man-in-a-barrel
syndrome”). The muscle stretch reflexes are initially absent. The central cord syndrome results from damage to the central gray
matter and lateral corticospinal tracts at the cervical spinal cord enlargement.

ALS, also known as Charcot disease or Lou Gehrig disease, the most common degenerative motor neuron disease of adulthood, is

cervical cord ipsilateral to the symptomatic side [68 , 109 , 118].

Vascular Disorders of the Spinal Cord and Spinal Canal
Arterial Spinal Cord Infarction
Spinal cord infarction accounts for 1% of all strokes and most result from involvement of the anterior spinal artery territory [41 , 122 ,
132 , 161]. Any spinal cord segment can be involved; however, the lower thoracic segments and conus medullaris are most frequently
compromised. Patients have sudden onset of neurologic deficits, often associated with radicular or “girdle” pains. Loss of motor
function below the level of the lesion develops within minutes to hours. There is impaired bowel and bladder control and
thermoanesthesia and analgesia below the level of the lesion. Position, vibration, and light touch remain intact due to preservation of
the dorsal columns, which are supplied by the posterior spinal arteries. Patients may have burning dysesthesias below the level of
spinal cord injury, likely due to selective neospinothalamic deafferentation and preservation of the posterior columns [151].
Concomitant infarctions of one or more vertebral bodies may be present [ 166]. Spinal cord infarction often occurs in watersheds or
boundary zones where major arterial systems supplying the spinal cord anastomose at their most distal branches; these zones include
the T1-T4 and the L1 spinal cord segments.
In the cervical region, the anterior spinal artery is collateralized at several levels by unpaired medullary arteries arising from the
vertebral arteries and the subclavian arteries. Due to a relative hypovascularity, the midthoracic regions (T4) are most vulnerable to
hypotensive spinal cord infarctions [167]. Hypotensive spinal cord infarctions usually involve the central gray matter [24].
Initially described with syphilitic arteritis, spinal cord infarction may result from aortic dissection, aortic atherosclerosis, acute aortic
thrombosis, surgery of the abdominal aorta or aortic coarctation, cervical spondylosis [64], fracture/dislocation of the spine,
traumatic neck injury [ 62], spinal decompression and stabilization [153], lumbosacral nerve root block [59], fibrocartilaginous
embolism [101], rib resection for sympathectomy [66], thoracoplasty and thoracotomy [127], following repair or thrombosis of spinal
arteriovenous malformation (AVM) [24], pineal surgery in the sitting position [ 24], severe arterial hypotension or cardiac arrest [74],
celiac plexus block [24], decompression sickness [24], antiphospholipid syndrome, and vasculitis [24 , 31 , 63]. Surgical insults to the
spinal cord may result in serious myelopathy [ 43]. Ischemia/reperfusion of the spinal cord may rarely complicate decompression of a
long-standing spinal cord compressive lesion. In a substantial number of cases, no cause of spinal cord infarction is recognized.
Spinal cord infarction in the territory of the posterior spinal arteries results in loss of proprioception and vibration sense below the
level of the lesion and loss of segmental reflexes [ 65]. Less frequently, there is unilateral involvement of the dorsal horn and lateral
column with sparing of the dorsal columns [65 , 72]. Symptom onset is more gradual in cases of hypoxic myelopathy, and with spinal
cord claudication, symptoms are triggered by exertion. Salient features of the major clinical syndromes of spinal cord infarction are
summarized in Table 5-9.
Venous Spinal Cord Infarction
Venous spinal cord infarction results from impaired blood drainage associated with dural arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) or
hypercoagulable states. Because the spinal veins are valveless and drain into the azygos and pelvic venous systems, there is a
potential risk for retrograde embolization of thrombi associated with a number of infectious abdominal disorders, fibrocartilaginous
embolism, or sclerotherapy for esophageal varices [138]. In cases of the decompression sickness (Caisson disease), which typically
involves the white matter of the upper or middle thoracic cord, there is impaired epidural spinal cord venous drainage [50 , 81 , 91].

Spinal cord dural AVFs account for 80% of spinal cord vascular malformations. Dural AVFs arise at the dural nerve root sleeve and

Spinal Canal Hemorrhages
Whether spontaneous or traumatic, spinal canal hemorrhages are unusual. Spinal canal bleeding is subdivided into epidural,

promptly recognized and treated.
Spinal epidural hematomas may result from trauma, hemophilia, thrombocytopenia or other bleeding diatheses, anticoagulant and
fibrinolytic therapy, antiplatelet therapy, liver disease with portal hypertension, epidural vascular malformations, neuraxial
anesthesia, or lumbar puncture [58 , 110 , 164].

Extramedullary Cord Lesions and Their Differentiation from Intramedullary Cord Lesions
Although the clinical distinction between intramedullary and extramedullary spinal cord lesions is never absolute, certain guidelines
are clinically useful (Table 5-11) [45 , 123].
Pain
Oppenheim [108] described three stages of spinal cord compression, characterized by (a) radicular pain and segmental motor and
sensory disruption, (b) incomplete transection (i.e., Brown-Séquard syndrome), and (c) complete cord transection [19].
Pain is an important early red flag manifestation of spinal cord compression and is often categorized as follows:
1. Radicular pain characterized by unilateral, lancinating, dermatomal pain often exacerbated by cough, sneeze, or the
Valsalva maneuver. More common with extradural lesions and unusual with intramedullary lesions, radicular pain may occur
in isolation before the development of dermatomal hypesthesia, segmental muscle paresis, amyotrophy, or fasciculations.
2. Vertebral pain is characterized by localized pain, often associated with focal spine tenderness.

### [Page 126 begins | PDF 196]


Spinal pain is more commonly observed with neoplastic or inflammatory extradural lesions.
3. Funicular (central) pain, commonly observed with intramedullary lesions, is characterized by deep and ill-defined painful
dysesthesias, usually remote from the affected spinal cord level, and attributed to spinothalamic tract or posterior column
dysfunction.
Disturbances of Motor Function
Motor impairment results from involvement of the LMN or UMN or both. Gradually progressive compressive corticospinal tract
lesions often result in spasticity, whereas acute lesions cause a flaccid paresis.


<!-- PDF page 197 -->

UMN signs may be late with intramedullary lesions and early with extramedullary lesions. The coexistence of UMN and LMN signs
suggests an intramedullary process, but do not exclude intradural-extramedullary pathology.
Sensory Disturbances
Paresthesias may follow a radicular or funicular distribution. Patients often have distal “pins-and-needles” sensation, resembling a
polyneuropathy. Others may develop an “ascending” pattern of sensory symptoms. Sensory complaints may occur with or without
objective sensory findings. Descending progression of paresthesias suggests intramedullary lesions; conversely, ascending
progression of paresthesias suggests extramedullary lesions.
A sensory level to pain and temperature must be diligently sought after in all cases of suspected spinal cord disease. However, a
sensory level is not a reliable clue to distinguish intramedullary from extramedullary lesions. For example, a Brown-Séquard
syndrome, although more commonly observed with extramedullary lesions, may also occur with intramedullary pathology.
Dissociated sensory loss and sacral sparing and greater impairment of vibratory sense than position sense characterize
intramedullary lesions.
Disturbances of Sphincter Function
Early loss of sphincter control with associated saddle anesthesia is commonly observed with conus medullaris and cauda equina
tumors. Higher spinal level lesions, whether intramedullary or extramedullary, are often associated with sphincter dysfunction,
especially when the spinal cord damage is extensive and bilateral.
Autonomic Manifestations
A Horner syndrome may result from either extramedullary or intramedullary tumors. Vasomotor and sudomotor abnormalities are
not helpful in distinguishing intramedullary from extramedullary lesions. Sexual dysfunction is unusual.
Localization of Spinal Cord Lesions at Different Levels
Foramen Magnum Syndrome and Lesions of the Upper Cervical Cord
Foramen magnum lesions may result in a complex array of sensory, motor, and neuro-ophthalmologic findings [147]. Suboccipital
pain in the distribution of the greater occipital nerve (C2) and neck stiffness may be an early occurrence. A Lhermitte sign is
indicative of posterior column dysfunction and most often due to multiple sclerosis or cervical spondylosis [ 47]. Occipital
paresthesias and findings indicative of posterior column dysfunction or a syringomyelic pattern of sensory loss may be present.
Numbness and tingling of the fingertips are common. As a result of regional expansion of the pathologic process, in addition to
spastic tetraparesis, long tract sensory findings, and bladder disturbance, there may be palsies of cranial nerves IX-XII. There may
be a “clockwise” pattern of UMN weakness, initially involving the ipsilateral upper extremity, spreading to the ipsilateral lower
extremity, before involving the contralateral lower extremity, and then the upper extremity may be present. This may result in
hemiparesis, triparesis, or quadriparesis. There may be downbeat nystagmus, papilledema, and cerebellar ataxia. Major etiologies
include tumors (e.g., meningiomas, neurofibromas, gliomas, teratomas, metastases), cervical spondylosis, basilar invagination in
Paget disease, syringomyelia, atlantoaxial subluxation, multiple sclerosis, and Chiari I malformation [ 102].
Lesions at the cervicomedullary junction may also result in the unusual combination of contralateral upper extremity paresis and
ipsilateral lower extremity paresis (hemiplegia cruciata). The term cruciate paralysis has also been used to indicate arm weakness
greater than leg weakness in patients with injuries or malformations of the cervicomedullary junction [ 30]. Patients with cruciate
paralysis may also have an “onion-skin” pattern of facial sensory loss, respiratory insufficiency, bladder dysfunction, and other
cranial nerve impairments [ 30].
Compressive lesions of the upper cervical cord (C1-C4) segments may compromise cranial nerve XI function, resulting in
anomalous head position, inadequate contraction and atrophy of the sternocleidomastoid muscle, as well as impaired elevation of the
shoulder toward the ipsilateral ear. Diaphragmatic paralysis results from lesions involving the C3-C5 cord segments, leading to
limited lateral expansion of the lower rib cage during inspiration. Involvement of descending respiratory

False localizing sensory and motor findings, including thoracic sensory levels, proprioceptive sensory loss, paresthesias, and hand

Lesions of the Fifth and Sixth Cervical Segments
Compression of the lower cervical spinal cord results in LMN signs at the corresponding segmental levels and UMN signs below the
level of the lesion. Lesions affecting the C5 and C6 segments results in LMN signs, especially involving the spinati, deltoid, biceps,
brachioradialis, brachialis, pectorals, latissimus dorsi, triceps, and extensor carpi radialis muscles, among others. Spastic paraparesis
of the lower extremities is often present. Diaphragmatic function may be compromised (C5). With C5 segment lesions, the biceps
and the brachioradialis reflexes are either absent or depressed, whereas the triceps reflex and the finger flexor reflex are exaggerated
(the latter two are exaggerated due to corticospinal tract compression at the C5 level). Therefore, C5 segmental lesions may result in
an inverted brachioradialis reflex whereby tapping the radius elicits an exaggerated finger and hand flexion without flexion and
supination of the forearm. With C6 segmental lesions, the biceps, brachioradialis, and triceps reflexes are hypoactive or absent, but
the finger flexor reflex is exaggerated. With complete C5 segment lesions, sensory loss occurs over the entire body below the neck
and anterior shoulder; with C6 sensory lesions, there is similar distribution of sensory loss, except for the lateral aspect of the arm
that is spared.
Lesions of the Seventh Cervical Segment
Diaphragmatic function is normal with C7 segment lesions. There is paresis of flexors and extensors of the wrists and fingers. The
biceps and brachioradialis reflexes are preserved, whereas the finger flexor reflex is exaggerated. There may be an inverted triceps
reflex characterized by flexion of the forearm following tapping of the olecranon. There may be sensory loss at and below the third
and fourth digits, including the medial aspect of the arm and forearm.
Lesions of the Eighth Cervical and First Thoracic Segments
Lesions at the C8 and T1 segments result in weakness, predominantly involving small hand muscles. There is associated spastic
paraparesis. With C8 lesions, the triceps reflex and the finger flexor reflex are hypoactive or absent. With T1 lesions, the triceps
reflex is preserved, but the finger flexor reflex is hypoactive. With C8-T1 lesions, there may be a Horner syndrome. Sensory loss
involves the fifth digit and the medial aspect of the forearm and arm, as well as the rest of the body below the lesion.
Rarely, spondylotic narrowing of the spinal canal at C3-C4 and C5-C6 levels may cause intrinsic hand muscles (C8-T1) wasting
with sparing of the C5 and C6 myotomes. These findings have been attributed to anterior horn cell damage due to stagnant hypoxia
from venous congestion of the low cervical cord as a result of midcervical lesions [144].
Spondylotic cervical myelopathy may, at times, present with a glove distribution pattern of sensory loss, also known as the
syndrome of numb, clumsy hands[ 129 , 155]. In some patients, the sensory involvement spreads to the elbows. Motor findings in the
hands and motor and sensory impairment in the legs are often mild. This syndrome has been attributed to ischemia of the intrinsic
border areas of collateralization between the superficial pial network and the central arterial supply to the cervical cord (Fig. 5-4).
Venous stagnation may also play a role [155]. Acute quadriplegia or paraplegia following falls in elderly subjects may result from
underlying cervical spondylotic myelopathy [119].
Lesions of the Thoracic Segments
These are characterized by radicular-type pain or paresthesias, mimicking an intercostal neuralgia. Establishing of segmental LMN
involvement is challenging. Paraplegia, sensory loss below the involved cord level, and bladder, bowel, and often sexual
dysfunction may occur. There may be a Brown-Séquard syndrome, a central cord syndrome, or an anterior cord syndrome. Lesions
above T5 may impair vasomotor control, resulting in syncope upon arising. Autonomic dysreflexia may result from lesions rostral to
the splanchnic sympathetic outflow (above the T6 level) whereby a stimulus (usually bladder or rectal distention) results in a
sympathetic overdrive response characterized by excessive sweating below the level of injury, severe arterial hypertension, reflex
bradycardia, pounding headaches, blurred vision, nausea, blockage of nasal passages, and cutaneous flushing. Untreated, autonomic
dysreflexia may result in seizures,

Lesions of the First Lumbar Segment
With L1 cord lesions, all muscles of the lower extremities are weak. However, lower abdominal paresis (internal oblique,
transversus, and abdominis muscles) may be difficult to demonstrate. Sensory loss often involves both the lower extremities up to
the groins and the back up to a level above the buttocks. With long-standing lesions, there is patellar and ankle hyperreflexia.
Lesions of the Second Lumbar Segment
There is spastic paraparesis without weakness of the abdominal muscles. The cremasteric reflex (L2) is not elicitable. The patellar
reflexes are depressed, and the ankle reflexes are brisk. There is normal sensation on the upper anterior aspect of the thighs.
Lesions of the Third Lumbar Segment
There is some preservation of hip flexion (iliopsoas and sartorius) and leg adduction (adductor longus, pectineus, and gracilis). The
patellar reflexes are hypoactive or absent. The ankle reflexes are hyperactive. Sensation is normal on the upper anterior aspect of the
thighs.
Lesions of the Fourth Lumbar Segment
There is greater hip flexion and leg adduction than with L1-L3 lesions. Patients often stand by stabilizing the knees. There is patellar
areflexia and ankle hyperreflexia. Sensation is normal on the anterior aspect of the thighs and medial aspects of the knees.
Lesions of the Fifth Lumbar Segment
There is normal hip flexion and adduction, as well as leg extension. Due to normal strength of the quadriceps femoris, patients are
able to extend the legs against resistance when the extremities are flexed at the hip and knee. The patellar reflexes are present. The
ankle reflexes are hyperactive. Sensory function is intact on the anterior aspect of the thighs and medial aspects of the legs, ankles,
and soles.

With S1 segment lesions, there is weakness of the triceps surae, flexor digitorum longus, flexor hallucis longus, and small foot

The gastrocnemius and soleus muscles are stronger with S2 segmental lesions. However, the flexor digitorum, flexor hallucis

Conus Medullaris Lesions
Conus medullaris lesions cause paralysis of the pelvic floor muscles and early sphincter dysfunction. Disruption of the bladder
reflex arc results in an autonomous neurogenic bladder, characterized by loss of voluntary initiation of micturition, increased
residual urine, and impaired bladder sensation. Constipation and impaired erection and ejaculation are often present. Patients may
also have a symmetric pattern of saddle anesthesia. Pain is uncommon; when present, it involves the thighs, buttocks, and perineal
region [ 3].
A tethered cord may result in an array of neurologic, urologic, orthopedic, and dermatologic manifestations, often seen in
association with spina bifida occulta or prior surgery of myelomeningoceles or lipomeningoceles. Patients may have numbness of
the feet, asymmetric muscle atrophy of the calf or thigh muscles, UMN signs, bowel, and bladder dysfunction. Moreover, patients
often have foot deformities, pes cavus, equinovarus, talipes, scoliosis, and cutaneous manifestations of spinal dysraphism, such as
lumbosacral midline hair tuft, midline nevus, dermal sinus tracts, hypertrichosis, or subcutaneous sacral lipomas [ 38 , 135].
Cauda Equina Lesions
Due to compression of the lumbar and sacral nerve roots below the L3 vertebral level, cauda equina lesions often cause early pain in
the distribution of lumbosacral nerve roots [6]. The pain may be unilateral or asymmetric, increasing with the Valsalva maneuver.
With cauda equina tumors (e.g., ependymomas, schwannomas, meningiomas), the pain is often nocturnal and worse with
recumbence. With advanced lesions, there is flaccid, hypotonic, areflexic paralysis affecting the glutei, posterior thigh muscles, and
anterolateral muscles of the leg and foot, resulting in a peripheral type of paraplegia. Sensory testing demonstrates asymmetric
sensory loss in the saddle region, involving the anal, perineal, and genital regions extending to the dorsal aspect of the thigh,
anterolateral aspect of the leg, and outer aspect of the foot.
The ankle reflexes are absent, with variable responses of the patellar reflexes. Sphincteric changes are similar to those noted with
conus medullaris lesions, but occur late in the course of the disease. Lumbar disc herniations may result in a cauda equina syndrome
when associated with a narrow spinal canal and a low-lying conus medullaris [139].
It is often surmised that conus medullaris lesions result in early sphincter compromise, late pain, and symmetrical sensory
manifestations. Conversely, cauda equina lesions often present with late sphincteric manifestations, early pain, and asymmetrical
sensory findings. However, this subtle distinction is exceedingly challenging and not always present (Table 5-12).

Neurogenic Bladder with Spinal Cord Lesions
Classification of the neurogenic bladder is complex and requires adequate functional neuroanatomic and urodynamic knowledge [15
, 16 , 31 , 40 , 114 , 131]. Storage and evacuation of urine ultimately depend on a spinal reflex arc. However, supraspinal input is required
to preserve bladder continence and postpone bladder emptying under appropriate circumstances. The afferent arc of the spinal reflex
arising from the distention of bladder wall stretch receptors travels through the parasympathetic nerves to the micturition center

through the pelvic nerves to the pelvic plexus; short postganglionic fibers travel from the plexus to the detrusor muscle. Most
afferent fibers conveying sensation of bladder fullness do not end at the sacral spinal cord level but rather ascend to synapse on relay
cells in the dorsal pontomesencephalic reticular formation micturition center (located in the locus ceruleus, pontomesencephalic
gray matter, and nucleus tegmentolateralis dorsalis). Other afferent fibers travel further rostrally to the cortical and subcortical

substantia nigra, hypothalamus, and subthalamic nuclei. In addition, the anterior cerebellar vermis and fastigial nuclei are also

From the pontomesencephalic micturition center, efferents to the spinal cord descend by way of the reticulospinal tracts to the
detrusor motor neurons in the intermediolateral cell columns of the sacral gray matter (S2-S4). Efferents from the cortical and
subcortical micturition centers descend by way of the corticospinal tracts to the pudendal nuclei (Onuf nucleus) in the sacral spinal
cord (S2-S4). The pudendal nerves, whose motor neurons are located in the ventral horns of sacral segments S2-S4, innervate the
striated muscle around the urethra.
The central sensorimotor pathways concerned with micturition and sphincter control can be summarized as follows [131]:
1. Corticospinal pathways (from motor cortex to pudendal motor neurons) concerned with voluntary control of the sphincters
and pelvic floor.
2. The urethral reflex loop (from urethral afferents to pudendal motor neurons) maintains the sphincter tone when the detrusor
is inactive.
3. The detrusor reflex loop (detrusor afferents to pudendal motor neurons) results in sphincter relaxation when the detrusor is
active.
4. The cord loop (from brainstem structures to the conus medullaris) coordinates detrusor and sphincter contraction and
relaxation.
5. The cerebral loop (involving the brainstem, cerebral cortex, and basal ganglia structures) initiates and inhibits switching
between filling and voiding states.
As the normal bladder fills and urine is stored (Fig. 5-5), contraction of the detrusor muscle is inhibited. This inhibition requires the
presence of intact pathways between the sacral cord and the pontine micturition center [40]. Facilitation of striated urethral sphincter
activity also occurs during bladder filling. Continence is promoted by both passive and reflex activities. In the passive filling phase,
the intravesical pressure initially increases minimally due to the elastic property of the smooth muscle and connective tissue in the
bladder wall. Urinary retention is maintained at this low pressure because the proximal urethral pressure exceeds the pressure within
the bladder. The continence reflexes occur when the intravesical pressure exceeds the pressure at the urethral orifice. Stimulation of
the frontal micturition centers by bladder distention enhances sympathetic activity through the hypogastric nerves, resulting in
inhibition of the detrusor muscle and contraction of the internal urethral sphincter, as well as somatic activity through the pudendal
nerves, resulting in external sphincter contraction.
Voiding (Fig. 5-6) is normally a voluntary act resulting from the fine coordinated relaxation of the urethral sphincter and contraction
of the detrusor. Bladder fullness stretches afferents that activate the pontine micturition center, which, in turn, activates the detrusor
reflex and inhibits the sphincter. The pontine micturition center further coordinates the reciprocal relationship of the detrusor and
sphincter and the reflex organization of filling and voiding states [131]. Lesions between the pontine micturition center and the sacral
cord interrupt pathways that are inhibitory to the detrusor and those that coordinate normal sphincter-detrusor activity [31 , 157].
These disorders may occur in isolation or in combination and include the hyperreflexic bladder, detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia,
poorly sustained detrusor contraction, and increased postmicturition residual volume.

The hyperreflexic neurogenic bladder results from lesions above the level of the sacral bladder center and below the level of the
pontomesencephalic micturition center. This type of neurogenic bladder is associated with disorders causing quadriplegia or
paraplegia and in advanced stages of multiple sclerosis. Loss of the normal inhibition from higher centers results in detrusor
contraction during bladder filling. The contractions occur spontaneously or may be provoked by coughing or changing postures. In
most cases, the detrusor becomes overactive. Thus, there is urinary frequency, urgency, urge incontinence, and inability to initiate
micturition voluntarily. Small volumes of urine stimulate uninhibited detrusor muscle contractions. The bladder capacity is reduced,
but residual urine may be increased. The bulbocavernosus and superficial anal reflexes are preserved. With lesions above the
splanchnic outflow, bladder fullness may induce a “mass reflex” with paroxysmal arterial hypertension, headaches, diaphoresis, and
bradycardia.
Simultaneous contraction of the sphincter and the detrusor during voiding (detrusor-sphincter dyssynergia) results in obstructed
voiding, interrupted urinary stream, incomplete bladder emptying, and high intravesical pressures because the sphincter fails to relax
properly [40]. Upper urinary tract dilatation and kidney damage may subsequently develop. Abnormal hyperreflexic contractions

An autonomous neurogenic bladder (detrusor areflexia) may result from complete lesions below the T12 segment involving the


<!-- PDF page 207 -->

conus medullaris and cauda equina. This type of neurogenic bladder occurs with sacral myelomeningoceles and tumors of the conus
medullaris-cauda equina region. This is also the type of neurogenic bladder observed during the initial or shock phase of acute
spinal cord injuries. The bladder is paralyzed with awareness of the state of bladder fullness. In most instances, there is urinary
retention because the tone of the detrusor muscle is abolished, and the bladder distends as urine accumulates. There is inability to
initiate micturition, overflow incontinence, and increased residual urine develop. There is associated saddle anesthesia with absent
bulbocavernosus and superficial anal reflexes; the anal sphincter control is also compromised.
Stretch injury to the bladder wall may result in distention of the bladder wall, decompensation of the detrusor muscle, and,
eventually, bladder atonia. The bladder capacity increases considerably, and its walls may become fibrotic. A large residual urine
volume may, therefore, occur due to incomplete detrusor contractions.
A motor paralytic bladder may result from lesions involving the efferent motor fibers to the detrusor or the detrusor motor neurons
in the sacral canal spinal cord. Patients may develop a motor paralytic bladder in association with lumbar spinal stenosis,
lumbosacral meningomyelocele, or following radical hysterectomy or abdominoperineal resection. These patients have painful
urinary retention or impaired bladder emptying. Residual urine is markedly increased. The bulbocavernosus and superficial anal
reflexes are usually absent, but sacral and bladder sensation are present.
A sensory paralytic bladder may be associated with tabes dorsalis, syringomyelia, and diabetes mellitus. This type of neurogenic
bladder results from impaired afferent pathways innervating the bladder, or by dorsal column or lateral spinothalamic tract
dysfunction at the spinal cord level. Patients are capable of maintaining voluntary initiation of micturition. Urinary retention,
overflow incontinence, or urinary tract infection may be early symptoms. The bulbocavernosus and superficial anal reflexes may be
either absent, decreased, or intact.
Sexual Function
Neural and vascular mechanisms are closely integrated in the physiology of erection. Erection also has a psychogenic and reflex
element. The reflex center for erection is at the conus medullaris [90]. Genital organs are innervated by three sets of peripheral
nerves: (a) sacral parasympathetic (pelvic nerves), (b) thoracolumbar sympathetic (hypogastric and lumbar sympathetic chain), and
(c) somatic (pudendal nerves) [11]. The parasympathetic system is the main effector of penile erection (through pelvic nerves), but
there is also a sympathetic erectile pathway through the hypogastric nerves [11]. Furthermore, there is a sympathetic erectile (and
antierectile) outflow at the T12 spinal cord level (which reaches the genitalia through the hypogastric nerves, the pelvic nerves, and
the pudendal nerves) and a parasympathetic erectile outflow at the S2-S3 level (parasympathetic preganglionic fibers traveling
through the pelvic nerves to the pelvic plexus to innervate the corpora cavernosa through the cavernous nerves). Descending erectile
and probably antierectile pathways travel from the cerebrum through the lateral spinal cord columns. Therefore, spinal cord lesions
above T12 may impair psychogenic erection, whereas lesions at the conus medullaris or cauda equina regions abolish reflex
erection. Among men with complete spinal cord lesions below T12-L2, or with complete cauda equina lesions, psychogenic erection
may occur mediated by the sympathetic outflow, whereas reflex erection remains absent [25].
Limbic and hypothalamic pathways from the medial preoptic anterior hypothalamic areas are also important in controlling penile
erection [11 , 34]. Descending pathways pass through the medial forebrain bundle to the midbrain tegmental region and then through
the ventrolateral pons and lateral funiculi of the spinal cord to lumbosacral centers [89].
Ejaculation is coordinated at a hypothetical ejaculatory reflex center at the T12-L1 spinal cord segments that summates the
descending excitatory and inhibitory impulses and sensory impulses from the glans and frenulum [131]. The descending and
ascending pathways are probably located in the lateral spinal cord columns. Spinal cord lesions above the T10 spinal level result in
ejaculation, but a reflex ejaculation may be induced if afferent (S2-S3) and efferent (T10-L2) pathways remain intact. Even reflex
ejaculation is lacking if the spinal cord lesion damages the T12-L2 levels [23 , 131].
Fecal Incontinence
The anal sphincter mechanism comprises the internal anal sphincter, the external anal sphincter, and the puborectalis muscles [90 , 136
]. The external

### [Page 135 begins | PDF 207]

sphincter and the puborectalis muscles behave as a functional unit, known as the voluntary
sphincter[ 107]. A spinal reflex causes the striated sphincter to contract during sudden increases in abdominal pressure, such as
coughing or sneezing [ 113 , 146]. Receptors on the pelvic floor detect the presence of stools and are required for fecal continence [ 97].
Therefore, disorders such as diabetes, pudendal neuropathy, or cauda equina lesions may result in overflow incontinence due to
decreased rectal sensation [145 , 156]. With diabetes mellitus, an underlying autonomic neuropathy associated with “diabetic diarrhea”
may contribute to fecal incontinence. Fecal incontinence, however, at least among the elderly, most often results from constipation.
Fecal incontinence may result from disorders of sensory nerve roots, conus medullaris, motor nerve roots (S3-S4), or peripheral
nerves. In these disorders, rectal examination may demonstrate reduced anal sphincter tone. There may be decreased perianal


<!-- PDF page 208 -->

sensation and loss of the anal skin reflex (anal wink). The colon may be hypotonic and distended. The anal sphincter lax from
deafferentation or deefferentation. High spinal cord lesions may also cause fecal incontinence; these patients usually have better
sphincter control than patients with lower spinal cord lesions. With spinal cord lesions above the conus medullaris, defecation may
become urgent and precipitous. Because the same spinal cord segments and nearly the same spinal cord tracts subserve both bladder
and bowel control, spinal cord diseases often result in “double incontinence.” However, because the bowels are less often filled and
their contents usually solid, fecal incontinence is usually less problematic than urinary incontinence [1].
